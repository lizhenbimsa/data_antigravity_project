# Interlocking Directorates and Competition in Banking

GUGLIELMO BARONE, FABIANO SCHIVARDI, and ENRICO SETTE $ ^{*} $

## ABSTRACT

We study the effects on corporate loan rates of an unexpected change in the Italian legislation that forbade interlocking directorates between banks. Exploiting multiple firm-bank relationships to fully account for all unobserved heterogeneity, we find that prohibiting interlocks decreased the interest rates of previously interlocked banks by 14 basis points relative to other banks. The effect is stronger for high-quality firms and for loans extended by interlocked banks with a large joint market share. Interest rates on loans from previously interlocked banks become more dispersed. Finally, firms borrowing more from previously interlocked banks expand investment, employment, and sales.

INTERLOCKING DIRECTORATES (IDS HEREAFTER) ARISE when two or more corporate boards have one or more members in common. Most advanced countries forbid or limit IDs among competing firms to mitigate the risk that they

could facilitate collusion. $ ^{1} $ In practice, limits to IDs and outright bans are not strictly enforced and, as a result, IDs are very common. In the United States, however, both the Justice Department and the Federal Trade Commission have recently intensified efforts to enforce the ban on IDs, sparking a lively debate among practitioners.

A key challenge in regulating IDs is that we know little about the causal effects of board interconnections on competition. In this paper, we fill this gap by focusing on the Italian corporate lending sector in the early 2010s. This setting represents an ideal testing ground for three reasons. First, an unanticipated regulatory change in 2011 that forbade IDs among competing banks, $ ^{2} $ breaking board connections, represents an exogenous change in the structure of bank interconnections. Second, not only does each bank lend to multiple firms, but firms also typically borrow from multiple banks, which allows us to flexibly control for both fixed and time-varying unobserved heterogeneity between treated loans—loans of interlocked banks before the reform—and controls. Third, a database with detailed information on individual firm-bank lending contracts allows us to observe firm-bank individual loan prices and their evolution before and after the reform, and to test whether prices of treated loans decreased after the reform relative to controls.

Following the antitrust authority, we use Italian provinces as the relevant geographical market for business lending (NUTS 3 units, broadly comparable to a U.S. county). Within the province, we define a network of interlocked banks as a set of banks with IDs and with sufficiently high market share to exert market power. We then label loans of the banks belonging to a network as treated.

We apply a standard difference-in-differences (DiD) framework, in which we compare the change in the interest rate on treated and control loans before and after the reform. Our setting allows for a careful identification of the causal effects of IDs on loan rates. First, we exploit the exogenous and unexpected change in connections induced by the law, which allows us to overcome the problem of endogenous network formation and breakup that plagues most of existing empirical literature, namely, assortative matching of similar banks that apply similar pricing policies. Second, we exploit two features of the Italian lending market to control for unobserved heterogeneity potentially correlated with treatment: (i) banks typically lend in multiple provinces, which together with treatment defined at the bank-province level, implies that loans

from a given bank can be treated in one province and controls in another, and (ii) firms typically borrow from multiple banks, which implies that some of a given firm's loans can be treated and other controls. Accordingly, we can run our regressions with a full set of firm-period and bank-period dummies that account for all time-varying unobserved heterogeneity at the firm and bank levels, that is, identification comes from analyzing the evolution of the within-firm-period and within-bank-period difference in rates on treated and control relationships. This allows us to fully account for shocks that hit both the firm and the bank, as well as for any other time-varying confounding effects such as other measures contained in the Monti law that might affect differentially firms and banks and be correlated with the treatment. We also control for unobserved (time-invariant) characteristics of the bank-firm relationship, and thereby account for matching effects. To the best of our knowledge, no prior paper implements such a rigorous empirical design to identify causal effects of corporate interconnections on competition.

We find that the severance of IDs has a competition-enhancing effect: in our preferred specification, the interest rate on treated relationships drops by 14 basis points (bps) relative to the controls (1.5% of the average interest rate on treated loans before the treatment). In terms of size, the effect is of similar magnitude as the effects of common ownership on airline tickets as well as of the effects on interest rates of bold expansionary monetary policy measures undertaken by the European Central Bank after the financial crisis. Event study analysis shows no evidence of a pretend. Rather, the effect is significant starting from the fourth quarter after the reform, with the drop in interest rates reaching 29.2 bps (3.1% of the average rate) after three years.

We conduct extensive robustness checks of our main results. We first test alternative market share thresholds used to define treatment. We also examine sensitivity to the number of pre- and postperiods considered, and we employ a closed sample to account for the possible influence of attrition. We further repeat the analysis at the firm (rather than firm-bank) level, to address potential issues arising from the reallocation of credit demand across lenders. In all cases, our findings remain unchanged.

The theory of collusion predicts that prices are less dispersed in a collusive equilibrium than under competition. $ ^{3} $ Consistent with this view, we show that interest rates on previously interlocked relationships are less dispersed before the reform and that dispersion increases after it. We then run a series of comparative statics exercises based on firm and network characteristics that we expect to be related to the strength of the effect. In terms of firm characteristics, we find that more creditworthy firms observe a larger drop in rates. We interpret this result as indicating that, once the market becomes more competitive following the reform, such firms can better exploit their outside options and therefore renegotiate loans more aggressively. In terms of network characteristics, we find that the drop in the interest rates is larger for

loans whose network had a higher market share or was sustained by a larger number of IDs before the reform.

While we focus on anticompetitive effects, IDs may allow for information sharing that reduces the extent of asymmetric information on borrowers. If this were the case, the breakup of IDs might restrict credit supply due to rationing (Stiglitz and Weiss (1981), Crawford, Pavanini, and Schivardi (2018)). To assess this possibility, we examine the amount of credit granted by banks. We find no evidence of reductions in granted credit on treated relationships after the reform, which works against the hypothesis that IDs might benefit customers by reducing the extent of asymmetric information. Finally, we consider the real effects of the reform. We find that firms with a larger share of credit from treated relationships experience higher investment rates, employment growth, and sales growth in the postreform period. Overall, we conclude that the reform was instrumental in improving the performance of the corporate sector.

Our paper contributes to the debate on the anticompetitive effects of firms' interconnections, focusing on IDs. Prior literature that documents the diffusion of IDs shows that they are pervasive even within sector, and argues that this is reason for concern (Dooley (1969), Fich and Shivdasani (2006), Faleye, Hoitash, and Hoitash (2011), Heemskerk, Fennema, and Carroll (2016), Hauser (2018), Graham (2020), Nili (2020)). Until recently, however, direct evidence on the effects of IDs on competition was virtually nonexistent. Three recent contemporaneous papers address this issue. Geng et al. (2021) use the staggered adoption at the state level of a change in U.S. corporate law to construct an instrument for IDs. They provide evidence that IDs reduce competition, particularly for R&D-intensive firms. Colombo (2022) studies the U.S. airline market and shows that when two directors share an appointment in a third, noncompeting company, the two competing airlines reduce supply and increase prices. For U.S. listed firms, Gopalan, Li, and Zaldokas (2024) address causality using a similar approach and show that new IDs between product market peers increase profitability and prices. These results are in line with ours. Like Geng et al. (2021), we exploit a change in the corporate law as a source of exogenous variation in IDs. Like Colombo (2022), we have a precise measure of prices and of overlapping markets. In addition, we exploit special features of the corporate loan market in Italy—both banks and firms entertain multiple lending relationships—to flexibly control for all unobserved heterogeneity.

A number of papers document a positive effect of interindustry board connections on different measures of performance, such as mergers and acquisitions (M&A) transactions (Cai and Sevilir (2012)), risk-adjusted stock returns (Larcker, So, and Wang (2013)), cost of debt (Chuluun, Prevost, and Puthenpurackal (2014)), and innovation activities (Chang and Wu (2021)). Faia, Mayer, and Pezone (2022) study the effects of the "Save Italy" decree (the same regulatory change that we use) on the stock market returns of Italian listed corporations, including nonfinancial firms (although the ban on IDs only affected banks and insurance companies), and find that reducing the network centrality of a firm depresses returns. While the general result that IDs

positively affect performance is consistent with ours, the reason, and therefore the implication, is very different. The papers above focus on interindustry IDs and argue that positive effects derive from the fact that they provide useful information to firms. We focus instead on intra-industry IDs and document causal evidence of their anticompetitive effects. In this sense, our results speak to the competition policy debate about ID regulation, and stress the importance of distinguishing between inter- and intra-industry interlocks. $ ^{4} $

A recent body of work focuses on common ownership, the importance of which has increased substantially over time, as a potential anticompetitive mechanism (see, among others, He and Huang (2017), Azar, Schmalz, and Tecu (2018), Koch, Panayides, and Thomas (2021), Lewellen and Lowry (2021)). Compared to this literature, we focus on a different mechanism of interconnection. Our empirical design also fully addresses the identification challenges that plague the literature.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section I, we discuss the mechanisms underlying the impact of IDs on market outcomes as well as the “Save Italy” decree. In Section II, we introduce the definition of the network and motivate the empirical setting. Section III describes the data, while Section IV reports results and the robustness tests. Section V examines additional implications and performs heterogeneity analysis while Section VI considers credit quantity and firm performance. Finally, Section VII concludes.

## I. Shared Board Members, Collusion, and the Monti Decree

In this section, we first discuss the economic mechanisms through which shared board members (SBMs henceforth) can facilitate collusion; we then describe the content of the Monti Decree.

### A. How Can SBMs Facilitate Collusion?

Article 36 of the Monti Decree, which we discuss in detail in the next subsection, forbids SBMs among competing financial institutions, in an explicit effort to increase competition in the financial sector. $ ^{5} $ The implicit assumption motivating the ban is that IDs facilitate collusive behavior between banks. We now discuss the potential mechanisms through which this can occur.

First, note that IDs are not randomly assigned to banks but rather are chosen by the banks themselves. Banks can strategically choose to appoint a director who already sits on the board of a competing bank precisely because

they intend to collude, and the choice of connecting two boards with an SBM could directly be a signal of that intention. However, collusive equilibria must be sustainable. In this respect, IDs can be particularly useful, since SBMs comprise a direct channel of information sharing between the competing banks. The exchange of information between competitors helps sustain a collusive agreement, especially in markets such as the credit market, where the prices charged by competitors are not readily observable (Harrington and Skrzypacz (2007)). When prices are unobservable, it can be difficult to detect deviations from the collusive strategy, in which case the threat of punishment is not credible (Stigler (1964), Green and Porter (1984)). $ ^{6} $ Below, we show that, compared to nonshared directors, SBMs are more likely to hold an executive position in one of the banks. This increases the ability of a collusive equilibrium to be sustained, as executive directors have both more information about a bank's strategies and more power to determine them. If such interconnections are broken, this channel of information exchange and coordination disappears, making the collusive equilibrium unsustainable and decreasing prices.

The next question is the level at which coordination takes place. A first possibility is that an SBM can directly identify shared customers and coordinate the pricing of individual loans of the interlocked banks. While theoretically possible, the relevance of this mechanism is limited by the fact that only loans above a certain threshold go through the Board of Directors. $ ^{7} $ Moreover, large loans are typically granted to large firms, which borrow from multiple banks and have access to sources of external finance other than bank credit, limiting the capacity of banks to exert market power even when interlocked.

A more likely hypothesis is that coordination occurs at the local market level. Below we provide evidence of a province component of bank-level pricing. The board can contribute to the determination of prices at this level. SBMs can be instrumental in the coordination of less aggressive policies in markets in which interlocked banks have substantial overlap, as any deviation from the collusive strategy would be immediately detected by the SBM.

Since decisions of local loan officers are important determinants of loan conditions, particularly for small loans, another important question is how collusion at the board level is passed down to branch loan officers, who may not even be aware of IDs. The answer is that the bank can affect individual loan officers' pricing decisions. Even when granting autonomy to loan officers, the

headquarters can set market-specific rules for price setting. For example, the price might be a combination of soft information collected by the officer and hard information evaluated centrally, using market-specific scoring models. The headquarters can also affect loan officers' pricing decisions by differentiating across local markets the cost or quantity of funds provided through the internal capital market, $ ^{8} $ and they can set different incentive schemes in terms of targets for corporate lending, setting less aggressive targets for officers operating in markets in which interlocked banks jointly operate.

### B. The Monti Decree

Our analysis exploits the so-called “Save Italy” decree (Law Decree 201/2011 of December 6, 2011). This decree was passed as part of the newly appointed Monti government’s effort to avert the risk of government default and “Italexit” from the Euro Area. It aimed to improve the government’s long-run financial sustainability to restore financial markets’ confidence in Italian government debt. The decree entailed three broad lines of intervention: (i) cuts to public expenditures, most notably through reform of the pension system; (ii) increases in taxes, particularly the value-added tax and the real estate tax; and (iii) measures to foster the country’s long-run growth. Among these measures—which include removing barriers to competition, such as restrictions on retail trade operating hours—are those outlined in Article 36, which prohibits individuals from holding concurrent positions in the governing bodies (such as boards or other top management roles) of two competing banking groups. $ ^{9} $ In what follows, for the sake of brevity we refer to groups as “banks” given that our analysis is at the group level, and we use “individual banks” to refer to single banks within groups (for stand-alone banks, the two terms coincide). $ ^{10} $ Two banks are defined as competitors if they operate in the same local market. After the reform, individuals holding more than one appointment in competing banks had to choose only one office, resigning from the other(s) within 90 days. In the transition period, the choice had to be made within 120 days (by the end of April 2012). Throughout the paper, we assume that 2011Q4 is the last quarter before the policy (which was passed in December 2011), rather than 2012Q2 (the deadline within which banks had to comply with the new regulation) to avoid anticipation effects.

The chain of events that led to the approval of the decree makes it an ideal quasi-natural experiment, as the policy was completely unexpected and led to the exogenous breakup of bank connections. During the summer of 2011, the sovereign debt crisis erupted throughout Europe. Italy was badly affected, with the spread between the Italian and the German 10-year government bond yield increasing from 150 bps before the summer to above 500 bps by year-end. Both financial markets and European institutions exerted strong pressure on the Italian government to undertake reforms to increase the country's growth potential. Against this background, the Berlusconi government resigned on November 12, 2011, and four days later the Monti government took office. The government was formed mostly by nonpoliticians and had the explicit mandate to undertake structural reforms and bring the budget under control to ease the tension on sovereign debt. The “Save Italy” decree, as the name itself suggests, was the first strong signal of Italy's commitment to remain in the euro. It was drafted under very strong time pressure and approved less than a month after the government took office. The content of the decree, in particular the aspects that we exploit, was totally unanticipated from the perspective of both banks and firms. Arguably, only the dramatic situation that the country faced allowed the government to approve measures such as the ban of IDs that would have been very hard, if not impossible, to approve in normal times due to banks' lobbying activity.

Despite the fact that Article 36 was not the only measure contained in the law to improve growth, our identification strategy isolates the effect of this specific channel. First, Article 36 is the only one that affects banks directly. Second, and more importantly, our empirical framework, discussed in the next section, allows us to control for any observable and unobservable determinants of credit conditions that could be correlated with other measures contained in the decree, as well as with other concurrent shocks that materialized over the period.

## II. Empirical Design and Identification

In this section, we first describe our definition of treated loans. We then discuss our identification strategy.

### A. Defining Treated Loans

Our definition of treatment is at the bank-market level. As is typical in the banking literature, we define markets in terms of geographical units. $ ^{11} $ This choice is based on a large body of empirical evidence that underlines the local nature of the corporate lending market, especially for small firms like most of those in our sample. For example, Petersen and Rajan (2002) and Degryse and Ongena (2005) point to the existence of a banker's rule of thumb according to

which banks prefer to lend to customers located within 4 and 1.4 miles of a branch, respectively. Using a sample comparable to ours, Crawford, Pavanini, and Schivardi (2018) show that firms are on average 2.9 km (1.8 miles) away from the nearest branch of their main bank. Researchers rationalize proximity between borrowers and lenders as a tool to facilitate information acquisition and reduce travel costs for borrowers. In Appendix B, we provide direct evidence of the local component of lending markets. In particular, we show that banks tend to specialize geographically, that there is an important bank-local market component in interest rates, and that the degree of geographical specialization is much stronger than that of sectoral specialization.

The geographical component of banking markets is embraced by the regulatory bodies, which typically use provinces as relevant markets. For example, when entry was regulated, the Bank of Italy relied on provinces to decide whether to authorize the opening of new branches. The Italian Antitrust authority typically considers the province as the relevant geographical market in banking, in particular when assessing M&A. $ ^{12} $ Based on this evidence and practice, we also use the Italian provinces, administrative units roughly comparable to U.S. counties, as our definition of geographical market. In Italy, there are 110 provinces, and the average radius, after approximating its surface with a circle, is 30 km. $ ^{13} $

We next discuss the definition of networks of interconnected banks. Connections are between boards of individual banks. Around one-third of individual banks have at least one SBM (1.4 on average). Moreover, each connected individual bank is connected, on average, with three other individual banks (two if we only consider individual banks belonging to different banking groups). However, as we stated in Section I, the law bans board connections at the group level, so we define connections at the group rather than the individual bank level. Specifically, we say that the link between groups j and k exists when there is a common board member between at least one individual bank belonging to group j and one individual bank belonging to group k. $ ^{14} $ When two groups (“banks” in the terminology defined above) are connected, all individual banks belonging to the groups are connected. In the last quarter of 2011, 125 banks were connected, out of a total of 604 banks.

In sum, our definition of treatment is based on three conditions: (i) two or more banks are connected via IDs, (ii) they operate in the same province with nonnegligible individual market shares, and (iii) they account for a “sufficiently large” provincial market share. We now describe in detail how we implement these conditions.

(i) Board connection: We start by requiring that banks be connected through IDs. Consider the case in which bank j is connected to one or more banks (say k and l). We say that banks j, k, and l are jointly connected and we call bank j "pivotal," as it is directly connected to the other banks. Note that banks k and l are not necessarily directly connected, it is sufficient that both are directly connected to bank j.

(ii) Lending in the same province with nonnegligible individual market shares: Bank j and at least one of its connected counterparts must serve the same province p, each with a nonnegligible market share. This condition is imposed to avoid assigning treatment status to connected banks in p in which some bank has a large market share and all other banks have very small market shares. For example, suppose that the market share of bank j in province p is 20%, and that of banks k and l is 0.1%. In such a case, breaking the connection is unlikely to affect pricing behavior because even before the policy, banks k and l were adding very little market share to that of the dominant bank. In our data, the supply side of the market is rather fragmented as the distribution of market shares at the province-bank level, computed in the year before the Monti Decree (from 2011Q1 to 2011Q4), has a large mass of density in the left part of the distribution. We require a minimum market share of 1% (approximately the 87th percentile of the market share distribution at the province-bank level). As we show below, our core results hold both if we do not impose any minimum threshold and if we raise it to 2% (91th percentile).

(iii) “Sufficiently large” aggregate provincial market share: The aggregate market share of banks satisfying conditions (i) and (ii) must be “sufficiently large” to give these banks the capacity to affect market prices. Choosing this threshold is tricky, as there is no obvious way to determine the market share above which the network is able to exert market power. Some guidance can be gained from the Antitrust Authority. The general rule of the Authority is that, in case of an M&A, participating banks may be required to dismiss branches in local markets in which the market share of the merging banks exceeds 15% (Lotti and Manaresi (2015)). In practice, in the three years before the reform, the decisions of the Antitrust Authority suggest that the actual threshold above which the provision is enforced is around 35%. $ ^{15} $ Note, however, that in

an M&A, the Authority trades off increases in market power with efficiency gains, so that it should be willing to accept an increase in market power if mitigated by the cost savings induced by the M&A. This suggests that 35% might be too conservative to detect situations in which banks exert market power. We address this problem using a robust approach. We set the cutoff at 20% in our baseline specification. Under this cutoff, 29% of the lending relationships are classified as treated. We show that our results hold, and change in the expected way, if we modify this threshold to either 10% (41% of relationships classified as treated) or 30% (10% of relationships classified as treated).

We define a set of banks satisfying conditions (i) to (iii) in province p as a network in p. Note that in each province, a bank can belong to more than one network, as illustrated in Figure A.1. If bank j belongs to at least one network in p, we set the (time-varying) dummy  $ D_{jpt} = 1 $ for all loans this bank issues in province p in  $ t $. $ ^{16} $ Figure 1 shows that, after the reform, the relevance of IDs in the local credit markets collapses. We show the time pattern of the provincial market shares of banks for which  $ D_{jpt} = 1 $, normalized to one in 2011Q4. We use the market share to take into account both the existence and the local relevance of IDs. For comparison, we also report the total number of bank-provinces. Against a rather stable path for both variables in the year before the policy, the share of loans for which  $ D_{jpt} = 1 $ drops discontinuously when the reform becomes effective and quickly converges to a reduction of around 95% in the postperiod, while the total number of bank-provinces declines only marginally and smoothly. $ ^{17} $

Finally, we determine the quarters in which the conditions stated above must hold to classify a relationship as treated. In practice,  $ D_{jpt} $ can vary across quarters due to the formation or dissolution of links and because market shares might cross the thresholds defined above, particularly for banks/networks close to the respective thresholds. For our analysis, both situations are problematic. First, ID formation and breakup before the law may be endogenous. Second, changes in  $ D_{jpt} $ due to small changes in market shares around the thresholds do not represent real changes in networks. To account for both issues, we set the time-invariant treatment status  $ TR_{jp} = 1 $ if  $ D_{jp2011Q4} = D_{jp2011Q3} = D_{jp2011Q2} = D_{jp2011Q1} = 1 $, that is, a loan extended by bank j is treated if the conditions above hold in all four quarters before the Monti law. Analogously, we set  $ TR_{jp} = 0 $ if  $ D_{jp2011Q4} = D_{jp2011Q3} = 0 $.

### B. Identification

We analyze the effects of the reform using a DiD framework, which, by exploiting the unique features of our setting, allows us to carefully address several identification challenges that can affect our estimates. Our dependent variable is the interest rate  $ r_{ijpt} $ that bank j charges firm i located in province p in period t. Our preferred measure is the gross interest rate, which includes fixed costs such as fees and commissions, as market power can be exploited by increasing such components (Sufi (2009)). Below we show that results are similar using the net interest rate. The basic regression equation is

 $$ r_{ijpt}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}POST_{t}+\alpha_{2}TR_{ijp}+\alpha_{3}TR_{ijp}\times POST_{t}+\alpha_{4}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{ijpt}+D_{ijpt}+\epsilon_{ijpt}, $$ 

where  $ POST_{t} $ is a dummy for the postperiod (from 2012Q1 onward),  $ TR_{i,jp} $ is a dummy for treated relationships as defined above,  $ \mathbf{X}_{i,jt} $ is a vector of firm, bank, and firm-bank time-varying characteristics, and  $ D_{i,jpt} $ denotes various combinations of fixed effects used in different specifications. Interpreting  $ \alpha_{3} $ as the causal effect of the breakup of IDs is challenging, as there may be many potential sources of unobserved heterogeneity correlated with both the treatment and the outcome. In what follows, we explain why we believe that our empirical design can robustly account for any potential correlated effect.

The first fundamental identification challenge that plagues the empirical literature on network effects is endogenous network formation and breakup. In our context, banks might share a board member as a consequence of the fact that they have similar customers or adopt similar pricing policies, which would raise questions about the causal interpretation of the treatment. Similarly, changes in market strategies and in pricing policies might lead to changes in board composition and therefore in the endogenous breakup of networks. Due to the unexpected policy change, in our framework the breakup of IDs was both exogenous, that is, not chosen by the banks but rather mandated by law, and unanticipated. This ensures that the treatment is not an endogenous bank response to characteristics of their portfolio of customers or to a shock that directly affects the interest rates.

Exogenous network breakup is a necessary but not sufficient condition to identify the causal effects of IDs on interest rates. First, one might be concerned about correlated fixed bank and firm attributes or aggregate time effects. For example, firms with interlocked relationships might be different from others, if anything because the probability of having interlocked relationships increases with the number of relationships a firm has, which is correlated in turn with size. Moreover, interest rates have an obvious time component. To address these concerns, we exploit our DiD framework and in all regressions include firm, bank, and quarter fixed effects.

The estimation of equation (1) with individual and quarter fixed effects is the standard setting for DiD exercises. However, it leaves open the possibility that fixed firm and bank attributes have time-varying effects, which would invalidate the causal interpretation of  $ \alpha_{3} $. This concern is particularly important in our setting. Specifically, there is evidence that the Eurozone debt crisis, which prompted the sudden adoption of the ban on IDs (and of other measures as discussed in Section I.B) had differential effects on firms and banks according to their size or their financial strength: small firms usually suffered more than large ones during the crisis, and banks' lending policies were heavily influenced by their capital ratios (Chodorow-Reich (2014), Schivardi, Sette, and Tabellini (2022)) and funding structure (Cingano, Manaresi, and Sette (2016)). These characteristics might be correlated with the treatment and therefore induce a spurious correlation between treatment and the interest rate. To continue the example above, suppose that, for some reason, small firms are less

likely to have treated relationships. $ ^{18} $ If the deterioration of their performance leads to an increase in interest rate relative to large firms, we would observe a negative value of  $ \alpha_{3} $: large firms, more likely to have treated relationships, record a drop in interest rates relative to small firms, and hence we would erroneously interpret the decrease as an effect of the reform. The typical fix is to interact firm characteristics with the Post dummy. However, there may always exist unobserved characteristics that we do not control for and that bias our estimates. The special features of the business loans market and the richness of our data allow us to fully tackle this important threat to identification. Indeed, firms generally have multiple banking relationships (see data description below). This implies that, following the seminal idea of Khwaja and Mian (2008), we can include a full set of firm-quarter dummies, a standard identification tool in the literature using credit registry data. $ ^{19} $ Thus, in this specification, we exploit only within firm-quarter variability in interest rates. In particular, identification comes from the within-quarter comparison of rates on loans that a firm obtains on treated and control relationships; put differently, the control sample consists of the relationships that the same firm has with banks that do not belong to any network in the local market (province). The same concerns in terms of time-varying unobserved heterogeneity apply to banks. In this case too, we can capture any time-varying effect on rates by using a full set of bank-quarter dummies. Consider bank j, whose loans are classified as treated in province p, where the bank belongs to a network, but not in province q, where it does not. The treatment dummy turns on only for loans that bank j extends in province p, while those in province q end up in the control group. Conditional on the bank-quarter effects,  $ \alpha_{3} $ is estimated as the difference in the change in interest rates on treated with respect to control relationships of the same bank. Note that this framework also fully controls for other potential confounding factors, such as other measures included in the “Salva Italia” or other concurrent policy interventions that might have differentially affected firms or banks. To pose a threat to identification, such confounding factors should apply differentially to treated and control relationships, within bank-quarter and within firm-quarter. In particular, the confounding factor should affect only the loans a firm obtained by banks that we classify as treated at the provincial level.

One may also be concerned that province-quarter effects that are correlated with the intensity of treatment at the province level. For example, a

province might be particularly affected by the downturn generated by the sovereign debt crisis and also have a particularly high presence of treated relationships. Again, given that in the same province we have both treated and control relationships, we can fully account for this possibility using a set of province-quarter dummies. Note that our preferred specification with firm-quarter dummies controls directly for province-quarter shocks, as firms are only located in one province. $ ^{20} $

A final concern is that there may exist features specific to the firm-bank match, above and beyond separate firm and bank (also interacted with time) effects. For example, treated relationships might entail a higher degree of information on the bank's side precisely because they are treated, which may in turn affect the bank's lending policy to the treated firms compared to other firms. These differences are addressed of by our DiD design. However, the same unobserved heterogeneity at the match level could imply different attrition rates in the postperiod, implying that the estimating sample changes over time in nonrandom fashion, possibly biasing the coefficients. To account for this possibility, in our most saturated specification we also add firm-bank fixed effects. In a series of robustness exercises, we also check if the estimates change when focusing on alternative samples, such as a closed sample of relationships that exist throughout the estimation period.

To summarize, our identification is based on the exogenous breakup of interconnections and on the possibility of fully accounting for fixed and time-varying confounding factors at both the firm and the bank level. To the best of our knowledge, no prior paper in the literature on the anticompetitive effects of firm interconnections implements such a robust empirical strategy.

Finally, we describe the data used in the regressions. The sample period spans 2011Q1 until 2014Q4 (16 quarters). The preperiod comprises four quarters from the beginning of the sample to 2011Q4, the last quarter before the policy. $ ^{25} $ The postperiod covers 12 quarters from 2012Q1 to the end of the sample. We let the sample run until 2014Q4 to better detect longer-term effects of the reform, but in robustness checks we employ a shorter window. Given that we are interested in the DiD estimate of  $ \alpha_{3} $, in our basic specification we focus on the sample of relationships that existed in the last quarter of 2011. The sample includes 192,732 unique firms and 3,561,068 firm-bank-quarter relationships, of which 85,898 firms have at least one treated relationship, accounting for 1,046,785 bank-firm-quarter relationships, which means that 29% of the observations are treated. Figure A.3 shows the share of treated relationships by province. While there are clusters in certain areas, they are spread across the whole country. Treated relationships are frequent both in the North and in the South, and in provinces with very different economic and demographic structure. $ ^{26} $

Table A.II reports descriptive statistics for interest rates for treated and control relationships in the pre- and postperiods. $ ^{27} $ Control relationships display a lower interest rate on average in the preperiod (9.14% versus 9.31%). Rates on both treated and control relationships increase in the postperiod, but by a lower extent for treated ones (1.12% versus 1.35%). As a consequence, rates of treated and control relationships converge in the postperiod.

Table A.III reports descriptive statistics for lending relationships, firms, and banks in 2011 (i.e., before the reform), separately for treated and controls. The unit of observation is a lending relationship. This implies that the same firm (bank) can be in both the treatment group and the control group, as long as it has both treated and control relationships. The size of the loan is somewhat higher in treated than in control relationships. In terms of relevance for the firm, treated relationships represent on average half of the total credit obtained by the firm, conditional on having at least one treated relationship.

Firm characteristics indicate that our sample is very comprehensive as it includes a large fraction of small firms, in line with the structure of the Italian economy. Firm characteristics are rather similar across treated and control relationships. This is not surprising, as many firms have both treated and control relationships. To delve deeper into the potential selection of firms into treated and control relationships, we split firms into three mutually exclusive groups: those with only treated relationships, those with only control relationships, and those with both treated and control relationships. Table A.IV, columns (1) to (3), shows that firm characteristics are balanced across the three groups. The only exception is size, which is larger for firms with mixed relationships. This is expected, as the probability of having both treated and control relationships increases with the number of relationships, which is positively correlated with size. $ ^{28} $ We repeat the analysis conditioning on having two or three relationships, respectively, in columns (4) to (9), in which case size is also balanced across the three groups (with a marginal exception for three relationships, all treated). This result indicates an absence of selection into treatment. However, it is important to stress that, as explained above, our most saturated specification fully accounts for time-varying observed and unobserved heterogeneity at the firm level, fully addressing this potential issue.

Bank characteristics are very similar across treated and control relationships: banks have a similar leverage ratio, reliance on wholesale funding, liquidity ratio, and profitability, with a difference emerging for size. This is in line with the descriptive statistics in Table A.I on connected and unconnected banks, despite the fact that there are two important differences in the way the

statistics are computed. First, Table A.III is computed at the relationship level, so that each bank is weighted by its number of relationships, while Table A.I is at the bank level. Second, in Table A.III a given bank can be treated in some provinces and a control in others, while in Table A.I a bank is either connected or not. Again, our most saturated specification fully accounts for time-varying observed and unobserved heterogeneity at the bank level, fully addressing any potential issue arising from differences in bank characteristics.

## IV. Results

In this section, we present the main results as well as a series of robustness checks.

### A. Main Results

We estimate equation (1) to determine the effect of the exogenous breakup of IDs on interest rates. We cluster standard errors at the bank-province level, which is the dimension at which the treatment varies. $ ^{29} $ Table V reports the results. Column (1) runs a parsimonious specification in which we only include sector-quarter, province-quarter, firm, and bank fixed effects. The interest rate on treated relationships in the preperiod is 29 bps higher than on controls and highly significant. It drops by 20 bps after the law becomes effective, with the decrease statistically significant at the 5% level. Moreover, we fail to reject the hypothesis that, in the postperiod, the sum of the two coefficients is equal to zero (p-value 0.213), that is, that after the reform, the interest rate on treated relationships fully converges to that on control relationships.

These patterns are confirmed in the other columns, where we increase the granularity of the controls. In column (2), we add a standard set of firm controls—size (log assets), leverage (debt to equity), ROA (EBIT to assets), liquidity (liquid assets to assets), and, as a summary measure of creditworthiness, a dummy equal to one for firms with an Altman Z-score in the three higher risk categories, out of a total of nine; $ ^{30} $—and bank controls—size (log assets), equity to assets ratio, ROA, interbank deposits (including repos) to assets, liquidity to assets. Firm and bank controls are lagged one period. The estimates of the treatment in the pre- and postperiods increase in absolute value, to 0.37 and -0.32, respectively, and are significant at the 1% level. We again fail to reject the hypothesis of full rate convergence after the reform. The coefficients on firm characteristics are all significant and with the expected sign. The same holds for banks characteristics, with only the liquidity ratio and interbank funding not statistically significant.

The results of Table V are clear-cut and indicate that the prohibition of IDs reduced the interest rates on treated relationships. The estimated effect ranges from 32 bps (column (2)) to 14 bps (columns (4) and (5)). It decreases slightly when introducing firm-period and especially bank-period fixed effects. This drop might signal the importance of accounting for unobserved heterogeneity. However, it may also be due to spillover effects from treated to control relationships that should be taken into account when evaluating the effects of reform. For example, the drop in the coefficient when adding bank-period effects indicates that banks with IDs charge higher rates in the prereform period and reduce them in the postperiod, also in provinces in which they are not classified as treated (below we provide suggestive evidence against this hypothesis). This might be due to a bank-level shock that we want to control for, but it may also signal a spillover effect from treated to control provinces. We therefore interpret the estimates of column (5) as a lower bound on the effects of the reform, and that in column (2) as the upper bound.

Other robustness checks. We now test the robustness of our results along a series of additional dimensions. First, we experiment with the definition of the interest rate. We use the gross rate as our preferred measure because fees and commissions represent a relevant part of the total cost of loans and an important source of banks' income. Still, the net rate is also important, as it captures the marginal cost of credit, which, once a relationship is established, is the relevant cost measure for investment decisions. To check whether our results are affected by this choice, we repeat the exercise using the net interest rate, which excludes fees and commissions. Column (1) of Table VII shows that the effect is slightly smaller (-0.090), in line with the view that market power affects fees and commissions too, but significant at the 1% level, indicating that the marginal cost of credit is also affected.

In our preferred specification, all firm and bank time-varying attributes are absorbed by dummies. However, we can still include time-varying characteristics of the relationship. In column (2), we include the share of total credit to the firm by the bank and the share of overdraft loans out of total loans supplied by the bank to the firm. While the two additional regressors are highly significant and negative, the decrease in the average interest rate on treated relationships is -0.135, very similar to the baseline specification.

We also shorten the number of quarters in the postperiod to 4 from 12, as one might argue that considering a longer period might confound effects not necessarily due to the reform. Doing so also results in the same number of quarters in the pre- as in the postreform period. The estimate decreases slightly to -0.109 (column (3)), consistent with the evidence in Figure 4 on the evolution of the treatment effect, but remains highly statistically significant.

Another issue relates to sample selection. Our baseline specification uses the open sample, that is, with all relationships, independent of the fact that at some point some dissolve. The number of treated relationships in the postperiod, therefore, shrinks over time due to attrition. This might induce selection bias in our estimates. As discussed above, this concern is greatly mitigated by the fact that we use firm-bank effects, which account for unobserved heterogeneity that is time-invariant at the relationship level. Still, one might argue that long-lasting relationships have specific features that might make them respond differently to the treatment. To address this concern, we construct a closed sample, that is, we drop all relationships that disappear from the sample at some point over the sample period. To avoid losing too many observations, we restrict the sample to the same four quarters in the postperiod as used in column (3). $ ^{34} $ This substantially reduces the number of observations, to around half a million. Despite this, column (4) shows that the estimate remains significant and similar to that in column (3), suggesting that, conditional on our rich set of controls, selection is not an issue.

In column (5), we drop the first two quarters of 2012, during which the banks had to transition toward complying with the regulation but could still have IDs. Consistent with the evidence of Figure 4, which shows little effect in those quarters, the estimate increases (in absolute value) to 15.8 bps.

Our regressions so far are unweighted, and as such inform us of the average change in interest rate, independent of the size of the credit lines. While this is the primary object of interest, the firm's cost of credit does depend on the size of credit line. To account for this, column (6) repeats the basic regression using the contemporaneous share of used credit as a weight. The estimate is very similar to the unweighted one (11.3 bps), indicating that composition effects due to the size of the credit lines do not affect the results.

Our last robustness exercise considers the definition of the connection between banks. As explained in Section II.A, the ID ban applies to banking groups, so we use groups as our unit of analysis. A possible drawback of this choice is that two groups may be connected because two small banks belonging to the groups are connected. This is more questionable than the case in which the connection is between the two main banks in the groups. This concern is mitigated by the fact that, in our data, most of the connections (79%) are driven by IDs between the largest banks in the groups. In any case, to check for this possibility, in column (7) we define two banking groups as connected if and only if the ID involves only the bank holding companies of the groups. We find an estimated coefficient that is basically the same as in the baseline.

## V. Extensions

We now corroborate our main results in a series of extensions that also help nail down the mechanism through which the ID ban affects interest rates.

### A. Price Dispersion

Different banks may have different information about a borrower or assess the same information differently, in line with the heterogeneity in the rates offered to the same firm that we observe in the data. An implication of collusive behavior is that rates set by network members should be less dispersed than those offered by other banks, and the dispersion should increase after the reform if the ban on IDs reduces the scope for collusive behavior. To test this hypothesis, we compute the standard deviation of the interest rates at the province-quarter level separately for treated and control relationships,

 $$ \sigma_{pct}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{pct}}\sum_{ij\in pc}(r_{ijpt}-\overline{r_{pct}})^{2}}, $$ 

where $p$ is the province in which the firm is located, $c$ is an index for treated and control relationships, $pc$ is the set of relationships of type $c$ in province $p$, $n_{pct}$ is the number of relationships of type $c$ in province $p$ at time $t$, and $\overline{r_{pct}}$ is the average interest rate on such relationships. Note that for each period, $\sigma_{pct}$ assumes two values in provinces in which there are both treated and control relationships, and one in the others. We then run the regression

 $$ \sigma_{pct}=\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{1}POST_{t}+\gamma_{2}TR_{pc}+\gamma_{3}TR_{pc}\times POST_{t}+Dum_{pct}+e_{pct}, $$ 

where  $ TR_{pc} $ is a dummy equal to one if  $ c = \text{treated and } \text{Dum}_{pct} $ are different sets of dummies.

The results are reported in Table VIII. In column (1), we only include quarter and province fixed effects. Consistent with the theoretical predictions, in the prereform period the standard deviation is 25 bps lower for treated relationships and increases by 7 bps postreform, implying that it partially converges to the level of the controls (we reject the null of full convergence). In column (2), we add province-quarter fixed effects to account for time-varying province shocks and province-treated fixed effects to control for fixed attributes at the province-treatment level. In this specification, in which the  $ Treated\ dummy $ is absorbed and cannot be estimated, the point estimate on  $ Treated*Post $ becomes larger (0.100 vs. 0.067).

One potential issue with these estimates is that the characteristics of the pool of treated and control firms might differ, possibly leading to differences in the dispersion of riskiness of the two groups. This possibility would not explain why the dispersion drops for treated relationships in the postperiod. However, to account for it, we also compute a measure of dispersion after accounting for firm-level determinants of the interest rates. Specifically, we regress the interest rate on the large set of firm controls that we include in column (2).

### B. Heterogeneity

We now explore the heterogeneity of the effect of the ID ban in terms of characteristics of the firm and the network. We do so using the following equation:

 $$ \begin{aligned}r_{ijpt}&=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}TR_{ijp}\times POST_{t}+\beta_{2}TR_{ijp}\times POST_{t}\times HET_{ij}+D_{it}+D_{jt}+D_{ij}\\&+\eta_{ijpt},\\ \end{aligned} $$

In column (2), we test the prediction that tighter connections are more capable of sustaining collusion. To proxy for the tightness of the connections, we exploit the fact that two banking groups may be connected through multiple SBMs of individual banks within the group. For example, banking groups A and B may be connected by bank 1 in group A and bank 2 in group B sharing a board member, and also by bank 3 in group A and bank 4 in group B sharing a board member. It is reasonable to assume that two groups have more opportunities to share information and collude if more of their banks share board members. We find that firms borrowing from networks with an abovemedian number of within-banking-group connected banks record a substantially higher drop in rates in the postperiod, consistent with networks involving more connections having a higher ability to collude.

Another potential determinant of network performance is the degree of symmetry of market shares between network members. Theoretically, the effect could go either way. On the one hand, networks with more equal market shares might better sustain collusion (Compte, Jenny, and Rey (2002)). On the other hand, a “leader” within the network might facilitate the emergence of a leader-follower type of collusion (Mouraviev and Rey (2011), Davies and De (2013)). In practice, column (3) shows that there is no difference in the treatment effect according to the Herfindahl-Hirschman concentration index of bank market shares in the network. In column (4), we use a different measure of network symmetry. Specifically, we split on the basis of the difference between the market share of the largest and the second-largest banks in the network. Again, there is no evidence that this network characteristic affects the outcomes of the reform.

Another implication from theory is that collusive equilibria might be easier to sustain the greater the number of markets in which banks share membership in a network. The reason is that, in the case in which a network member “defects,” the “punishment” is stronger the greater the number of markets in which it can be implemented. Moreover, as the literature on dynamic games documents, the larger the punishment, the higher the level of collusion that the network can sustain in equilibrium (Abreu, Pearce, and Stacchetti (1990)), and therefore the larger the drop in prices once the collusive mechanism is eliminated. We compute multimarket contacts as the average number of provinces in which each pair of banks in the network is active and create the Low dummy accordingly. The results in column (5) weakly support the view

### C. Spillovers on Control Relationships

In this section, we provide some evidence of possible spillovers from treated to control relationships. First, we analyze within-province spillovers. When a substantial share of credit in a province is supplied by network banks, nonnetwork banks might adjust prices to take advantage of a more concentrated market structure. As a measure of exposure to spillovers, we compute the share of interlocked credit in the province in the last quarter of 2011,  $ Z_p = \frac{\sum_j TR_{jp} loan_{jp}}{\sum_j loan_{jp}} $, where  $ loan_{jp} $ is the credit of bank j in province p in the last quarter of 2011, and  $ TR_{jp} $ is a dummy equal to one if loans extended by bank j in province p are treated.

Second, we look at within-bank, across-province spillovers, that is, on loans issued by interlocked banks in provinces in which they are not part of any network. Having a substantial share of treated credit might affect lending strategies in provinces in which the bank is not part of a network. For instance, the bank might expand its credit supply in these provinces by cutting interest rates. As a measure of exposure to spillovers, we compute the share of a bank's interlocked credit over its total credit in the last quarter of 2011:  $ Z_{j} = \frac{\sum_{p} TR_{jp} loan_{jp}}{\sum_{p} loan_{ip}} $.

To detect spillovers, we run a regression similar to (1) using only control relationships,

 $$ r_{ijpt}=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}POST_{t}+\theta_{2}Z_{jp}+\theta_{3}Z_{jp}\times POST_{t}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{4}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{ijpt}+D_{ijpt}+u_{ijpt}, $$ 

where  $ Z_{jp} $ is either  $ Z_j $ or  $ Z_p $, and  $ \theta_3 $ measures the extent of spillovers. When  $ \theta_3 $ is positive, an increase in the share of treated loans is related to higher rates on control loans in the postperiod. Note that we cannot fully saturate this regression. Indeed, when using  $ Z_p $, we cannot include province-time or firm-time fixed effects, while when using  $ Z_j $ we cannot include bank-time fixed effects. These results should therefore be taken as suggestive, as we cannot fully account for time-varying unobserved heterogeneity either at the firm or at the bank level.

Results are reported in Table A.VI. We estimate the most saturated specification that can identify  $ \theta_{3} $ in both regressions, that is, with firm-bank, province, and industry-time fixed effects. We find no evidence of spillovers in either dimension: in none of the specifications are the estimates of  $ \theta_{3} $ significantly different from zero. We also report the results when including bank-time effects in the within-province spillover regression and firm-time effects in the within-bank spillover regression, again finding no evidence of spillovers.

## VI. Credit Quantity and Real Effects

Our focus is on prices, as our main objective is to test for the presence of collusive behavior. However, it is also interesting to examine the effects of the reform on credit quantity and on firm performance.

### A. Credit Quantity

## VII. Conclusions

We study the effects of IDs on banks' corporate loan pricing. We use a legislative change that unexpectedly banned IDs to test their effects on interest rates. We find that the interest rate on treated relationships declined by between 32 and 14 bps (in our most saturated specification) relative to controls.

after the law became effective. We also find that the effect is stronger if the combined market share of the interlocked banks is higher, if connections are tighter (more banks within a group are involved), and if firms are large and ex ante financially stronger. Moreover, consistent with the prediction of models of collusion, price dispersion across loans of previously interlocked banks increases after the reform. Finally, the performance of firms more exposed to interlocked banks improved after the reform.

Our results indicate that prohibiting IDs can have competition-enhancing effects. These findings can inform the policy debate on the enforcement of the existing ban in the United States and on its possible adoption in the European Union, where IDs are not specifically regulated but rather are managed by the general competition law. In particular, the results lend support to the recent emphasis that the U.S. Justice Department has put on enforcing ID bans. They also indicate that stricter antitrust policies can help contrast the generalized reduction in competitive pressures documented by recent work (Gutiérrez and Philippon (2017), De Loecker and Eeckhout (2020)).

## Appendix B: Additional Evidence on the Geographical Component of the Corporate Lending Market

We show that banks tend to specialize at the province level by calculating the distribution of the number of provinces in which a bank is active. We define a bank as active in a province if it has at least one branch in that province. For this analysis, we use the total population of banks operating in Italy, including those that do not report information on interest rates and are therefore excluded from the estimation sample. The distribution is highly skewed, with a large number of banks operating in a small number of provinces. As of December 2011, 88.8% of banks are active in at least one and at most five provinces, 6.3% are active between 6 and 20 provinces, 2.2% between 21 and 50, 2.0% between 51 and 100, and 0.7% in more than 100 provinces. On average, banks are active in five provinces, while the median is one and the  $ 90^{th} $ percentile is six. Only two banks are active in all provinces. These results support the idea that space matters from the supply side: many banks specialize in lending in a limited number of provinces.

To analyze the bank-province component of interest rates, we run regressions of interest rates on various combinations of fixed effects, as well as on the firm-level controls that we use in our baseline regression (size, leverage, ROA, liquidity, and creditworthiness). The firm-level controls ensure that we compare similar firms in terms of creditworthiness. These regressions are run on the 2011Q4 cross-sectional data. (i) Provincial specialization:  $ X_b = \sum_{p=1}^{110}(S_{bp} - S_p)^2 $, where  $ S_{bp} $ is the share of province  $ p $ in the business loan portfolio of bank  $ b $, and  $ S_p $ is the share of province  $ p $ in the business loan portfolio of all banks; the sum is over the 110 provinces we have in the database. (ii) Industrial specialization:  $ Y_b = \sum_{k=1}^{85} (S_{bk} - S_k)^2 $, where  $ S_{bk} $ is the share of industry  $ k $ in the business loan portfolio of bank  $ b $, and  $ S_k $ is the share of industry  $ k $ in the business loan portfolio of all banks; the sum is over the 85 industries we have in the database. with most of the mass close to zero, suggesting that the industry composition of banks' loan portfolios is relatively close to the country average.
