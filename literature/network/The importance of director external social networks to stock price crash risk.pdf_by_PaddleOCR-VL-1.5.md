## ABSTRACT

Prior research documents that information transmitted via director networks affects firms' policies and real economic activities. Given a manager's potential monopoly over firm information, it is important to analyze whether information transmission through director social networks undermines the manager's control. Specifically, we explore whether information flow through director networks influences managers' ability to hoard bad news. We predict and find that the extent of external connections of the board of directors is negatively associated with future stock price crash risk. Additional analysis implies that this evidence is driven by firms with more powerful executives, with weaker auditor monitoring, or subject to strong investor protection, and by directors with greater monitoring incentives or responsibilities and directors with less firm-specific knowledge. Collectively, our research lends empirical support for the monitoring view under which better-informed directors narrow the scope for bad news hoarding evident in stock price crash risk. In another series of tests, we fail to find evidence consistent with the information leakage view under which directors pass sensitive firm-specific information to connections that trade on the information before its public release. Other analysis helps dispel the concern that the endogenous match between directors and companies is spuriously responsible for our core results. Our empirical findings have important implications on how social networks affect the proper functioning of capital markets.

Keywords: crash risk, social network, social connections, board monitoring

# Importance des réseaux sociaux externes des administrateurs quant au risque d’effondrement du cours des actions

## RÉSUMÉ

De précédentes recherches ont démontré que l'information transmise par le truchement des réseaux des administrateurs influe sur les politiques et les activités économiques réelles des sociétés.

Compte tenu du monopole que peut détenir un gestionnaire sur l'information relative à la société, il importe de se demander si la transmission d'information par le truchement des réseaux sociaux des administrateurs nuit au contrôle exercé par ledit gestionnaire. Les auteurs cherchent plus précisément à déterminer si la transmission d'information par le truchement de ces réseaux infue sur la capacité des gestionnaires à retenir les mauvaises nouvelles. Ils formulent et démontrent l'hypothèse selon laquelle l'étendue des liens externes du conseil d'administration est en relation négative avec le risque d'effondrement futur du cours des actions. Une analyse supplémentaire révèle que ces résultats sont plus marqués chez les sociétés dont les cadres supérieurs sont plus puis-sants, sur lesquelles l'auditeur exerce moins de surveillance ou dont les investisseurs bénéficient d'une plus grande protection, et chez les administrateurs qui ont davantage d'incitatifs ou de responsabilités en matière de surveillance et les administrateurs moins bien renseignés sur l'entreprise elle-même. Dans l'ensemble, l'étude justifie empiriquement la perspective de la surveillance selon laquelle des administrateurs mieux informés diminuent la mesure dans laquelle les mauvaises nouvelles sont retenues que met en évidence le risque d'effondrement du cours des actions. Les résultats d'une autre série de tests ne produisent aucune preuve appuyant la perspective des fuites d'informations selon laquelle les administrateurs communiquent des données sensibles relatives à la société à des relations qui s'en servent à leurs propres fins avant que ces données ne soient diffusées publiquement. Une autre analyse contribue à dissiper la crainte que l'association endogène entre administrateurs et sociétés soit indûment responsable des principaux résultats de l'étude. Les constatations empiriques des auteurs fournissent d'importants éclaircissements sur la nature de l'influence des réseaux sociaux sur l'aptitude à fonctionner des marchés des capitaux.

Mots clés : risque d'effondrement, réseau social, relations, surveillance exercée par le conseil d'administration

## 1. Introduction

We extend prior research on the determinants of stock price crash risk to include director external social networks. Among different interpretations of stock price crash, bad news hoarding proposed by Jin and Myers (2006) has emerged as the theoretical underpinning of most empirical studies. This theory holds that compensation, reputation, and career concerns motivate the manager to withhold current bad news in hopes of camouflaging it with future good news. A core assumption underlying the bad news hoarding perspective is that the manager has control over the release of firm-specific bad news. We consider whether information from alternate sources undermines the manager's control over bad news and attenuates stock price crash risk.

We examine the role that director social networks play in shaping crash risk. We focus on board directors, given their responsibility for alleviating agency conflicts stemming from managers' informational advantage by ensuring more transparent disclosures (Armstrong et al. 2010). We specify director external networks as the aggregate social ties between directors of the firm and executives, officers, and directors of other firms. Director social networks may affect crash risk in several ways. First, information flowing from external social networks to directors supplements the existing information set of directors, who can leverage such information to enforce timelier disclosure of bad news in fulfilling their monitoring role (Fama and Jensen 1983). Social networks facilitate information transfer by improving the flow and quality of information, particularly for actors with better connections (Haythornthwaite 1996; Jackson et al. 2016). $ ^{1} $ Given that stock price crash risk stems from the manager's bad news hoarding that impedes information transfer through formal channels, information compiled by directors through alternate informal channels, including social networks, could lessen the manager's monopoly over information and deter bad news hoarding. Consequently, we expect a lower stock price crash risk for firms with better-connected directors. We label this mechanism as the monitoring view of the network-crash risk link.

Second, information could also flow from directors to connections in their external social networks. It is plausible that directors may deliberately or inadvertently transmit sensitive bad news about the firm to their connections in external networks who subsequently trade on it (Cohen et al. 2008; Akbas et al. 2016), or profit from the information themselves through insider trading (Cao et al. 2015). Such informed trading accelerates the impounding of bad news into stock prices and dampens market reactions to the public announcement. As such information flow disseminates the bad news through leakage from directors to social networks, we term this mechanism as the information leakage view of the network-crash risk link. Although both perspectives predict a negative association between director networks and stock price crash risk, timelier disclosures under the monitoring view generate greater benefits to average investors than the informed trading under the information leakage view.

However, injecting additional tension into our analysis, managers' incentives and ability to withhold bad news may be insensitive to director connections for several reasons. First, directors may conclude that information coming from informal channels such as networks is unreliable or irrelevant. Second, given that it is difficult to verify the information set of directors collected through informal social networks, failing to act upon such information may not directly increase directors' legal liability, particularly when the information is potentially noisy. Accordingly, the link between director social networks and crash risk remains unclear.

Examining 28,531 firm-year observations spanning 2000–2015, we find a strong, robust negative link between director external network size and future stock price crash risk. Reflecting its first-order economic impact, as director external network size rises by one standard deviation, future stock price crash risk falls, on average, by 25.58% of the sample mean. This result holds when we control for a comprehensive set of previously documented crash risk determinants. Also, it is plausible that well-connected directors more closely monitor managers due to their superior ability rather than information collection from their networks, or that they provide better advice to help improve firm real performance in the first place, lowering the incidence of bad news. Our results are robust to additionally controlling for director innate ability, their advisory function, or focusing on a set of firms with bad news, reflecting that it would be hard to attribute our results to well-connected directors having greater inherent ability or providing better advice.

Next, we deepen the analysis by relying on cross-sectional variation to help distinguish between the competing explanations. If the monitoring view prevails, then we would expect results to be concentrated in the presence of stronger incentives or greater demand for monitoring. Alternatively, if the information leakage view dominates, then the impact should be more pronounced in the presence of greater opportunities for informed trading. At the firm level, we find that the results are concentrated in firms with more powerful CEOs, with more capable CEOs, with weaker alternative monitoring by the auditor, and subject to stronger shareholder rights protection. At the director level, we find that the results are mainly driven by independent directors, by audit committee members, and by directors lacking firm-specific knowledge. To further explore whether the information leakage view holds, we examine multiple measures of informed trading, including the short interest ratio, option trading volume to stock trading volume ratio, probability of informed trading (PIN) score, inside trading, and institutional ownership. However, we fail to find that our main results vary systematically with informed trading. Collectively, our analysis lends support to the monitoring rather than the information leakage view.

We also conduct tests to tackle the potential identification threat stemming from the possibility that the hiring of a director with an expansive network might be an endogenous firm decision. First, we exploit the relatively exogenous shifts in board composition induced by mergers and acquisitions (M&A) and director retirements. We find that firms with a larger increase in director connections stemming from the addition of new board members enjoy a steeper fall in crash risk from the pre- to the post-M&A year, and that firms with a larger loss in director connections exhibit a greater increase in crash risk from the pre- to post-retirement year. Second, we fail to

find any evidence supporting that directors with better networks refrain from accepting appointments to companies with greater stock price crash risk, implying that it is unlikely that the endogenous company-director match is spuriously responsible for our results.

We make several contributions to extant research. First, we gauge the role that alternative information sources play in crash risk. Prior research extensively examines whether crash risk is shaped by financial reporting quality (Hutton et al. 2009; Kim et al. 2011a; Kim and Zhang 2014, 2016; Zhu 2016; Ertugrul et al. 2016; Lobo et al. 2020), and the manager's incentives and characteristics (Kim et al. 2011b; He 2015; Callen and Fang 2015a; Kim, Li et al. 2016; Kim, Wang et al. 2016). However, most studies implicitly assume that managers control the release of bad news due to their information monopoly, and that the board critically depends on the manager for firm-specific information (Adams and Ferreira 2007), ignoring the presence of directors' own information channels. $ ^{2} $ Our results support the intuition that information shared through directors' social networks narrows information asymmetry between directors and the manager, weakens the incentives and ability of the manager to hoard bad news, and attenuates stock price crash risk.

Second, we extend prior work on the impact of director outside connections on monitoring effectiveness. Specifically, prior research finds that monitoring suffers in the presence of directors with multiple outside directorships and ascribe the findings to director busyness diverting their attention (e.g., Core et al. 1999; Fich and Shivdasani 2006; Falato et al. 2014). However, Ferris et al. (2003) do not detect that these directors shirk their duties on board committees. To the extent that multiple outside directorships also capture director connections, Field et al. (2013) and Brown et al. (2019) report that directorship connections enhance their advisory (not monitoring) contribution to firms. We complement this research by focusing on director external social networks, which capture a broad scope of connections beyond multiple directorships. Compared with multiple outside directorships, our measure of director external social networks potentially confers greater benefits in the form of more access to diverse information sources. Our evidence supports the notion that director external connections strengthen the monitoring function by constraining bad news hoarding. Indeed, we find that even after controlling for multiple outside directorships, our findings remain both statistically and economically significant.

Third, despite the negative consequences of stock price crash for investor welfare and market stability (e.g., Yan 2011), extant research only identifies a few formal control mechanisms that curtail such extreme downside tail risk, including conditional accounting conservatism (Kim and Zhang 2016), effective internal controls (Lobo et al. 2020), and stricter monitoring by auditors (Callen and Fang 2017). Our research has practical implications for firms eager to improve their corporate governance. In evaluating prospective directors, firms could consider the role that director social networks play in preventing bad news hoarding that is behind stock price crashes.

Our study is related to two recent studies examining how director connectedness affects firm performance and financial reporting quality. In analyzing proprietary centrality data to capture a board's connectedness, Larcker et al. (2013) find that firms with well-connected boards have superior accounting performance and stock returns, which they attribute to their better access to resources and information. In another study, Intintoli et al. (2018) find that the connectedness of independent non-co-opted audit committee members exerts a positive monitoring effect on accrual quality and conservatism. However, our analysis is distinct from Larcker et al. (2013) in that they focus on the advisory function of the board in terms of firm performance, whereas we examine its monitoring function evident in stock price crash risk. On one hand, the advisory

function can indirectly lead to a lower incidence of stock price crashes by improving firm performance via a series of real actions (e.g., attracting new customers and adjusting production efficiency). On the other hand, although both functions are likely based on the same information set which flows through the network, their economic impacts on firms and the capital market can be mutually independent since the board of directors can fulfill both functions concurrently. Indeed, we implement a series of empirical tests to help validate that the relation we document between director social connections and stock price crash risk reflects the direct impact of monitoring, rather than the intermediate effect through the advisory channel as suggested in Larcker et al. (2013).

We also complement Intintoli et al. (2018) in that they examine accrual quality and accounting conservatism, whereas we focus on bad news hoarding behavior evident in stock price crashes. Our core results are robust to controlling for various measures of earnings quality and conservatism, suggesting that the role that director connections play in shaping stock price crash risk operates beyond the channel of financial reporting quality. Importantly, measuring bad news hoarding by managers is challenging since they have multiple channels through which to obscure bad economic news such as accrual manipulation, accounting aggressiveness, tax avoidance, classification shifting, off-balance-sheet devices, management earnings guidance, and opaque notes accompanying the financial statements. In contrast, stock price crash risk represents “a far more comprehensive metric that should reflect all manner of managerial bad news hoarding in comparison to a specific channel metric such as accruals manipulation or classification shifting” (Callen and Fang 2017, 1717). In a major upside, relying on this market-based measure minimizes the likelihood of drawing incorrect inferences concerning the link between director connections and managerial bad news hoarding. $ ^{3} $

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews prior theory and evidence in motivating the testable predictions. Section 3 outlines the empirical design and sample construction. Section 4 covers the results, while section 5 concludes.

## 2. Motivation

#### Prior research on stock price crash risk

Jin and Myers (2006) model crash risk from an agency standpoint. They hold that opacity, along with poor investor protection, leads to stock price crash. Given their career and compensation concerns, managers have both the incentive and ability to hide bad news stemming from temporary bad performance by at least partially controlling public access to information about firm fundamentals. Yet, in the limit, as accumulated bad news reaches a tipping point, it is suddenly released to the market at once, leading to a large drop in stock price, or a crash.

In relying on the agency perspective proposed in Jin and Myers (2006), prior empirical research implies that ex post realized stock price crash risk increases with opacity measured with absolute discretionary accruals (Hutton et al. 2009), particularly accruals in more recent years and less reliable accruals (Zhu 2016). Crash risk rises when the bad news hoarding is more evident (e.g., complex tax strategies, Kim et al. 2011a; more ambiguous annual reports, Ertugrul et al. 2016 and Kim et al. 2018; and real earnings smoothing, Khurana et al. 2018) or when the incentives for suppressing bad news are stronger (e.g., executive equity-based compensation, Kim et al. 2011b; CEO overconfidence, Kim, Li et al. 2016 and Kim, Wang et al. 2016; and clawback provisions in compensation contracts, Bao et al. 2018). Prior research identifies several formal control mechanisms that can curtail the extreme downside tail risk, including the passage of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (Fang et al. 2010), financial reporting conservatism (Kim and Zhang 2016),

effective internal control systems (Lobo et al. 2020), the adoption of IFRS (DeFond et al. 2015), and tighter monitoring by auditors through long-term relationships with their clients (Callen and Fang 2017).

Given that crash risk critically hinges on the extent to which the manager can conceal adverse information from internal and external monitors as well as investors, it is crucial to consider whether alternative information sources, such as social networks, weaken the incentive and capacity of the manager to withhold bad news. We help close this gap in extant research.

#### Prior research on director external connections and monitoring

Directors with wide networks are potentially strict monitors for several reasons. First, well-connected directors have greater access to information and resources (Haythornthwaite 1996), which facilitates tougher monitoring. Second, labor market incentives persuade directors to develop their reputations for monitoring (Fama and Jensen 1983). $ ^{4} $ Given that directors' reputations are closely tied to their social connections, it follows that reputation and legal liability concerns motivate well-connected directors to impose more stringent oversight on managers.

Early research on outside directorships focuses on their impact on firm performance (e.g., Core et al. 1999; Ferris et al. 2003; Fich and Shivdasani 2006). Prior work documents that firms suffer various negative outcomes when more directors hold multiple directorships, including lower firm performance, greater CEOs rent extraction, and lower CEO turnover-performance sensitivity (e.g., Core et al. 1999; Fich and White 2003; Fich and Shivdasani 2006). $ ^{5} $ The literature generally attributes these findings to director busyness diverting their attention and undermining monitoring. Reflecting the emergence of social network analysis (i.e., Bianchi et al. 2019), recent research has begun to recognize that multiple directorships capture not only director busyness but also director social connections that lubricate access to information and resources. Field et al. (2013) and Brown et al. (2019) provide evidence that directorship connections enhance their advisory (not monitoring) contribution to firms. $ ^{6} $

As outside directorships reflect both director connections and busyness, it is difficult to disentangle their respective effects on monitoring. Moreover, outside directorships only capture director connections through boardroom ties, while overlooking many other types of social connections. Consequently, the relation between outside directorships and monitoring documented in prior research might not extend to much broader social networks. In contrast, our study captures director social connections through professional experience, educational histories, and civic activities, reflecting directors' access to more diverse information sources beyond boardrooms. From a monitoring perspective, director social networks potentially confer greater benefits in the form of access to more expansive information sources, thereby better facilitating director monitoring than do boardroom ties. We advance extant evidence on director external connections and monitoring by evaluating the role that director social links play.

##### Director external connections and crash risk

Social networks operate as a major conduit for information exchange. Network theory suggests that actors with larger networks and highly connected actors have greater access to information and resources (Haythornthwaite 1996), and are more likely to take on the role of a hub in the network (Jackson et al. 2016). Importantly, actors serving as hubs are well-positioned to collect and

control information, which they can exploit to their advantage. Consistent with external networks transmitting value-creating information, prior research documents real economic effects of social networks. $ ^{7} $ Since monitoring improves when the board is better informed (Adams and Ferreira 2007), we anticipate that information exchanged through external networks facilitates monitoring by the board.

Depending on the direction of information flow, director external networks could affect crash risk through two mechanisms. Under the monitoring view, information travels from networks to directors, enabling them to better monitor the manager. Specifically, boards with more expansive networks access more comprehensive and timelier public and private information relevant to the current operations and future outlook of the firm (e.g., Mizruchi 1996; Haunschild and Beckman 1998; Mol 2001; Nicholson et al. 2004; Larcker et al. 2013), including information about industry trends, market conditions, regulatory shifts, the launch of a new product by peer firms, whether major customers are suffering financial distress, whether major lenders are imposing tight credit rationing, the pending acquisition of an important supplier, potential strategic alliances among competitors, the loss of major customer contracts, and so on. Although some information stemming from social networks is already publicly available, it may fail to register with directors due to their limited attention given their multiple responsibilities. $ ^{8} $ However, information from directors' social networks, including even firm-relevant public information rebroadcast via such networks, may still help attract their attention. In addition, social networks may help directors assimilate firm-specific public information so that they can more accurately evaluate its implications; for example, directors can access more in-depth consultations or opinions from connections in their network. In this manner, social networks also help better focus directors' attention on their monitoring duties. Moreover, even though certain private information conveyed via director networks does not meet the recognition or disclosure criteria under GAAP for the current period, as long as it potentially undermines future performance, its disclosure could still affect the stock price by altering investors' prior beliefs about the firm's prospects. Accordingly, such value-relevant private information also plays an important role in our setting. $ ^{9} $

Public and private information secured from social networks complements disclosures by the manager, ensuring that directors are in a better position to evaluate the risks, threats, and uncertainty faced by the firm that, in turn, make it harder for the manager to conceal bad news. Also, given the potential benefits of corporate information disclosure on reducing litigation risk (Field et al. 2005; Donelson et al. 2012), it is expected that, upon receiving bad news via their social networks, directors would likely respond by urging the manager to make timely and accurate

disclosure in order to preempt litigation risk. $ ^{10} $ Consequently, managers are more inclined to admit and disclose bad news in the presence of a well-connected board. Additionally, directors may also recommend real actions to help avoid poor firm performance, lowering the likelihood of future stock price crashes. Nevertheless, given the uncertainty concerning the efficacy of the real actions, timely disclosure of bad news will effectively transmit an early warning signal that helps prevent future stock price crashes. $ ^{11} $ In the other direction, information collected from informal social networks will not be useful if it is unreliable or does not pertain to the specific firm. Moreover, to the extent that it is difficult for outsiders to clearly observe the private information directors collected through their networks, failure to act upon such private information by directors may not increase their legal liability. $ ^{12} $

Under the information leakage view, material non-public information about the firm flows from directors to firm outsiders through director networks. Despite extensive laws, regulations, and corporate policies prohibiting both the release of private information and its exploitation for trading, leakage could still occur inadvertently through unguarded interactions between a director and connections in their networks, or by sophisticated traders piecing together information from multiple sources (Borgatti and Cross 2003). In examining abnormal trading returns of network connections, prior research implies that privileged information flows from directors to mutual fund managers, short sellers, option traders, and institutional investors (Cohen et al. 2008; Akbas et al. 2016). In particular, Akbas et al. (2016) find that sophisticated traders better anticipate earnings surprises of highly connected firms, and that a larger fraction of negative news concerning such firms is already integrated in prices before the announcement. If such leakage accelerates the impounding of bad news into share prices, then it should alleviate the market surprise when bad news is publicly released. $ ^{13} $ We expect under both the monitoring and information leakage views that crash risk will subside when director external networks are larger, which we formalize in this alternative hypothesis $ ^{14} $:

HYPOTHESIS 1. There is a negative association between the size of director external social networks and stock price crash risk.

Although both views predict a negative association between crash risk and director external network size, the mechanisms at work as well as implications for investors are fundamentally

different. Under the monitoring view, bad news is disclosed through formal voluntary and mandatory disclosure channels on a timely basis. Such disclosure alerts all investors and strengthens corporate governance and investor protection. Under the information leakage view, bad news travels discreetly within exclusive networks and the trading on such news enriches only the small group of connected investors and corporate insiders.

## 3. Sample, variable measurement, and descriptive statistics

#### Data sources and sample

Our sample period starts in 2000, the earliest year with data available in BoardEx for director networks, and ends in 2015, the latest year with complete data to measure one-year-ahead variables. In addition, we collect (i) stock return data from the CRSP; (ii) accounting data from Compustat annual files; (iii) analyst information from I/B/E/S; (iv) institutional ownership data from Thompson-Reuters Institutional Holdings Database; and (v) audit-related data from Audit Analytics. We exclude observations meeting any of the following criteria: (i) non-positive book values of total assets and shareholders' equity; (ii) stock prices of less than one dollar at the end of a year; and (iii) equity return data available in fewer than 26 weeks. After imposing these restrictions, we are left with a panel data set including 28,531 observations for the period ranging from 2000 to 2015.

### Measure of director external social networks

We follow Akbas et al. (2016) by measuring director external social networks to reflect a variety of links through current and former employers, schools attended, military service, and civic activities. Specifically, we first aggregate at the board level each director's social connections with executives, officers, and directors of other firms obtained from BoardEx, and then regress the natural logarithm of board-level total external connections on the natural logarithms of firm size, board size, firm age, number of analysts, and institutional ownership. We rely on the residual values from the regressions to proxy for board-level director external social networks (CONNECTEDNESS). This approach helps ensure that our measure of director networks is not driven by potentially correlated firm characteristics, and helps attenuate potential coefficient bias on CONNECTEDNESS arising from any omitted variables correlated with these firm attributes. $ ^{15} $

## Measures of firm-specific stock price crash risk

We follow extensive prior research in relying on two firm-specific, daily returns-based measures of stock price crash risk for each firm-year observation (e.g., Chen et al. 2001; Jin and Myers 2006; Hutton et al. 2009; Callen and Fang 2015a): (i) the negative coefficient of skewness (NCSKEW); and (ii) the difference in days with negative extreme returns and days with positive extreme returns (COUNT). We employ one-year-ahead NCSKEW and COUNT (NCSKEW $ _{T+1} $ and COUNT $ _{T+1} $) as the dependent variables in our empirical tests. We closely follow Callen and Fang (2017) in the estimation of the two measures of stock price crash risk. $ ^{16} $

##### Research design and control variables

To test the prediction in Hypothesis 1, we estimate the following regression:

 $$ \begin{aligned}CRASHRISK_{j,T+1}=&\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}CONNECTEDNESS_{j,T}+\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}Controls_{j,T}^{k}+YearDummies\\&+IndustryDummies+\varepsilon_{j,T+1},\end{aligned} $$ 

where CRASHRISK $ _{T+1} $ is measured by NCSKEW $ _{T+1} $ and COUNT $ _{T+1} $ in successive estimations. All regressions control for year and industry (i.e., the 2-digit SIC code) fixed effects. Regression equations are estimated using pooled OLS with White standard errors corrected for firm clustering. We focus on the role that CONNECTEDNESS plays in future crash risk. $ ^{17} $

We follow prior research by controlling for these variables in the regressions (Chen et al. 2001; Jin and Myers 2006): the negative coefficient of skewness for firm-specific daily returns ( $ NCSKEW_{T} $); the kurtosis of firm-specific weekly returns ( $ KURTOSIS_{T} $); the standard deviation of firm-specific weekly returns ( $ STDEV_{T} $); the cumulative firm-specific weekly returns ( $ RET_{T} $); the market-to-book ratio ( $ MB\_RATIO_{T} $); the book value of all liabilities divided by the total assets ( $ LEVERAGE_{T} $); the log of market value of equity ( $ LNSIZE_{T} $); the operating income divided by the book value of total assets ( $ ROA_{T} $); the average monthly share turnover over fiscal year T minus the average monthly share turnover over the year T - 1 ( $ DTURNOVER_{T} $); and analyst coverage ( $ ANALYST_{T} $). Consistent with Hutton et al. (2009), we include financial reporting opacity ( $ OPAQUE_{T} $), computed as the three-year moving sum of the absolute value of annual performance-adjusted discretionary accruals (Kothari et al. 2005). To control for real earnings management, we include abnormal production costs ( $ ABN\_PROD_{T} $), abnormal discretionary expense ( $ ABN\_DISEXP_{T} $), and abnormal cash flow from operations ( $ ABN\_CFO_{T} $) (Francis et al. 2016). We also control for the presence of dividend payments ( $ DIV_{T} $) (Kim et al. 2018), auditor tenure ( $ TENURE_{T} $) (Callen and Fang 2017), firm age ( $ AGE_{T} $), director-CEO ties forged through prior employment, education, or civic activities ( $ BOARD\_CEO\_TIE_{T} $) (Bruynseels and Cardinaels 2014), and Big4 ( $ BIGN_{T} $) and industry specialist ( $ SPEC_{T} $) auditor status (Lim and Tan 2008; Reichelt and Wang 2010). We follow Callen and Fang (2013, 2015b) by controlling for the short interest ratio ( $ SIR\_RATIO_{T} $) and institutional ownership ( $ INST\_OWN_{T} $). Finally, we include the strength of SEC monitoring ( $ DISTANCE_{T} $), which equals one if the distance between a firm's headquarters and the closest SEC regional or national office is within 100 km, and zero otherwise (Kedia and Rajgopal 2011). In the Appendix, we provide the variable specifications.

### Alternative explanations: Director advisory function and innate ability

The advisory function conducted by well-connected directors may translate into better performance (Larcker et al. 2013), which, in turn, lowers the incidence of bad news. To disentangle the direct monitoring effect from the indirect advisory effect, we undertake several tests. First, in our main regression, we explicitly include stock returns (RET) as a control to help dispel the concern that our core evidence is simply driven by firm performance. Unreported results show that even after excluding RET from the regressions, we find nearly identical results regarding both the magnitudes and t-statistics, reinforcing that the relation between CONNECTEDNESS and future crash risk reflects a direct effect of the monitoring channel rather than the intermediary impact through firm performance (i.e., the advisory channel). In another unreported sensitivity test, we also control for the occurrence of the negative event year using the loss dummy variable which equals one if RET is negative, and zero otherwise. The coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS remain very similar and significant at the 1% level. It is also plausible that informed boards provide good advice to management (e.g., insist that firms abandon negative NPV projects on a timely basis), although the effect is not reflected in stock returns until future years. To tackle this possibility, in Table 3, models (3)–(5), we successively utilize one-year-ahead, two-year-ahead, and three-year-ahead RETs to capture the future impact of the advisory

channel, and find that the coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS remain negative and significant at the 5% level or better. $ ^{20} $

Further, we analyze whether the monitoring and advisory channels through which director external networks affect crash risk are independent. To the extent that the two channels are independent, we would still observe the impact of CONNECTEDNESS on managerial bad news hoarding reflected through future stock price crash risk even if well-connected directors fail to effectively fulfill their advisory responsibility. In other words, even without a well-functioning advisory channel, we expect that the monitoring channel still plays a first-order role in shaping future crash risk. To test this conjecture, we focus on a subsample of observations with future (i.e., one-year-ahead) firm-specific stock returns in the bottom tercile of the sample distribution, which most likely represents the failure of well-connected directors to fulfill their advisory responsibility. Since this subsample enables us to test the effects of the monitoring channel while holding constant the presence of bad news, it helps dispel the concern that our results are driven by the lack of bad news stemming from well-connected directors delivering more effective advisory services. Supporting our main results, we find that CONNECTEDNESS remains negative and significant at the 1% level in Table 3, model (6). In panel B, we find very similar results when we measure stock price crash risk with  $ COUNT_{T+1} $. We interpret this evidence as not only direct support of the monitoring channel, but also as evidence that the monitoring channel affects stock price crash risk independently of the advisory channel.

Moreover, we directly examine whether board connections are associated with the extent of bad news by substituting future stock returns for crash risk as the dependent variable. If firms with better-connected boards release less bad news, then we would expect to observe a positive relation between CONNECTEDNESS and future stock returns. In untabulated results, we find that CONNECTEDNESS has no perceptible impact on future stock returns (t-statistic = -0.46). Collectively, although more effective advisory by well-connected directors could reduce crash risk by improving firm performance, our evidence is primarily explained by their monitoring role. $ ^{21} $

It is possible that well-connected directors are inherently more capable and can more effectively monitor the manager even without the help of additional information from their network, that is, director innate ability might be a correlated omitted variable. To address this concern, we follow Intintoli et al. (2018) by controlling for four variables capturing director innate ability: (i) the mean value of external board seats held by directors, (ii) the percentage of directors graduating from prestigious schools based on the school list in Intintoli et al. (2018), (iii) the mean value of professional appointments at the vice president or higher level held by directors, and (iv) the average age of directors. In addition, we control for the average educational level of directors. We assign a score of 3, 2, and 1 to PhD degree, master's degree or equivalent, and anything else, respectively. Directors with higher innate ability likely hold more external board seats, graduate from more prestigious schools, have a greater number of high-level appointments, and earn advanced degrees. We also expect older directors to accumulate larger social networks than younger directors. In untabulated results, the coefficient of

CONNECTEDNESS remains negative and significant at the 1% level using both measures of crash risk. The coefficient reflecting the mean number of external board seats held by directors is negative and significant at the 5% level when  $ COUNT_{T+1} $ is the dependent variable; otherwise, none of the director innate ability variables is significant. This analysis suggests that our main results are robust to controlling for director innate ability. However, given that we cannot possibly control for all dimensions of director quality, it is important to exercise caution in interpreting our results.

#### Cross-sectional analyses: Monitoring explanation

Next, we deepen our analysis by exploring several factors that may prompt cross-sectional differences in the economic consequences of director networks to investors: (i) the severity of agency conflicts evident in CEO characteristics; (ii) the strength of external auditor monitoring; (iii) the protection of shareholders' rights; and (iv) the degree of directors' monitoring incentives.

#### Agency conflicts

Prior research implies that director networks will have a larger impact on constraining crash risk when CEO power is high. Lisic et al. (2016) argue that a powerful CEO is more apt to provide the board with low-quality information or less information, which undermines the monitoring process. Friedman (2014) develops an agency model holding that a powerful CEO can extract rents by manipulating financial reporting to exaggerate firm performance. Abernethy et al. (2015) document that powerful CEOs exploit their influence over corporate policy in pursuing private benefits. A similar rationale applies to CEO ability. Koester et al. (2017) and Habib and Hasan (2014) find that firms with superior managerial ability exhibit more tax avoidance and more severe empire building in the form of overinvestment, both of which facilitate managerial bad news hoarding (Kim et al. 2011a; Kim, Li et al. 2016; Kim, Wang et al. 2016). Consequently, we predict that the impact of director networks on future crash risk is more salient when a firm's CEO has more power or ability to suppress firm-specific bad news because directors will be more likely to rely on the knowledge obtained from their own social networks in helping identify and deter bad news hoarding in this situation. Alternatively, when CEOs become more powerful, they would be more capable of withholding bad news or resisting director monitoring, which further impedes directors, irrespective of their connectivity, from fulfilling their monitoring role. It follows that the effectiveness of director external connectedness on constraining managerial bad news hoarding would be weaker in this case.

Rooted in prior research (e.g., Abernethy et al. 2015; Finkelstein 1992; Coles et al. 2014; Lisic et al. 2016), we employ principal component analysis to derive a unidimensional construct from the following six attributes: (i) the number of board committees on which the CEO serves; (ii) the length of the CEO's tenure; (iii) whether the CEO is a company founder; (iv) whether the CEO chairs the board; (v) the fraction of directors appointed after the CEO assumes office (i.e., co-opted directors); and (vi) the equity stakes held by the largest five institutional investors. Specifically, we create a factor score that reflects the relative importance of each of the observed items (CEO_POWER). We also follow Demerjian et al. (2012) by gauging CEO ability (CEO_ABILITY) based on CEOs' efficiency at generating greater output from a given set of inputs. We re-estimate equation (1) after interacting CONNECTEDNESS with CEO_POWER and CEO_ABILITY.

Callen and Fang (2017) document that auditor tenure is negatively related to stock price crash risk. Their analysis lends support to the monitoring-by-learning perspective in that development of client-specific knowledge over the course of the auditor-client relationship enhances auditors' ability to detect and deter bad news hoarding activities. $ ^{22} $ Moreover, extant research

suggests that, relative to nonspecialist auditors, industry specialist auditors, which focus intently on developing their expertise and protecting their valuable reputations, conduct higher-quality audits (Lim and Tan 2008; Reichelt and Wang 2010). Relevant to our research questions, Robin and Zhang (2015) report an inverse relation between auditor industry specialization and stock price crash risk. We expect that the impact of director networks on future crash risk is less salient for firms with strong external monitoring measured by longer audit-client relationships and the presence of specialist auditors. Accordingly, we re-estimate equation (1) after adding the interaction terms CONNECTEDNESS × TENURE and CONNECTEDNESS × SPEC.

Shleifer and Vishny (1997) suggest that the protection of shareholders’ rights plays a major role in persuading directors to strictly monitor managers. We expect that the impact of director networks on future crash risk is concentrated in firms subject to stronger investor protection. We initially capture investor protection with the governance index (GINDEX) derived by Gompers et al. (2003), which is based on 24 antitakeover provisions set by the Investor Responsibility Research Center (IRRC). $ ^{23} $ A higher value of GINDEX reflects lax corporate governance, fewer shareholder rights, and poor shareholder protection. We code LO_G as one for observations having a GINDEX value below the sample median, and zero otherwise. Our second measure of investor protection is based on the staggered adoption of universal demand laws at the state level. Houston et al. (2018) suggest that the enactment of universal demand law compromises shareholders’ litigation rights against corporate management by adding significant obstacles to derivative lawsuits, causing shareholders to experience worse agency conflicts. We set NOUDL to one if a firm is headquartered in a state that has not adopted universal demand law by year T, and zero otherwise. We re-estimate equation (1) after separately interacting CONNECTEDNESS with LO_G and NOUDL.

In Table 4, the coefficient estimates on the interaction term CONNECTEDNESS×CEO_POWER are negative and significant at the 10% level or better across both crash risk specifications, while the interaction terms CONNECTEDNESS×CEO_ABILITY only loads negatively (at the 5% level) in the  $ COUNT_{T+1} $ regression. Table 5 shows that the coefficient estimates on the interaction term CONNECTEDNESS×TENURE and CONNECTEDNESS×SPEC are positive and significant at the 10% level or better in both crash risk specifications. In Table 6, we find that the coefficient estimates on the interaction terms CONNECTEDNESS×LO_G and CONNECTEDNESS×NOUDL enter negatively at the 10% level or better in both crash risk specifications. Altogether, the results in Tables 4–6 suggest that better-informed directors are more capable of restricting bad news hoarding in the presence of more intensive agency conflicts stemming from managerial power, weaker monitoring by external auditors, and tougher shareholders' protection, supporting the monitoring view as an explanation for the main findings.

The interaction term POSTMA×LARGE_INCREASE captures the incremental change in crash risk for acquirers with larger increases in director networks from the pre- to post-M&A period relative to acquirers with smaller increases. Among the 1,065 M&A transactions, the mean (median) increase in board network size is 428 (59) (untabulated). In Table 9, we report in models (1) and (2) of panel A that the coefficient for POSTMA×LARGE_INCREASE loads negatively at the 10% and 5% levels, respectively. Exploring within-firm variation pursuant to the M&A shock, we find that acquirers with a greater increase in director networks experience a sharper fall in crash risk from the pre- to post-M&A period.

Central to justifying the DID design is validating the parallel trends assumption under which we expect no differential trend in crash risk between the treatment and control groups absent the shock from M&A activity. After Lennox (2016), we focus on observations from the pre-M&A period and regress crash risk on LARGE_INCREASE, TREND, and LARGE_INCREASE×TREND. TREND takes the value of  $ -3 $,  $ -2 $, and  $ -1 $ for the corresponding year before M&A activities. A negative coefficient for the interaction would indicate a decreasing trend in crash risk for the treatment group before the treatment. Untabulated evidence shows that the interaction term has no perceptible impact across both crash risk measures, providing some assurance that parallel trends assumption is defensible. $ ^{26} $

#### Stock price crash risk and subsequent changes in director networks

The alternative explanation of endogenous director-company match predicts a negative association between future changes in director networks and past crash risk. After recent research on board attributes (Badolato et al. 2014; Larcker et al. 2013), we directly test this explanation by regressing changes in director networks from year T-1 to T (ΔCONNECTEDNESS) on the lagged values of both measures of crash risk and all control variables in equation (1). We use the prior-year crash risk since it is observable to directors who contemplate joining or departing the company. In Table 9, panel B, we find that the coefficients on both lagged crash risk variables are statistically insignificant and nearly zero. It is possible that only more reputable directors with large networks and, in turn, more employment options are more selective concerning the boards on which to serve. In an untabulated test, we restrict the analysis to observations with the addition or departure of directors whose network size is above the sample median value, and find that the both lagged crash risk variables remain insignificant. Overall, we fail to find any evidence that directors with large networks are less likely to match with firms with greater stock price crash risk.

#### Additional analyses

### Centrality of director connections

Although our main test variable CONNnectEDNESS follows Akbas et al. (2016) by primarily gauging director network size, it is also important to consider the quality of board connections. Next, we follow Larcker et al. (2013) and Intintoli et al. (2018) by measuring connection quality (centrality) based on the four centrality proxies commonly used in the network literature: degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. Intintoli et al. (2018) provide detailed specifications of these proxies in their Appendix. Reflecting that they capture related underlying constructs, the centrality proxies are all highly correlated with each other (Pearson correlation coefficients range from 0.303 to 0.826). Accordingly, we implement factor analysis to extract the common factor (FACTOR) from the four underlying centrality proxies to explain the variation across them (Intintoli et al. 2018). ##### Channels of director connections based on experiences

To provide insight on whether the negative relation between director connections and stock price crash risk varies with directors' experiences, we decompose CONNECTEDNESS along these three dimensions: (i) connections built through directorships (CONNECTEDNESS_DIR) versus through all other channels (CONNECTEDNESS_OTHER); (ii) connections built through employment (CONNECTEDNESS_EMPLOY), education (CONNECTEDNESS_EDUCATION), and involvement in civic and non-for-profit activities (CONNECTEDNESS_NFP); and (iii) connections built through positions currently held (CONNECTEDNESS_CURRENT) versus through positions held in the past (CONNECTEDNESS_PAST). We construct each component using a similar algorithm as the construction of CONNECTEDNESS.

In models (3) and (4) of Table 10, we find that, while the coefficient on CONNECTEDNESS_DIR is significant only when NCSKEW is the dependent variable, the coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS_OTHER are negative and significant for both crash risk measures. Further, untabulated tests suggest that the magnitudes of the coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS_OTHER are not significantly different from those on CONNECTEDNESS_DIR. Taken together, the evidence suggests that director connections through nondirectorship activities play at least as an important role as director connections through directorships in affecting stock price crash risk.

In models (5) and (6), we find that the coefficient on CONNECTEDNESS_EMPLOY is negative and significant for both crash risk specifications; in contrast, the coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS_EDUCATION and CONNECTEDNESS_NFP are insignificant when NCSKEW is the dependent variable. Untabulated tests imply that the magnitudes of the coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS_EMPLOY are at least as large as those on CONNECTEDNESS_EDUCATION and CONNECTEDNESS_NFP. This evidence implies that, to curb managerial bad news hoarding, directors heavily rely on connections built through employment, most likely because director communication with employment-based connections mainly centers on job-related topics. Finally, in models (7) and (8), we find that the coefficients on CONNECTEDNESS_CURRENT and CONNECTEDNESS_PAST both enter significantly negatively, with the coefficient magnitude on the latter being significantly larger than that on the former, implying that connections involving longer relationships are more constructive for mitigating crash risk.

## 5. Conclusions

The agency costs perspective of manager bad news hoarding proposed by Jin and Myers (2006) is a widely accepted explanation for future stock price risk. However, despite the large crash risk literature built on this premise, evidence on whether information from alternate channels weakens the manager's control of bad news remains scarce. Our analysis suggests that the external networks of the boards attenuate future crash risk, with this link varying predictably with firm and director characteristics. At the firm level, the results are more pronounced for firms with more powerful and competent CEOs, firms with weaker auditor monitoring, and firms subject to stronger shareholder protection rights. At the director level, our evidence is mainly driven by directors with stronger incentives or responsibility to monitor and directors who will likely benefit more due to their lack of firm-specific knowledge. We fail to find evidence that our main results are driven by firms with greater informed trading. Collectively, we provide robust evidence consistent with the monitoring view and no support for the information leakage view.

Overall, the evidence suggests that information exchange via director networks weakens the information monopoly of managers, constraining them from suppressing bad news that, in turn, engenders stock price crash risk. Future research could explore how Internet information intermediaries, who have positive capital market impacts (Drake et al. 2017), facilitate the flow of information. This analysis could broaden our understanding of the role that alternative information channels play in shaping stock price crash risk.
