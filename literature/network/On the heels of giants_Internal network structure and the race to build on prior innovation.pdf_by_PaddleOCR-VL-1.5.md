## Abstract

Research Summary: Strategy research has long been concerned with how firms build on the technological knowledge they create, and has focused on legal enforcement, complementary assets, and location decisions. Less attention has been paid to how internal firm structures support the appropriation of future cumulative innovations: what has been termed "generative appropriability." Drawing on innovation and social network research, we propose that more integrated intrafirm inventor networks, and those with nearly decomposable structures, facilitate generative appropriability by accelerating within-firm generation of follow-on innovations, thus outpacing rivals. We find evidence for these effects in patent data from 1,417 large corporations over 26 years. The acceleration effect is strongest in the critical first few years after an initial invention.

Managerial Summary: Innovation is a cumulative and competitive process, so if firms fail to build on their own innovations quickly, rivals will capture value by

doing so. We study how the degree to which a firm's inventors are connected to each other through copatenting relationships is related to its ability to build on its prior innovations faster than rivals. We find that firms whose researchers are connected to more of its other researchers are better able to quickly develop follow-on innovations, but this advantage decays over time. We also find that the pattern of connectedness most associated with this outcome is one in which tight clusters of researchers in the core of the network are linked to each other by "bridging ties."

generative appropriability, inventor networks, organizational structure, patents, technology and innovation management

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Gaining or sustaining competitive advantage often requires firms to build on their own innovations before rivals do (Kogut & Zander, 1992; Scotchmer, 1991). Some firms fail in this effort. For example, Merck developed the antiviral Boceprevir to treat hepatitis C in the early 2000s, yet it was Pfizer that repurposed a key component of Boceprevir to treat COVID at the height of the pandemic. Pfizer's Paxlovid went on to generate $17 billion in 2022, while Merck watched from the sidelines (Lauerman, 2022). On the other hand, some firms succeed in this pursuit. For example, Genentech built upon its initial research on monoclonal antibodies to develop a string of successful drugs to treat a wide range of conditions, such as cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, and autoimmune diseases. Ahuja, Lampert & Novelli (2013) termed a firm's effectiveness in building on its own innovations before its rivals do "generative appropriability," and defined it as "a firm's effectiveness in capturing the greatest share of future inventions spawned by its existing inventions" (p. 248). They highlighted how generative appropriability encompasses both the speed at which a firm develops follow-on innovations from its own innovations and its ability to prevent rivals from expropriating that knowledge.

Strategy research on the determinants of appropriability by firms, generative and otherwise, has identified various factors, such as the use of patents (Somaya, 2012), employee noncompete agreements (Marx et al., 2009), selection of geographic locations (Alcácer & Chung, 2007), ownership of complementary assets (Teece, 1986), and early market entry (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988). There has been little empirical research, however, on how structures internal to the firm affect appropriability of any kind. This is surprising given that organization structure has long been recognized as a critical channel for knowledge flows and innovation (Simon, 1991), while also being a lever subject to managerial control (Argyres & Silverman, 2004).

In related work, Liebeskind (1996) proposed that job designs can reduce knowledge leakage by dividing tasks so that few employees possess sufficient knowledge to leak, but warned that such moves might also impinge on organizational integration mechanisms that are needed to build cumulatively on the firm's knowledge (Grant, 1996). Ahuja et al. (2013) discussed how

incentive systems, researcher team sizes and organizational structures can affect generative appropriability. To our knowledge, however, the only systematic empirical study of how internal structures affect appropriability is Zhao (2006), who found evidence that multinational firms protect the technologies they develop in countries with weak legal intellectual property regimes by using them more internally, and by linking them more tightly to other technologies of the firm.

We extend this research stream by investigating how one aspect of internal structure—the degree of structural integration within an intrafirm network of inventors—might help a firm accelerate rate of its generative appropriability. Network integration has been defined as the degree to which formerly disconnected subgroups form new bridges to each other (Argyres et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2023; Kogut et al., 2007). When such integration involves networks of inventors, the novel ties among subgroups have been shown to provide inventors with “opportunities for reciprocal knowledge exchange” (Carnabuci & Operti, 2013, p. 1593), and “[bring] together diverse good ideas and knowledge from different subgroups” (Chang et al., 2023).

Our analysis thus follows the literature relating inventor network structure to innovation (Grigoriou & Rothaermel, 2017; Moreira et al., 2018; Reagans & McEvily, 2003). We contend that greater integration of a firm's inventor network increases its inventors' exposure to knowledge about projects in which they are not directly involved, increasing opportunities for recombination and cumulative innovation. Thus, firms with more integrated intrafirm inventor networks build on their own technologies faster, thereby improving generative appropriability. However, increased connectivity might also involve trade-offs: Although it speeds the flow of knowledge within the firm, the increased number of within-firm inventors knowledgeable about an innovation may also facilitate knowledge leakage to rivals. Such leakage might over time reduce a firm's ability to preclude others from building on that innovation. In addition, increasing integration might hamper exploration and innovation, as inventors' knowledge becomes homogenized (Fang et al., 2010). Thus, it is important to empirically document the dynamic relationship between integration and innovation. Finally, we explore Ahuja et al.'s (2013) proposition that networks with nearly decomposable structures mitigate some of the tradeoffs produced by integration, and thus enhance generative appropriability more effectively than otherwise similar nondecomposable or fully decomposable networks.

To test our hypotheses, we study the internal co-invention networks and patent citation patterns of 1,417 large American corporations from 1986 to 2019. Analyzing the patterns of citations by the focal firm relative to its rivals, we find evidence consistent with the notion that greater network integration among a firm's inventors enables a focal firm to cumulatively build upon a given invention faster than its rivals. We also find evidence that the generative appropriability advantages afforded by such networks decay over time. Finally, the benefits of integration are more pronounced to the extent that increased connections coincide with nearly decomposable network structures.

## 2 | LITERATURE AND HYPOTHESES

### 2.1 | Knowledge, imitation, and the speed of generative appropriability

Strategy scholars have long emphasized that a firm's competitive advantage often derives from the development of unique and difficult-to-imitate knowledge, particularly of a technological

nature (Kogut & Zander, 1996; Spender, 1996). In this view, firms are seen as institutions that serve to coordinate and integrate diverse and specialized knowledge stocks, which are often held by individual employees (Grant, 1996). The literature on dynamic capabilities similarly highlights the firm's ability to continuously upgrade its knowledge base in response to environmental changes (Teece et al., 1997).

Given the centrality of technological knowledge to competitive advantage, protection of that knowledge is paramount. This is because rivals constantly seek ways to build on the knowledge created by other firms. Ahuja et al.'s (2013) construct of generative appropriability highlights that not only must a firm build upon its knowledge, it must do so more effectively than rivals to prevent expropriation. $ ^{1} $ Ahuja et al. propose two components of generative appropriability: (1) a cumulative invention component, a focal firm's effectiveness in creating new inventions building on its prior inventions, and (2) a preclusive component, the effectiveness in preventing others from building inventions based on the focal firm's inventions. Much work has focused on strategic, legal and institutional mechanisms of protection against expropriation, such as location choices (Alcácer & Chung, 2007); patent enforcement efforts (Agarwal et al., 2009); confidentiality and employee noncompete agreements (Marx et al., 2009); and job design (Liebeskind, 1996; Rajan & Zingales, 2001). Recent work has also called attention to subtler internal mechanisms available to firms in the pursuit of knowledge protection, such as the ways in which knowledge is manifested in prototypes, formulas, routines, blueprints, and the like (Sharapov & Macaulay, 2022). $ ^{2} $

Extending this work, we propose that organizational structure can affect a firm's generative appropriability by influencing the speed of follow-on invention. As Kogut and Zander (1992, p. 393) note, knowledge-based competition can be characterized as “a race between an innovator and the ability of the imitating firm [to] reverse engineer...the substantive technology. The growth of the firm is determined by a combination of the speed of technology transfer and of the imitative efforts of rivals.” Thus, to the extent that a firm is better able to rapidly build upon its own innovations, it will more successfully pre-empt rivals from doing so. In the next section, we propose how structure can facilitate intrafirm knowledge flow, increasing the speed of generative appropriability.

### 2.2 | Inventor network structures and generative appropriability

Scholars have demonstrated that co-invention networks affect the flow of knowledge in organizations. At the individual level, Singh (2005) finds evidence that inventors' knowledge is more likely to flow to someone with whom they have previously co-invented than to a non-collaborator. Sorenson et al. (2006) similarly find that an inventor's knowledge is more likely to diffuse to socially proximate inventors (as measured by co-invention), and that this diffusion rate decays slowly with degrees of separation between two inventors in a co-invention network.

Singh and Agrawal (2011) find that the knowledge of a newly hired inventor diffuses more readily to incumbent inventors who collaborate with the inventor than to non-collaborating inventors (see also Tzabbar et al., 2015).

The literature shows similar patterns at the organizational level. For example, Guler and Nerkar (2012) demonstrate that a firm's internal inventor network influences the extent and type of innovations generated and absorbed. Carnabuci and Operti (2013) find that firms with more integrated inventor networks and a more diverse knowledge base tend to mitigate the trade-off between recombining new knowledge vs. recombining prior knowledge. Moreira et al. (2018) show how such structures can speed the absorption of external knowledge. Collectively, these studies imply that more integrated networks may spur the development of follow-on innovations necessary for exploiting earlier innovations. $ ^{3} $

Conversely, as put by one pharmaceutical R&D manager, the absence of linkages among a firm's inventors can prevent a firm from building upon its own knowledge:

“…an idea in one area may be able to be translated into another therapeutic area. Quite often an indication may be unsuccessful in one therapeutic domain but have legs in another, however with the wrong structure (they) may not be able to take advantage of this.” (Balachandran & Eklund, 2023)

What kinds of information pass through a firm's internal inventor networks to facilitate the process of building on prior innovations? We suggest that there are at least three types. First, information about the existence of new innovations on which the firm can build may flow through such networks, making inventors in disparate locations or subunits of the firm aware of new opportunities. The role of social networks in transmitting such information has long been emphasized by network scholars (Obstfeld, 2005; Reagans & Zuckerman, 2001). Second, tacit knowledge may flow through internal inventor networks. Such uncodified knowledge is excluded from patent applications or invention descriptions, and hence unavailable to rivals, while interpersonal communication may facilitate building on the original innovation (Winter, 1987).

Finally, a large literature emphasizes the importance of learning from failures in general (Cannon & Edmondson, 2005; Sitkin, 1992), and from failed R&D projects in particular (Eggers, 2012; Khanna et al., 2016). Thus, information about prior failed innovative efforts related to an innovation may be transmitted through internal inventor networks. Such information is not publicly disclosed, yet may be extremely valuable in guiding efforts to build upon an initial innovation by enabling researchers to avoid dead-ends in the research and development process.

The foregoing highlights the importance of understanding which features of intrafirm networks enable faster diffusion of information within the firm, relative to its diffusion to rivals. Given the demonstrated role of network ties as conduits of information, we propose that firms with more integrated internal inventor networks enjoy more robust flows of these three types of

information across a wide range of inventors. Such firms will therefore more efficiently build on their existing innovations. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. Greater integration of a firm's internal inventor network is associated with greater generative appropriability.

### 2.3 | The speed of generative appropriability

Despite the availability of numerous mechanisms for protecting a firm's intellectual property, all but the most complex and/or tacit technological knowledge eventually diffuses across organizational, technological, and geographic boundaries (Jaffe et al., 1993; Sorenson et al., 2006). Studies of innovation have repeatedly demonstrated that rival firms eventually manage to reverse engineer or "invent around" valuable patents (Cohen et al., 2000; Mansfield et al., 1981). In addition, researchers often move to rival firms, or start their own firms, bringing technological knowledge from their previous employer with them (Campbell et al., 2012; Marx et al., 2009). Some inventors also advertently or inadvertently reveal valuable technological knowledge to buyers, suppliers, or even rivals, through social connections external to the firm (Saxenian, 1996). Therefore, generative appropriability naturally declines over time.

We contend that this natural tendency will be exacerbated for firms with more integrated internal inventor networks. The reason is that knowledge of a firm's innovation spreads faster and more widely in firms with more integrated internal inventor networks because more connections create more paths for it to flow (Schilling & Fang, 2014). Because more of the firm's researchers will become aware of a given innovation, firms with more integrated networks will be at greater risk of knowledge leakage to rivals (Liebeskind, 1996). Therefore, firms with integrated networks will have a shorter time window within which to build on their own knowledge before leaks and spillovers enable rivals to develop follow-on or imitative innovations (Ceccagnoli, 2005; Winter, 1987). On the other hand, firms with less integrated networks will be able to preclude rivals' innovation for longer due to slower leakage of knowledge. The implication is that the advantage in producing follow-on innovations provided by a more integrated internal network is temporary, declining over time. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. The positive relationship between the integration of a firm's internal inventor network and its generative appropriability is strongest immediately after the initial invention, and weakens as the initial invention ages.

### 2.4 | Nearly decomposable networks and generative appropriability

The above hypotheses contend that greater internal network integration supports greater generative appropriability, at least in the short term. Ahuja et al. (2013) propose that one particular pattern of integration dominates other forms of integration in achieving generative appropriability: “near decomposability” (Sanchez & Mahoney, 1996; Simon, 1962). Nearly decomposable structures consist of subunits within which interactions are frequent and intense, and between which interactions are less frequent and less intense (Simon, 1962). Per Ahuja et al. (2013), nearly decomposable R&D structures within firms facilitate cumulative invention because they effectively manage the tradeoff between specialization and coordination.

Specifically, subunits capture the benefits of specialization and responsiveness to local needs in their R&D activities, while structures connecting the subunits capture the benefits of broader knowledge sharing and coordination (Argyres & Silverman, 2004; Kay, 1988).

While Ahuja et al. (2013) focused on formal reporting structures, we contend that their logic also applies to intrafirm inventor network structures because innovations obviously emerge from both formal and informal processes. Tight clusters of researchers enable information for follow-on innovations to flow between specialists in particular technical areas, while bridging ties between these clusters create the information channels needed for follow-on innovations to be made in other technological domains of the firm. Therefore, following Ahuja et al. (2013), we predict that:

Hypothesis 3. Near decomposability of an internal inventor network positively moderates the relationship between network integration and generative appropriability.

## 3 | DATA AND MEASURES

Our data are derived from three sources: the European Patent Office's (EPO's) PATSTAT database, Bureau van Dijk's ORBIS database, and COMPUSTAT. We collected patent data for the time period 1986 through 2019 from the PATSTAT database, which features perhaps the most comprehensive global coverage of patenting in existence. We linked patents to original owners using ownership structure data from ORBIS, following Rios (2021). This procedure allowed us to link to corporate parents any patents assigned to their wholly owned subsidiaries.

This is important because patents assigned to subsidiaries might be mistaken for external patents, even though they were generated within the firm (Arora et al., 2014).

Consistent with prior work, we limit our sample to firms with more than 250 US patents over the sample period to ensure that we focus on firms for whom patenting is an important economic activity. We also limit the sample to firms that appear in COMPUSTAT, so that we can control for relevant firm characteristics. The resulting panel matches all patents, applications, and reassignments between 1986 and 2013 for 1,417 firms, and allows us to record citations, renewals, foreign applications, divisionals, and continuations to these through 2019. The final sample includes measures at both the patent-family level  $ (n = 430,061) $ and the firm-year level  $ (n = 45,248) $.

We adhere to conventional practice in our use of patent data, with the exception of adopting two improvements to traditional variable construction. First, rather than treating each patent as a stand-alone entity, we follow recent work that aggregates patents to the domestic priority family level (Kuhn et al., 2020; Rios, 2021). Patent families are groups of patents and ungranted patent applications whose members all trace back to the same initial priority application. Patent families thus capture the full impact of a technological advance more clearly than a simple tabulation of patents (Martínez, 2011). Figure A1 in the Online Appendix provides an illustrative example from the patents earned by Square, Inc. The seminal technology behind Square's payment system spawned a large domestic family of continuations that claimed the earlier priority date and covered the exact technical content (i.e., drawings and descriptions) but refined and extended the claims or application of the technology. The resulting nine-patent domestic family comprises a bundle of legal rights encapsulating one invention, rather than multiple independent inventions. Aggregating forward citations at the level of the domestic family is therefore a

more accurate way of capturing the impact that a firm’s invention has on the internal and external innovation landscape. $ ^{4} $

Second, we include citations to patent applications as well as to granted patents. Patent research has traditionally included only citations to already-granted patents, largely because these data were easy to collect. However, because patent applications are published 18 months after submission, patent applications also receive a large number of citations. Kuhn et al. (2020) demonstrate that the inclusion of such citations substantially challenges much “received wisdom” in the literature.

Given our focus on the value of rapid follow-on innovation and knowledge protection, such citations are likely to be particularly important. For example, a patent for Google Glass was published on March 9, 2006 (see Online Appendix Figure A2). The US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) ultimately granted the patent on April 13, 2010. The ungranted patent application received 97 citations (all prior to April 13, 2010), while the granted patent received 42 citations between April 14, 2010 and June 2022. Thus, omitting citations to patent applications can change the interpretation of an innovation's impact, and, particularly important for this study, fail to identify follow-on innovations that are developed quickly after an initial innovation. ### 3.1 | Dependent variable

#### 3.1.1 | Self-citation ratio

Our measure of generative appropriability is a patent-family's forward self-citation ratio. As Ahuja et al. (2013, p. 266) note, “A simple archival measure of [generative appropriability] is provided by the ratio of self-citations to a firm's patents to the total citations received by the firm's patents.” Their logic follows much work that views citations as a reasonable albeit noisy proxy for cumulative innovation, with the citing patent building on the cited patent (Galasso & Schankerman, 2015; Jaffe et al., 1993). In this view, self-citations reflect a firm's appropriation of the value of its inventions through follow-on inventions, while citations by others reflect knowledge leakage or expropriation. The self-citation ratio has been used to measure appropriation by Belenzon (2012) and Novelli (2015), among others.

One concern may be that the self-citation ratio is far removed from the actual returns to subsequent innovations. To mitigate this concern, we demonstrate in post hoc analyses that this ratio is in fact positively related to patent value in our sample. For now, we note that prior research suggests a correlation between self-citation rates and commercial success in a range of settings. Wagner and Wakeman (2016) find evidence that self-citations are associated with the commercial success of new pharmaceuticals. Similarly, Moser et al. (2018) find that versions of hybrid corn created to generate valuable follow-on versions received nearly 50% more self-citations than hybrids that were not generative in this way.

### 3.2 | Independent variables

#### 3.2.1 | Intrafirm inventor networks

Our measures of the structure of a firm's intrafirm inventor network consider each inventor in the firm to be a node, and are calculated based on nondirectional ties that are recorded each time an inventor collaborates on a patent application with another inventor within the firm. $ ^{6} $ These ties are observed separately for each firm-year, and are based on patent applications filed in that year.

Unlike most social networks, which are defined by realized connections, intrafirm networks often include a multitude of disconnected subnetworks or components. $ ^{7} $ This is because many subgroups do not interact, and therefore have no path to other parts of the firm. Another difference with most social networks is that the boundary of the firm allows us to identify who is at risk of connecting to a component at any point in time. Network integration can therefore be measured based on patterns of ties among network components within a firm (Carnabuci & Operti, 2013; Chang et al., 2023). This conceptualization of network integration allows us to consider the contribution of isolated components, which have been largely ignored in prior studies (Cattani & Ferriani, 2008; Gulati et al., 2012; Uzzi & Spiro, 2005).

Inventor networks are more or less integrated for numerous reasons; for example, clusters may exist because researchers are separated geographically (Singh & Marx, 2013) or organizationally (Arora et al., 2014). For the purposes of this study, we are agnostic as to the determinants of structure because whether a group of inventors is isolated from the core network of inventors due to geography or organization design should not change the fact that this isolation is likely, on average, to reduce the potential for knowledge transfer. The literature on social networks has tended to take such networks as exogenous, and has only recently begun to investigate the determinants of network structures (e.g., Ahuja et al., 2012).

While it may seem straightforward to think of a network as more or less integrated, formal measures of network integration have many dimensions and can be conceptualized in several different ways. The utility of each concept depends on the nature of the data and the phenomenon being investigated. At a whole-network level, a network is maximally integrated if there is a path between every pair of nodes (Wasserman & Faust, 1994), and becomes less integrated with each additional isolated “component” in the network, where a component refers to a node or group of nodes disconnected to any other group (Carnabuci & Operti, 2013). The largest component in the network is termed the “giant” component (Bollobás, 2001).

Most studies that examine inventor networks analyze the properties of this giant component, because standard ego-network measures necessitate that there be at least one edge connecting all nodes in a network (Nerkar & Paruchuri, 2005). Ego-network measures are informative when the giant component encompasses most of the nodes in a network. However, as the number of nodes located in isolated components grows, the network becomes less integrated (more fragmented), and network measures that focus solely on the structural features of the giant component therefore lose appeal because they ignore the topography of the peripheral components. In our sample, roughly 50% of the patents are generated by inventors in

peripheral components, so focusing solely on the giant component would not capture the richness of our networks. This is a common problem, as recent empirical studies indicate a high prevalence of isolated subgroups or individuals in many large, social networks (e.g., Fleming et al., 2007; Gulati et al., 2012; Kogut et al., 2007). We therefore employ two increasingly popular measures of whole-network integration: relative giant component size and normalized entropy (Chang et al., 2023).

Relative giant component size (Giant) is defined as the number of nodes integrated in the largest component of a network relative to the maximum number that could be integrated (Argyres et al., 2020; Kogut et al., 2007). A large relative giant component indicates that most inventors are connected to each other, at least indirectly. Thus:

 $$ Giant_{kt}=\frac{Number of Inventors in the Largest Component_{kt}}{Number of Inventors in Firm_{kt}} $$ 

for firm k in year t. The numerator and denominator are based on all patent applications that firm k submits in year t. Giant is a less comprehensive measure of network structure for our empirical context because it does not capture the structure of the periphery components. Nonetheless, we include it in order to contextualize our paper within the aforementioned series of studies that have used this measure.

Normalized entropy is a more comprehensive measure of integration because in addition to reflecting the relative size of the giant component, it incorporates information about the peripheral subnetworks (Amburgey et al., 2008). Normalized entropy captures the degree of equality in the size of a network's components (both giant and peripheral), and is conceptually similar to other concentration and diversity measures, such as the Herfindahl–Hirschman index and the Shannon Diversity measure. Networks with high normalized entropy have components that are of relatively equal size, whereas lower entropy values indicate variation in size of components. Thus, a high entropy value indicates that the components are all relatively small and thus have a low "critical mass" of inventor integration within each of the subnetworks for a firm. In contrast, lower entropy values indicate the presence of larger components with higher critical mass of integration.

To calculate normalized entropy, one first calculates the level of entropy in a network, H:

 $$ H=-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\left[(Nc/N)*log(Nc/N)\right] $$ 

where C is the number of components and Nc is the number of nodes in component c. Since the maximum value that H can take is  $ \log C $, we can normalize its value to between 0 and 1, so that normalized entropy equals 1 when all components are exactly the same size. Normalized entropy is therefore calculated for firm k in year t as:

 $$ Entropy=Hnorm=\frac{H}{\log C} $$ 

Note that normalized entropy measures the inverse of network integration. In other words, higher values of  $ H_{norm} $ are associated with lower levels of network integration. Thus, higher levels of Entropy, which reflect lower levels of network integration, are predicted to be negatively associated with the self-citation ratio.

Entropy and Giant capture potential channels for knowledge flow between inventors that have anywhere from one to infinite degrees of separation between them. This reflects the large sizes of the publicly traded firms in our sample, which often employ thousands of inventors and have, on average, 200 completely isolated clusters (subnetworks) in a given year. Thus, even in what would be considered a highly integrated structure (low normalized entropy and large giant component), relevant knowledge may lie anywhere along extensive paths between inventors in the firm who are only indirectly integrated to each other. These paths involve co-authorship links not only between any first-degree pair downstream in the network, but between any such pair that can be traced back to original inventors through intermediate connections. $ ^{8} $ In light of this fact, and given that we exclude forward citations by the focal patent's inventors, the relationships we observe between network structure and the self-citation ratios cannot merely be the result of a mechanical correlation between our dependent and independent variables. #### 3.2.2 | Near decomposability and small worlds

There is no standard empirical measure of near-decomposability; it is what can be called a vague “meta-property” (Gábor & Babarczy, 2022). In this spirit, much prior research has sought to capture decomposability by using tools from the modularity literature (Baldwin & Clark, 2000). For example, Zhou (2013) uses an interdependency matrix that maps the complexity of a firm’s task system, which she then uses to predict organizational structure. Yayavaram and Ahuja (2008), on the other hand, map the structure of firms’ knowledge base through a coupling matrix that identifies the recombinations of patent classes in its portfolio. However, to our knowledge, no systematic study has sought to capture the near decomposability of organizational structure by measuring the patterns of activity of the actual individuals within the firm.

Simon (1962) emphasized that near-decomposable systems were those in which “the interactions among the subsystems are weak, but not negligible,” and emphasized the importance of creating sparse links among discrete and independent subgroups because doing so increases the chances of achieving the potentially dueling goals of exploration and exploitation (e.g., Ahuja et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2010). Thus, near-decomposable systems share features with the so-called “small world networks.” Such networks similarly feature clusters or cliques that are sparsely connected to each other by a small number of links (Uzzi & Spiro, 2005; Watts & Strogatz, 1998). For example, studies have found evidence for the superiority of small world-like knowledge structures for innovation among alliance partners (Schilling & Phelps, 2007), or in simulated organizations (Fang et al., 2010). We thus use small-world metrics as a proxy for near decomposability. Following Watts (1999), we calculate SmallWorld for an intrafirm inventor network using a combination of two measures: the average (characteristic) path length, and the clustering coefficient. Characteristic path length (L) is the average number of ties along the shortest path between any two inventors in a firm. By definition, L is a measure of the global structure of a network, because determining the shortest path length between any two nodes

High values of L indicate that knowledge must pass through a large number of intermediaries to connect inventors in the firm. This measure requires that all nodes in a network be connected to each other by some path, so it describes a property of the giant component within any given network.

Conversely, the clustering coefficient C is a measure of local network structure. The clustering coefficient is computed by calculating the number of existing connections among all k inventors of a focal firm divided by the number of possible ties  $ [k(k-1)/2] $ that could exist among the focal firm's inventors. This fraction is then averaged over all inventors in a firm.

Higher values of C indicate that the firm features many densely interconnected cliques.

The small-worldness of a network is determined by comparing the values of these two parameters for the actual network in question (subscript “A”) with the values of the parameters for a random network with the same number of nodes, n, and ties, k (subscript “R”). For a random network the clustering coefficient can be calculated as k/n, and the characteristic path length can be computed as  $ \ln(n)/ln(k) $ (Watts, 1999). If a network is a small world, it will exhibit a much larger clustering coefficient than the random network ( $ C_A > C_R $), and a characteristic path length approximately equal to that of the random network ( $ L_A \sim L_R $). In other words, a network is small worldly when it is much more highly clustered, containing more cliques than a comparable random network, but the average distance among its nodes is similar to the random network. We used the standard summary statistic to measure the degree to which a firm's inventor network has small world characteristics:

 $$ Small World=(C_{A}/C_{R})/(L_{A}/L_{R}). $$ 

Unlike much prior work, we do not make any arbitrary assumptions about the threshold that SmallWorld must meet in order for a network to qualify as small worldly. As pointed out by Baum et al. (2003), average values of SmallWorld in a population tend to be positively correlated with the average number of nodes in the sampled networks, forcing researchers to approximate the threshold above which the network can be considered small worldly. For the purposes of our study, we are not interested in comparing small-world networks to non-small-networks, but rather in quantifying the relationship between this network characteristic and generative appropriability. Thus, because our theory does not necessitate that we assign a binary type to the networks we study, we use the continuous measure SmallWorld.

We also include a control for the number of network components in our regressions in order to draw inferences across networks of different sizes.

Conversely, the giant component in Panel b is much more fully connected as a single cluster with no structural separation. The giant component in Panel a can thus be interpreted as nearly decomposable, whereas that in B is non-decomposable.

As noted above, SmallWorld is based only on a network's giant component. Since different firms have giant components of different sizes, SmallWorld is likely to have greater impact on firms with a larger giant component. We therefore interact SmallWorld with Giant, and also with Entropy (which is strongly correlated with Giant), and conceive of these interaction terms as more precise measures of near decomposability than the main effect of SmallWorld.

Therefore, in post hoc analyses, we estimate the effect of a patent family's self-citation ratio on various measures of its private value to the firm. The purpose of this analysis is to assess the extent to which SelfRatio does in fact proxy for successful appropriation of returns.

We construct four measures of value. Our first proxy is based on patent renewals. The USPTO charges a renewal fee at certain points during a patent's lifetime. $ ^{9} $ The patent assignee has the option to pay this fee in exchange for continued patent protection, or to forgo paying this fee and allow patent protection to lapse before the patent's maximum possible 20-year life expires. A long line of research has used patent renewals to infer the value of a patent because firms are presumably only willing to pay renewal fees if the patent's value exceeds the value of the fee (Pakes, 1986; Serrano, 2018). Therefore, we calculate  $ YrsRenewed_{jk} $, measured as the average number of years that patents in patent family j were renewed by firm k. This is an ex post realization of invention value.

A second proxy for patent value is the number of countries (“jurisdictions”) in which a firm applies for patent on a given invention. While it is well known that most patents are not commercially valuable, a firm with private knowledge about the potential for a technology will seek to protect it in more international jurisdictions in proportion to its assessment of quality (Wagner & Wakeman, 2016). International filings are costly, given the need to translate and conform to various different patenting regimes in addition to the actual filing fees. Each international patent can easily cost over 20,000. Jurisdictions $ _{jk} $ is a count of the number of countries in which the firm filed applications for a given family. A third alternative measure of patent value is Triadj $ _{k} $, a dummy for whether the family included filings in the three major patent jurisdictions: United States, EPO, and Japan. These are ex ante expectations of invention value.

Our final proxy of patent value is AvgPatentFamilySize $ _{kt} $. As noted above, priority patent families consist of groups of patents within the same jurisdiction (in this case, the United States) that all relate to the same underlying innovation. Often, patent families include a core, or “productive” patent, along with surrounding “defensive” patents (Abrams et al., 2018).

Average patent family size reflects the value of productive patents, because firms are presumably willing to invest in a greater number of defensive patents in order to protect more valuable productive patents. Family size and number of jurisdictions are correlated but not identical, because a firm may file multiple applications within multiple jurisdictions. These are therefore two dimensions indicating the value and exploitation of a seminal idea.

### 3.4 | Control variables

#### 3.4.1 | Patent-level measures

Prior work has found positive associations between inventor network integration and the impact of inventions generated (Argyres et al., 2020). We therefore control for several

characteristics of the underlying innovation that might affect subsequent citation patterns. To control for the breadth of search underlying an innovation, we construct the variable  $ Originality_{jk} $ (Hall et al., 2001), calculated as one minus the Herfindahl index of the primary US patent classes of the patents cited by each patent in patent family j.

Similarly, we measure the breadth of innovation impact by constructing the variable Generality $ _{jk} $, calculated as one minus the Herfindahl index of the primary US patent classes of the patents that cite a patent in family j (Hall et al., 2001). NonPatentReferences $ _{jk} $, a count of the references made by the patent family to non-patent literature, such as academic journal articles (Brusoni et al., 2005) controls for the “basicness” of the research underlying a patent (Trajtenberg et al., 2002). This variable reflects the extent to which a firm is on the technological frontier, which also may affect the propensity of its patents to be cited. Finally, more complex knowledge may be more difficult to imitate; we therefore include PatentComplexity $ _{jk} $, the complexity measure introduced by Fleming and Sorenson (2004). This measure incorporates historical information on the frequency of recombination of each patent subclass, then creates a patent-level measure of interdependence by averaging across all subclasses represented within a patent. $ ^{10} $ We create a patent family-level measure by averaging across all patents in the family.

These patent family-level measures are then normalized within a family’s technology class and within their application year in order to mitigate arbitrary variance due to time trends or technology characteristics.

#### 3.4.2 | Firm-level measures

Firm size may influence the nature of innovation undertaken (Cohen & Klepper, 1996). We therefore include  $ LnSales_{kt} $,  $ LnAssets_{kt} $, and  $ LnEmployees_{kt} $, measured as the natural log of sales, assets, and employees, respectively, to control for size effects. A firm's R&D expenditure is also likely to affect its innovative efforts. We thus include  $ LnR&D_{kt} $, measured as the natural log of R&D expenditure. Other aspects of a firm's research effort may affect its innovative efforts. We include  $ LnPatentCount_{kt} $, which captures the natural log of the number of ultimately successful patent families that firm k applied for in year t. In estimations of network properties, we also control for the size of the network via  $ NumComponents_{kt} $, measured as the number of separate components in firm k's co-invention network. All firm-level measures except for NumComponents are lagged by 1 year. NumComponents is not lagged because the number of components directly impacts the interpretation of Small World, and these occur in a given network at the same time.

#### 3.4.3 | Fixed effects

in our patent family-level regressions, we include firm, technology class, and year fixed effects (firms rarely change primary NAICS, so we do not include industry fixed effects). Technology class and year fixed effect mitigate the concern that industry life cycles or secular trends would drive observed variation.

## 4 | METHODS AND RESULTS

### 4.1 | Network structure and generative appropriability

We first explore the relationship between inventor network structure and generative appropriability by comparing the cumulative forward citations accruing to patent applications of firms with above-median network integration vs. those with below-median network integration. Figure 3 presents this information for our two proxies of integration. Panel a contrasts firms based on Entropy, while Panel b contrasts firms based on Giant. In both panels, the left

hand chart shows the cumulative count of self-citations, that is, citations by the same firm to its own patent family. The right-hand chart shows the cumulative count of citations by all other entities. The charts show citations through the first 120 months after the seminal priority application.

Both panels show similar patterns. Self-citations are markedly higher for patents produced by firms with high levels of inventor-network integration. Self-citations also occur faster for such patents. For the Entropy panel, the set of high-integration applications exceeds 1000 self-citations by month 3, whereas it takes until month 6 for the set of low-integration applications to reach this figure. Conversely, citations by others are lower for patents produced by high-network-integration firms in both panels. This difference does not occur immediately: for the Entropy panel, citations to both above- and below-median applications are of roughly similar magnitudes for the first year, and for the Giant panel, citations to above-median applications slightly outpace those to below-median applications for the first year, before eventually falling steeply behind by the 18-month mark. $ ^{11} $

### 4.2 | Organizational structure and the dynamics of generative appropriability

Hypothesis 2 predicts that greater network integration eventually reduces the firm's generative appropriability. To test this hypothesis, we introduce a time-based interaction term. Specifically, we introduce the variable Months, which counts the number of months that have elapsed since the filing of the original priority application, as well as the interaction terms  $ Entropy \times Months $ and  $ Giant \times Months $. The coefficient on the interaction term provides insight into temporal change in the integration-appropriability relationship.

Table 4 presents the results of the ensuing estimations. Models 1 and 2 present results for Entropy. Model 1 introduces the main effect Months. The point estimate on Months is negative ( $ \beta = -.001 $, p = .000), indicating that the share of self-citations to a focal patent application declines over time. This is consistent with general expectation that a firm's knowledge eventually leaks out to other organizations. Model 2 introduces Entropy  $ \times $ Months. Its coefficient estimate is positive ( $ \beta = .002 $; p = .000), indicating that the salutary effect of integration as measured by Entropy on self-citation ratio declines over time. This is consistent with received wisdom that patented innovations that are closer to cutting-edge science—and therefore presumably cite greater numbers of scientific publications—are likely to be more valuable to firms, all else equal. Second, the number of patent applications submitted by a firm in a given year is negatively associated with these measures of value; this may reflect different firms' patenting strategies.

In summary, we find that the self-citation ratio is indeed associated with private value at the patent-family level. While it may be argued that renewals and international filings are only reflective of a firm's willingness to spend a few thousand dollars, the literature supports the view that firms do not take these decisions lightly—otherwise all patents would be renewed to term and filed internationally.

where Months Until Citation equals the number of months between the filing date of the seminal priority application and the filing date of the citing application, SelfCite is a categorical variable set equal to one if citation j is a self-citation and zero otherwise, and Networkmeasure is once again one of Entropy, Giant, or SmallWorld.  $ \beta_{2} $ reflects the association between network integration and the speed of citation by others, whereas  $ (\beta_{2} + \beta_{3}) $ reflects the relationship between network integration and the speed of self-citation. Tables A4–A6 in the Online Appendix present the results of these estimations for time to first cite, second cite, third cite, fourth cite, and fifth cite. As the tables show, lower Entropy and higher Giant are associated with markedly faster self-citations, consistent with Hypothesis 2. Entropy and Giant are also associated with slower citations by others for the first and second citations, but these effects disappear by the third or fourth citation. SmallWorld, on the other hand, is associated with slower citations until the fifth citation.

#### 4.4.4 | Alternative explanation for H2

It is possible that the reduction in SelfRatio over time does not reflect increased leakage of knowledge, but rather exhaustion within the focal firm of possible follow-on avenues. To explore this, we examine when annual self-citation counts peak for high- versus low-integration firms—put differently, the noncumulative version of Figure 3. For both Giant and Entropy, the peak in self-citation occurs later for high-integration firms than for low-integration firms: year 4 for the former, and year 3 for the latter. The general distribution of citations over time is similar for both types of firms. These patterns are inconsistent with the alternative exhaustion-based explanation.

## 5 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Strategy scholars have produced considerable research on the relationship between inventor networks and innovation, as well as on the strategies firms use to appropriate the fruits of their innovation efforts. Less studied, however, is the relationship between these two important research streams. In this article, we bridge this gap by theorizing that more integrated internal organizational structures increase appropriation through faster cumulative innovation. We find that firms with more integrated and nearly decomposable internal inventor networks are better able to build on their own knowledge relative to rivals' efforts to do so, and that this effect is particularly salient early in the lives of new patents. This finding is consistent with the idea that such structures help by facilitating faster follow-on innovations relative to their rivals. We also find that near decomposability of the core network, combined with more integrated networks, improve generative appropriability in the long-run.

Ahuja et al. (2013) proposed the aspects of generative appropriability: cumulative innovation by the firm, and preclusion of rivals from building on the firm's innovations. Our findings mostly relate to the cumulative innovation aspect, but a few bear on the preclusion aspect. In particular, Figure 3 suggests that integrated networks offer some preclusive benefit, as indicated by the lower rate of citation by other firms of patent applications by highly integrated focal firms. However, our duration models suggest that although greater network integration is associated with slower follow-on innovation by other firms during the very early period following a patent application, this effect dissipates—and, if point estimates are taken at face value, such

integration may be associated with faster follow-on innovation by other firms—by the time a given application has been cited a handful of times. The dynamics of such knowledge leakage hint at an intriguing tradeoff foreshadowed by Ahuja et al. (2013). Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying the extent and durability of integrated networks' preclusion advantage.

While this study does not establish causality in these relationships, we are nonetheless able to rule out the most likely alternative explanations: that the association we find between internal network structure and generative appropriability are driven by the types of patents produced by firms with different intra-organizational network structures, and that the observed relationships are simply a mechanical correlation between integration and self-citation. In addition, we provide large-scale evidence that higher forward self-citation ratios are positively associated with the private value that a firm obtains from a given patent family.

These findings are useful for managers because they suggest that, to the extent they can influence the structure of a firm's internal inventor network, they may be able to improve its innovative performance. Argyres et al. (2020) showed, for example, that centralizing a firm's formal budget R&D authority—a change that is within the power of top managers—is associated with a subsequent increase in the integration of the firm's internal inventor network, and with a corresponding increase in the impact of the firm's innovations. On the other hand, they also showed that networks respond slowly, taking years to change, so it is clear that top managers cannot simply choose the optimal level of internal network integration. In view of this long lag between managerial intervention and network structural changes, as well as the fact that network structures are the result of many small decisions by managers and researchers over time, it is unlikely that they are so strongly endogenous as to bias regression coefficients.

Another open question relates to the interaction between network integration and other appropriation mechanisms studied in the literature, such as employee noncompete agreements, patent enforcement efforts, internal security arrangements, location choices, and so forth. One risk of greater network integration may be knowledge leakage, because more employees become aware of the firm's recent innovations. Do firms featuring greater integration employ more security measures, or use more noncompete agreements, to reduce this risk? Do firms located in highly innovative geographic clusters such as Silicon Valley, where knowledge spillovers are more frequent, feature less network integration? Or are such firms able to use complementary mechanisms to mitigate the risk of leakage enough such that locating in such regions as a highly integrated firm poses little risk relative to the benefits of colocation? For example, do more integrated firms defend their patents more aggressively? There is clearly much to be learned about how firms appropriate returns from their innovation efforts.
