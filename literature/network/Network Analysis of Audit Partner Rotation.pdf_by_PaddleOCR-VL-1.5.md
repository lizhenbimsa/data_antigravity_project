## ABSTRACT

Focusing on mandatory partner rotations, we examine the importance of within-firm network connections to the selection of successor partners and the impact of those connections on post-rotation audit performance. Using data from China, we track partners' history and identify incumbent-successor connections stemming from jointly conducted prior engagements. Although these connections can enhance incumbent-successor information transfers and thus post-rotation audit performance, they may also pose a threat to quality by compromising the successor's independence. Among the pool of replacement candidates, we find that individuals with stronger connections with the incumbent are more likely to be appointed as successors. This finding is more pronounced when the audit engagement is more complex, client-specific knowledge is not readily available to the succeeding partner, and the engagement is more valuable to the audit firm. We also document that successor-incumbent connections are associated with equal or better post-rotation audit quality and fewer client defections. These results suggest that the benefits of network-based successor selection may outweigh its costs. By enriching our understanding of the partner transition process, this study contributes to the public policy discourse on partner rotation.

Keywords: networks, audit partner rotation, information transfer, auditor independence, audit quality, client defections

## 1. Introduction

Designed to enhance auditor independence, compulsory audit partner rotation regulations have been imposed in major audit markets, including Australia, Canada, China, the European Union (EU), Japan, and the United States. However, despite the prevalence of mandatory partner rotation, there remains hardly any evidence on how successor partners are selected and the economic consequences of different rotation strategies. $ ^{1} $ By exploring the network relationships among partners, we provide evidence on the following two research questions related to the successor partner selection under mandatory rotation: First, are partners with previous connections to the outgoing partner more likely to be appointed as the new partner? Second, if so, how does the network-based selection of successor partners affect post-rotation auditor performance?

We analyze these research questions in China’s audit market. On October 8, 2003, the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) and the Ministry of Finance (MOF) jointly instituted a regulation requiring partners who sign the audit report of a listed company in China to be rotated off every five years. $ ^{2} $ Also, China’s auditing standards require that at least two engagement auditors sign the audit report issued on public companies. In this setting, we can determine both partner rotations and the closeness of their connections by tracking the signing history in the audit reports. After prior network research, we specify connections between each pair of partners using their collaborations on prior audit engagements (Hochberg et al. 2007).

Mandatory audit partner rotation induces the discontinuity of client-specific knowledge that the outgoing partner has accumulated on the engagement, entailing nontrivial costs for both auditors and clients (Chi et al. 2009; Daugherty et al. 2012; Bedard and Johnstone 2010). Dodgson et al. (2020) stress that auditors, motivated by economic benefits such as avoiding client defections, and social incentives such as building and maintaining favorable relationships with clients, seek to smooth the transition process. For example, audit firms may use senior relationship partners and conduct annual client satisfaction surveys. $ ^{3} $ Auditors in China's highly competitive market are also eager to foster good relationships with their clients (Wang et al. 2008). To the extent that interpersonal relationships promote efficient information transmission (Cohen et al. 2010; Engelberg et al. 2012), assigning colleagues connected to the outgoing partner as successors can minimize interruption during the rotation process. By improving the transfer of client-specific

knowledge, connections with the outgoing partner enable the successor to hit the ground running and maintain client satisfaction. Such knowledge may also improve the successor partner’s audit judgment and decision making, engendering higher post-rotation audit quality.

However, in the opposite direction, assigning a familiar successor can lead to negative outcomes. Partner rotation is grounded in the importance of protecting independence by bringing a fresh perspective to the engagement. A friendly successor may not closely scrutinize the predecessor's work and is less likely to spot and correct problems in the auditing process. In addition, in narrowing the set of candidates, selecting successors from acquaintances may sacrifice partner attributes such as competence or industry expertise that can improve client satisfaction as well as audit quality. Consequently, the role that network-based rotation plays in shaping auditing performance amounts to an empirical question.

Based on a sample of mandatory audit partner rotations in China from 2003 to 2015, we begin by examining the selection of successors when the engagements are to be rotated. As only partners who are selected as successors can be observed, we identify for each rotation a group of potential replacement candidates to assess the impact of connections on the selection outcome. We refer to these candidates as counterfactuals because they are available for selection but not selected. Tracking partners' teamwork experience, we measure partner familiarity as pairwise connections in terms of the frequency of audit reports cosigned by a pair of partners in the prior five years. Pooling the actual successor and counterfactuals, we estimate the probability of being selected as the actual successor as a function of pairwise connections. The evidence implies that partners closely connected with predecessors are more likely to be selected. Relative to random chance, a one-standard-deviation increase in the number of cooperated prior engagements with the predecessor raises the estimated likelihood for a candidate to become the successor by 35.16%. The results on rotation decisions are robust to altering counterfactual pools, considering different types of rotations, aligning the distributions of partner characteristics, and exploiting exogenous shocks to partner networks arising from partner job changes or mergers between audit firms, suggesting that colleagues sharing more teamwork experience with the outgoing partners are preferred as their successor.

Selecting a connected partner as successor is consistent with auditors focusing intently on smoothing the transition by facilitating information transfer between incoming and outgoing partners. We substantiate this explanation by analyzing whether this tendency varies with the incentives of stakeholders involved in the rotation. First, we find that network-based successor selection is more evident in complex audit engagements, consistent with clients' demand for a smoother transition rising with engagement complexity. Second, we document that the lack of client-specific knowledge for the incoming partners increases the importance of connections with the rotated-off partner to successor selection. Finally, we observe that audit firms tend to rotate the engagement to a connected successor when it generates more fee revenues or its revenue is more stable, evident in long tenure; these incentives subside when the engagement becomes less valuable to the audit firm because of imminent mandatory audit firm rotation (MAFR) or higher audit risk.

We deepen the analysis by exploring the impacts of predecessor-successor ties on audit quality and client satisfaction. Mapping into DeAngelo’s (1981) theory that audit quality stems from auditor competence and independence, the beneficial information-transfer effect may be offset by the negative impact of the lack of fresh-eyes effect on the audit when the incumbent and successor partners are connected. We initially gauge audit quality with partners’ issuance of modified audit opinions (MAOs), which directly captures partners’ decision making and assesses how information from the predecessor partner helps the successor partner to improve audit reporting. The results suggest that the successor partners who have closely worked with the predecessor issue MAOs more frequently, especially among clients that are financially distressed or have accounting irregularities. The evidence points to a mechanism by which more efficient predecessor-successor information transmission improves the informativeness of MAOs in revealing clients’ financial condition or accounting problems. In analyzing other audit outcomes using

discretionary accruals (DAC) and the incidence of accounting irregularities, we find no perceptible impact of partner connectivity on these outcomes. Capturing client satisfaction with the incidence of client defections after rotation, we find that clients are less likely to switch to other audit firms when the engagements are rotated to successors with stronger ties with the incumbent partners. Collectively, these results support the intuition that successors acquiring a better understanding of clients through enhanced communication with the predecessor partners can improve post-rotation audit-reporting quality and client satisfaction without undermining the quality of the audited financial statements.

An emerging literature examines how personal networks among audit partners, client executives, and audit committee members influence audit quality (Hwang and Kim 2012; Bruynseels and Cardinaels 2014; Guan et al. 2016; He et al. 2017). As Bianchi et al. (2020) review, these studies, based on the “convergence” paradigm in social-network analysis, almost unanimously find that cozy interpersonal relations undermine external monitoring and jeopardize audit quality. However, networks in theory can enhance trust and facilitate information sharing between individuals (Granovetter 2005). Fei et al. (1992) stress that by emphasizing adherence to the informal rules within small groups, the social networking culture (or guanxi in Chinese) may run counter to the interests of other groups or society in general. Accordingly, evidence of auditor malfeasance in the prior literature could reflect such a negative externality of small networks to the outside public investors when embedded audit partners protect the interests of their friends. We advance this literature by focusing on within-organization network analysis. In such a setting, we show that by enhancing mutual trust, connections can foster subtle information exchange. Consistent with the notion that high convergence stemming from aligned and coordinated actions can encourage the transfer of expertise (Bianchi et al. 2020), our evidence implies that such benefits can translate into improved post-rotation audit-reporting quality and client satisfaction.

We also contribute to empirical research on audit service production (O'Keefe et al. 1994; Stein et al. 1994; Banker et al. 2003). This research stream depicts the organization of time and human resources according to client complexity, risk, and internal control systems in audit service production. We analyze another important aspect of audit production, namely the collaboration between the predecessor and successor partners under mandatory rotation. By introducing the analysis of within-audit-firm networks into auditing research, we document that such network relations shape the assignment of partners to the rotated engagements as well as audit performance. Through the lens of audit partner selection during the rotation process, our findings broaden our understanding of audit service production.

Our findings also have public policy and practice implications. After the PCAOB (2011) issued its concept release soliciting public comment on MAFR, there were lively debates on the merits of MAFR in the accounting and investment professions. $ ^{4} $ We contribute to this discourse by providing insight on the advantages of a partner rotation regime relative to MAFR. To the extent that internal networks facilitate efficient information sharing among partners, MAFR, which severs the information channel between the incumbent and successor partners, could lead to unintended consequences. Our evidence also echoes Dodgson et al.'s (2020) observation from the field that audit firms take steps to assign and train successor partners to prevent knowledge loss, making the partner transition smoother. It follows that our results are also informative to audit practitioners eager to design optimal rotation policies.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we develop the motivation behind our research questions. In section 3, we examine whether incumbent-successor connections influence rotation partner selection. In section 4, we analyze how selecting a closely tied

partner as the rotation successor affects post-rotation auditing performance. In section 5, we conclude the paper by outlining some implications of our evidence along with its limitations.

## 2. Background and research questions

## Background

Largely modeled after the US regime, the current audit partner rotation regulations in China prevent a signing partner from providing audit services to a public company for more than five consecutive years and prohibits the partner from resuming audit services for this client until a two-year cooling-off period expires. Dodgson et al.'s (2020) evidence from interviewing US audit partners implies that audit firms focus intently on managing partner rotations to minimize disruptions during the process. For instance, senior relationship partners are routinely assigned to the engagement to facilitate the rotation to the new partner and the auditioning of the new partner during the shadowing period ahead of the formal transition date. In the pre-rotation period, auditors solicit client input into the successor selection. Consistent with social exchange theory (Homans 1958; Blau 1964; Cropanzano and Mitchell 2005), these steps are constructive for maintaining client satisfaction. In return, auditors may benefit from client cooperation, assistance, and retention. China's audit market is highly competitive with the 10 largest audit firms only handling about 20% to 30% of public company audits (Wang et al. 2008). Competing for scarce public clients motivates auditors to maintain strong relationships with their clients during the rotation. Reinforcing Dodgson et al.'s (2020) evidence, our discussions with partners in China suggest that they pursue similar rotation management strategies, such as relying on senior relationship partners, in navigating the transition process. $ ^{5} $

#### The selection of successor partners

Given the importance of managing client relationships during the rotation process, we hypothesize that partners who are familiar with the incumbents are more likely to be assigned as successors in order to facilitate the transfer of client-specific information during the transition and to maintain client satisfaction. More broadly, this perspective stems from the role of networks in economic activities as a channel to foster better communication between individuals (Cohen et al. 2008, 2010; Engelberg et al. 2012). Although audits are performed by engagement teams, the partner possesses overarching knowledge about the client, including insights on its business model and strategy, specific transactions and contractual arrangements, internal control system characteristics, and even management integrity (Behn et al. 1997; Bamber and Bamber 2009). This knowledge plays an integral role throughout the audit process, including in the formation of the audit opinion. Consequently, in a major downside, mandatory partner rotation can lead to the loss of client-specific knowledge residing in the rotated-off partner, engendering nontrivial costs for both the client and auditor (Chi et al. 2009; Bedard and Johnstone 2010; Daugherty et al. 2012).

It follows that the auditors will pursue ways to better transfer information to the successor in order to reduce switching costs stemming from partner rotation. According to Hayes et al. (2005), people develop human capital specific to familiar coworkers through repeated collaboration over time. By enhancing mutual understanding, familiarity elicits information flow. Uzzi (1996, 678) stresses that: "social relations make information credible and interpretable, imbuing it with qualities and value beyond what is at hand." As the knowledge structure of the engagement partner is nuanced, tacit, and multidimensional, a more familiar counterparty smooths information transfer (Sanders et al. 2009). Accordingly, embedded ties improve the transmission of tacit and complex information between the predecessor and successor partners. Frequent interactions and a greater

comfort level with familiar colleagues further facilitate communication between connected agents (Granovetter 2005). For instance, the successor partner may be more inclined to ask questions and provide timelier feedback as they will not worry that requesting help from their friends will make them appear less knowledgeable (Vera-Muñoz et al. 2006).

In the other direction, audit firms may avoid choosing partners who are familiar with the incumbents as rotation successors. First, the audit firm may take this opportunity to groom new partners and thus may prefer a capable partner, rather than one connected with the incumbent, to take over the engagement. Second, audit firms may adopt the practice of “shadowing” of the outgoing engagement partner by the incoming partner before the official rotation (Dodgson et al. 2020). By helping the incoming partner acquire client-specific knowledge and develop a relationship with the client, shadowing can reduce the need for network-based successor selection. Finally, the rotated-off partners routinely resume auditing the client after the cooling-off period lapses (Firth et al. 2012b), likely because partners’ compensation hinges on the number and size of the clients in their portfolio (Knechel et al. 2013; Su and Wu 2016; Lennox et al. 2020). Selecting an acquaintance as successor may enable the incumbent partner to recover clients as it is much easier to negotiate with friends over issues that cannot be specified contractually (Uzzi 1996); that is, the presence of a familiar successor facilitates the incumbent recovering the engagement at the next rotation. As the incumbent partner may play a role in shaping successor selection (Dodgson et al. 2020), such self-interest incentives may sacrifice the successor’s competence and expertise. To avoid detrimental effects on the audits, the audit firm may elect to assign partners from outside the incumbent’s network as successors.

Consequently, the impact of incumbent–potential successor familiarity on rotation partner selection distills to an empirical issue, which we address by examining the following research question:

RESEARCH QUESTION 1 (RQ1). Does the familiarity between incumbent and candidate partners affect the appointment of the successors of the rotated audit engagements?

## Selecting a familiar partner and post-rotation auditing performance

Familiarity between the outgoing and incoming partners facilitates the transfer of client-specific knowledge. Given the importance of such knowledge in audit judgment and decision making, close ties should translate into better post-rotation audit quality. In the other direction, Bianchi et al. (2020) note that behavior convergence due to social networks can deter innovation and competition. Importantly, the strategy of preferring partners who are close to the incumbents in rotation successor selection may work against the rationale for mandatory partner rotation. Proponents of mandatory rotation argue that, by bringing a fresh perspective, an incoming partner can identify and correct problems that were missed by the outgoing partner (Lennox et al. 2014). Network-based successor selection may reduce the beneficial fresh-perspective effect on audit quality. In working closely together on the same team, the successor and incumbent partners may share similar auditing technologies and styles (Hu et al. 2021). By fostering favoritism, a close relationship may discourage the successor from challenging the incumbent's work, consistent with recent research on social links (He et al. 2017). Moreover, partner rotations can be viewed as a series of reciprocal exchanges between auditors and their clients, where audit firms may receive both social and economic benefits in return for the relationship management during the rotation process (Dodgson et al. 2020). Social-exchange theory predicts that, while such exchanges can build trust and commitment between the two parties, partners may feel obligated to respond more favorably to the client when resolving disputes over accounting estimates (Dodgson et al. 2020), engendering worse post-rotation audit quality.

A smoother transition process stemming from more efficient incumbent-successor information transmission can also benefit clients by avoiding managers having to spend time reeducating the auditor on accounting policies and business transactions to the extent that this information is

omitted from prior audit files, leading to greater client satisfaction. On the other hand, narrowing the selection pool to acquaintances prevents new talent outside the network from handling the engagement (Uzzi 1996). Prioritizing successor-incumbent familiarity over successor candidates' other attributes such as competence and industry expertise may undermine post-rotation client satisfaction.

These competing forces lead to the following research question about the post-rotation auditing performance:

RESEARCH QUESTION 2 (RQ2). Does incumbent-successor familiarity affect post-rotation audit quality and client satisfaction?

## 3. Analyses of RQ1: Network-based successor selection

##### Data sources

We obtain data for financial statement items, audit opinions, and the identities of audit firms and signing partners from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database. In the event that audit-related data are missing, we rely on the companies' annual reports or IPO prospectuses as well as the online public accountant database compiled by the Chinese Institute of Certified Public Accountants (CICPA) (http://cmispub.cicpa.org.cn/) to minimize missing observations. Our primary sample is based on 24,306 A-share nonfinancial company-year observations in CSMAR for the period of 2003–2015. Although our sample period begins in 2003, the year that mandatory rotation was imposed in China, we track partners' practice history from 1989, the first year that Chinese individual auditors began signing audit reports (for IPO firms), in measuring their experience and pairwise cooperation. We require that the partners' names for both year t and t−1 are available in order to identify signing-partner changes.

##### Research method

The CSRC and MOF (2003) stipulate that (i) partners should be rotated if they have served as a signing partner for the listed company for five consecutive years and (ii) the audits of three-year pre-IPO financial statements are included in the computation of the partner tenure and the signing partner should be rotated at the end of the second year after the client went public. Accordingly, we define partner mandatory rotation as cases in which the partners are rotated off when reaching the five-year threshold. Given that we are interested in whether within-firm networks shape rotation decisions, we do not consider partner changes after audit firm switches as rotations. $ ^{6} $ From our primary sample, we identify a total of 3,014 mandatory rotation company-years, involving 3,257 rotated-off partner engagement years.

Since we can only observe the actual pairs of rotated-off and successor partners, we need counterfactual observations in order to reliably examine how the successors are selected. We construct the sample as follows. For each rotated engagement, we first pair rotated-off partners with the successors, yielding 4,257 actual incumbent-successor pairs. $ ^{7} $ Next, we create counterfactual observations by pairing the rotated-off partner with potential successor candidates. The candidate

pool includes all partners who can serve but are not chosen as a successor when the rotations occur. To be included in this pool, a partner must be (i) within the rotated-off partner’s network formed by collaborations with other partners between t - 4 and t and (ii) active in year t. The rationale and procedures for defining this candidate pool are outlined in the Appendix. Altogether, we have 53,046 counterfactual pairs.

Given its counterfactual nature, we assess the validity of the candidate-pool data for modeling successor selection. In the above definition, we do not use partners' audit-office affiliation because the audit office is not necessarily disclosed in audit reports in China (Gul et al. 2013). As the CICPA database covers current office-affiliation data of the practicing CPAs, we approximate office affiliations in 2015, the final year of our sample period, by the office data collected in 2017. We find that in 11.72% of the engagements, the signing partners are affiliated with different offices. Given that cross-office collaborations in administering audit engagements are not unusual, successor candidates should not be restricted to the partners in the same office. In analyzing the same approximated office data, we find that 69.66% of the candidates belong to the departing partner's office, 43.89% had audit engagements in the same industry as the client of the rotated engagement, and 29.76% are in the same-office and same-industry intersection. This evidence shows that the pool does not include a large number of partners who are remote to the incumbent, implying that they have a slim chance of being selected. On the other hand, as we outline in the Appendix, the candidate pool under the above definition covers 92.11% of actual successors. Among the remaining (7.89%), most (4.28%) sign audit reports for public companies for the first time in their careers. By definition, partners who have yet to form a collaboration network cannot be included in the network-based candidate pool. Accordingly, our candidate pool is sufficiently broad to cover the potential successors.

To examine the role that incumbent-successor connections play in shaping successor partner selection, we estimate this model on the data set that pools actual and counterfactual pairs:

 $$ P(A c t u a l=1)=G[\alpha+\beta T i e+\gamma X+\kappa_{k}\left(\mathbf{o r}\mu_{l}\right)+\lambda_{t}+\varepsilon], $$ 

where G denotes the cumulative logistic function. In this model, the dependent variable, Actual, is an indicator equal to one for the real pairs, and zero for the counterfactual pairs. Tie is our test variable, which measures pairwise familiarity between partners. Specifically, Tie between incumbent partner i and successor partner j in year t is computed as the total number of engagements where i has cooperated with j from t - 5 to t - 1:

 $$ T i e_{i,j,t}=\sum_{T=t-5}^{t-1}n_{T,j}, $$ 

where n is the number of cooperated engagements by a pair of partners, and T indexes time. $ ^{8,9} $

We control for vector X that includes factors that may affect the likelihood of being selected as the successor partner. In rotations where not all of the incumbent partners are rotated off, the remaining partner in the engagement may have a say over the choice of successor. We control for

Remaining Tie, the connections between the successor and the remaining partner. It is measured identically to Tie, except that it is based on the remaining partner rather than the rotated-off partner. In cases where both partners are rotated off, we set Remaining Tie to zero. According to Downar et al. (2021), partners prefer to work with colleagues to whom they share closer affinity. Therefore, we predict a positive coefficient on Remaining Tie. We also control for the following characteristics of the candidates and rotated-off partners. Centrality is the degree centrality (Freeman 1977), equal to the number of connections the partner has built through collaborations during the most recent five years. Reflecting their richer human capital, partners who are central in the networks may have more opportunities to cooperate with others (Bianchi 2018; Bianchi et al. 2019). GENEXP equals the natural logarithm of the total number of years since the partner first signed audit reports for public companies, and INDEXP equals the natural logarithm of the total number of engagements in the industry where the focal client operates that are conducted by the partner. The experience of partners, general or industry specific, may influence partner competence and therefore the need for, and efficiency, of information transfer between the incoming and outgoing partners. We control for partners' Busyness with the natural logarithm of the total number of audit reports signed by the candidate partner in the year before rotation. Given their time constraints, a busy partner is less likely to be selected as a successor, and the busyness of the outgoing partners may increase the need for a more efficient transfer of information about the engagement. At the engagement level, we control for Ln(Pool Size), the natural logarithm of the total number of signing partners in the candidate pool in the rotation year. Rotating a partner with a larger network could thus be less disruptive, reducing the need for finding a connected successor. Finally, we add audit-firm fixed effects ( $ \kappa_{k} $), or rotation-case fixed effects ( $ \mu_{l} $), and switch-year fixed effects ( $ \lambda_{l} $), to control for variation in rotation patterns across audit firms or the engagements and over time. All the ratio variables are winsorized at the top and bottom 0.5 percentiles to mitigate the impact of outliers. Given that we pair each rotated-off partner with a pool of potential successor partners, a partner can appear in multiple selection pools. To account for the serial correlation among the partners, we calculate robust standard errors clustered by each candidate partner-year. $ ^{10} $

Tie between incumbent partner i and successor partner j in year t is computed as the total number of engagements where i has cooperated with j from t - 5 to t - 1:  $ Tie_{i,j,t} = \sum_{T=1}^{t-1} s^{n_{T,j}} $, where n is the number of engagements where a pair of partners have cooperated, and T indexes time. Remaining Tie is measured in the same way as Tie, except that it is based on the remaining partner, rather than the rotated-off partner; in case both partners are rotated off, we set Remaining Tie to zero. Outgoing refers to the rotating-off partner and Candidate is the candidate successor. Centrality is the degree centrality in Freeman (1977), which equals the number of connections with other signing partners the partner has forged through collaboration during the most recent five years. GENEXP equals the natural logarithm of the total number of years since the partner first signed audit reports for public company clients, and INDEX equals the natural logarithm of the total number of engagements in the industry where the client of interest operates, conducted by the partner. Busyness equals the natural logarithm of the number of audit reports signed by the partner in the year before rotation. Pool Size is the total number of signing partners in the candidate pool in the rotation year. Audit Complexity is the principal factor based on firm size (the natural logarithm of total assets), the number of consolidated subsidiaries (in natural logarithm form), the intensity of accounts receivable (its balance divided by total assets), the intensity of inventory (its balance divided by total assets), and an indicator for the existence of foreign subsidiaries in the year prior to rotation. Both Rotated is an indicator for rotations where both succeeding partners are new to the engagement. Past Experience is the mean of the natural logarithm of one plus the number of times the candidate has previously audited the client for the candidate pool of each rotated engagement. Audit Fee is the natural logarithm of the client's audit fees in the year prior to the rotation. Tenure Length is the number of years the audit firm has consecutively served the client until the year before rotation. MAFR is an indicator for rotations with mandatory audit firm rotation forthcoming in the next two years. Client Risk is an indicator for clients that suffer losses or receive MAOs in any of the three years prior to the rotation.

#### Further analyses of the successor selection

We perform several additional analyses to examine whether the evidence supporting the preference for assigning a connected partner as successor holds. First, we relax the functional form assumption about the linear relation between the outcome and control variables in regression analyses by implementing propensity score matching. For the candidate pool of each rotated-off partner, we sort all candidate partners into quintiles by Tie and only keep the observations in

the top or bottom quintile. Observations in the top quintile are denoted by an indicator variable, High Tie. Next, we estimate a logit model predicting the presence of High Tie with covariate set X in model (1). For each candidate with High Tie equal to one, we identify a matched candidate that has High Tie equal to zero and the closest estimated probability to the High Tie candidate from the candidate pool of the same rotated-off partner, without replacement. This procedure creates two groups of observations that differ in High Tie and aligns the distributions of covariates that may influence the successor selection outcome. Reassuringly, in untabulated t-tests, there is no significant difference in any covariate in X between the two groups. As the two groups are well balanced in the covariates, a univariate test of the difference in Actual represents a direct estimator of its impact on the choice of actual successors. Corroborating our evidence in Table 2, the probability of being selected as Actual successor for the High Tie group is 0.100, significantly higher than the probability of 0.018 for the otherwise similar matched group (t = 7.83).

Second, we exploit partners switching audit firms as a shock to the partners' network. If partners switch to a new audit firm, they become less embedded in the partner networks of the new firm and the difference between them and other candidates in Tie can therefore be ascribed to the exogenous change. To identify partner switch cases, we compare the audit firm a partner serves in year t to that in year t - 1. If they did not sign a report in year t - 1, we trace up to three prior years to find the audit firm they served in the recent past. We drop the switch cases caused by the closure or spinoff of the prior audit firm as several partners will switch to the same audit firm in this scenario, resulting in little change in the network of the switching partner. To ensure that we are able to compare the likelihood of being selected as a successor between firm switchers and others for each rotation, we focus on rotations with at least one firm switcher in the candidate pool. We examine two windows: the initial year and the first two years after partners switch firms. In panel A of Table 3, we compare Tie between firm switchers and others for the two windows. The indicator variable Firm Switcher denotes partners who have recently changed audit firms. Compared to other candidates, firm switchers have a significantly lower mean in Tie with the rotated-off partners, supporting that switching firms causes a shock to partners' networks. As the difference in Tie is now caused by exogenous changes, we estimate model (1) after replacing Tie with Firm Switcher. In results reported in panel B, we observe significantly negative coefficients on Firm Switcher for both windows. Based on regression estimates in column (1) (column (3)), a firm switcher is 4.44% (4.00%) less likely to be a successor in the initial year (the first two years) after switching firms. This effect is economically material given that the random chance of being selected as the successor is 4.56% (3.86%) according to the mean of the pool size.

Third, in another form of exogenous shock that disrupts partner networks, we leverage the mergers and acquisitions (M&A) among audit firms. The past two decades have seen several M&A waves in China's audit industry. We identify 538 observations for successor selection that take place in the first year after M&A and drop 132 successors that sign audit reports for the first time in their careers and thus do not have affiliation information before the M&A events. Accordingly, this evidence illustrates how audit teams may preserve the legacy structure and explains the high probability of appointment for prior collaborated partners.

Fifth, an alternative rotation strategy involves the audit firm assigning a successor from outside the incumbent's network to ensure more independence. Finally, we analyze the way in which review partners and engagement partners are rotated off. Based on the rotated-off partner's seniority, we classify rotations into four types: Substitution refers to the cases where the review (engagement) partner leaves and another review (engagement) partner replaces him/her; Promotion refers to the cases where the review partner leaves and the remaining partner becomes the review partner; Demotion refers to the cases where the engagement partner leaves and the remaining partner becomes the engagement partner; and Full Replacement is the case where the review and engagement partners are both rotated off. In Table 4, we report the results of the selection model when we separately evaluate the four cases. Interestingly, Tie loads highly positively except when we isolate Demotion cases. In other words, the connection with the rotated-off engagement partner is irrelevant to the likelihood of being selected if the incoming partner is more senior than the outgoing partner. We conjecture that rotating in a senior partner may smooth the transition process and reduce the demand for information transfer through connections. However, our evidence on this front should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size for the Demotion cases.

Collectively, the evidence in this section provides support for an affirmative answer to RQ1: peers well connected with incumbent partners are more likely to take over the rotated engagements. This tendency is consistent with the notion that successor-incumbent familiarity can smooth the transition process by facilitating the sharing of client-specific knowledge between the partners. To further evaluate the validity of this explanation, we next examine whether the network-based selection pattern varies with the incentives of the parties involved in the rotation.

#### Variation in the preference for connected successors

The demand for a close partner is likely stronger when the audit engagement to be rotated is more complex, making client-specific information harder to transfer. By credibly converting the soft information (e.g., the motive behind the contractual arrangements, the reliability of the internal control system, and customers' creditworthiness) into hard information (e.g., necessary audit procedures and adjustments), close network ties play a pivotal role in efficient information transfer between the departing and incoming partners for such clients. By saving time acquainting incoming partners with their transactions, complex clients also benefit more from a smooth information transfer. Consequently, client complexity can increase the demand for network-based successor selection. We gauge client complexity with the following variables: firm size (the natural logarithm of total assets), the number of consolidated subsidiaries (in its natural logarithm), the intensity of accounts receivable (its balance divided by total assets), the intensity of inventory (its balance divided by total assets), and an indicator for the existence of foreign subsidiaries. ## 4. Analyses of RQ2: Network-based successor selection and auditing performance

Given the differential impacts of enhanced information transfer and potentially weakened success-sor independence or competence on audit quality and client satisfaction, whether the network-based successor selection pattern influences auditing performance, as outlined in RQ2, is an empirical issue. We analyze the data to help resolve this issue empirically.

#### Network-based successor selection and audit quality

To examine whether post-rotation influences audit quality, we treat rotation as staggered events and apply a difference-in-differences (DiD) design. Based on a sample comprising client-year observations, the treatment group, denoted by indicator MR, includes client-years in the first three years after rotation. In some cases, voluntary partner changes can take place during the three post-rotation years, “interrupting” the effect of Tie. Thus, we only view post-rotation years without further changes in successors as treatment observations so that Tie captures the connection between the original pair of outgoing and incoming partners. We specify the model as:

 $$ y=\alpha+\beta_{1}MR+\beta_{2}MR\times Tie_{PS}+\gamma X+\kappa_{k}+\lambda_{t}+\nu_{m}+\varepsilon. $$ 

In this analysis, y is the dependent variable for audit quality and MR is defined above. We measure predecessor-successor connectivity by  $ T_{ieps} $. It is equal to the number of engagements where the predecessor and successor partners co-sign the audit reports in the prior five years. For rotation observations involving more than one predecessor-successor pairs (e.g., both incumbent partners are rotated-off), we use the mean value of the number of such cooperated engagements for all pairs involved. For observations not in the treatment group, we assign a zero value to the  $ T_{ieps} $ variable because such cases have no variation in  $ T_{ieps} $ by construction. $ ^{17} $ The interaction term,  $ MR \times T_{ieps} $, evaluates whether post-rotation audit quality depends on the  $ T_{ieps} $ variable. We follow prior research by including a set of client and auditor characteristics, denoted as vector X, which may influence audit quality, as well as fixed effects for audit firms ( $ \kappa_k $), time ( $ \lambda_t $), and clients ( $ \nu_m $) in the regression. The definitions of these client and auditor characteristics are detailed in Table 6. In the presence of client and year fixed effects, the  $ \beta $ coefficients in the above model can be interpreted in a generalized DiD manner (Bertrand et al. 2004):  $ \beta_1 $ reflects the differential audit quality during the post-rotation period versus other years relative to that of observations not experiencing rotation, and  $ \beta_2 $ further captures how the above differences vary with  $ T_{ieps} $.

Although audit reporting, which is strictly under the auditor's control, represents a direct measure of audit quality that can be more reliably measured, it does not capture the quality of the audited financial statements (DeFond and Zhang 2014). We complement the auditor opinion evidence by analyzing the influence of  $ Tie_{PS} $ on DAC and the incidence of Restatement and Sanction. The results for these measures are reported in columns (3) to (5) of Table 7, where we find that the interaction term  $ MR \times Tie_{PS} $ bears insignificant coefficients. This evidence implies that a strong connection with the predecessor does not impair the quality of financial statements audited by the successor. However, it is important to stress that the financial statements jointly reflect the client's innate characteristics, the manager's reporting quality, and audit quality (DeFond and Zhang 2014).

It is possible that the impact of having a connected successor is more evident when interpersonal ties are stronger. To capture strong-tie rotations, we use the top quartile of  $ Tie_{PS} $ (which is equal to two) as the cut-off and define Strong Tie as an indicator for engagements rotated to such highly connected successors. The results based on Strong Tie are reported in panel B of Table 7. We continue to find that successors with stronger ties with the incumbent issue more MAOs and their audit reporting is more accurate, although they do not perceptibly differ from other successors in DAC, Sanction, or Restatement during the post-rotation period.

Altogether, we find no evidence that incumbent-successor ties undermine audit quality, despite the possibility that they can theoretically weaken successor independence or competence. Instead, we find some evidence suggesting that post-rotation audit reporting improves when the incumbent and successor partners are closely connected.

#### Network-based successor selection and client satisfaction

Next, we examine how incumbent-successor connections shape client satisfaction. Although the extent of client satisfaction is not directly observable, a client disappointed with auditor services is more likely to replace its auditor, motivating our focus on post-rotation auditor switches. Given the right-censored time period to observe auditor switches, this test is based on the following discrete-time proportional hazard model:

 $$ P(Switch=1)=F[\alpha+\beta Tie_{PS}+\gamma X+\lambda_{t}+\mu_{k}+\varepsilon], $$ 

where  $ F(z) = 1 - \exp\{-\exp(z)\} $. We organize the data as follows. For each client with mandatory rotation, the hazard sample starts from the first complete year after the rotation and

ends on the fourth year after the rotation or the year when the firm switches auditor, whichever is earlier. For cases where multiple rotations occur within three years, we only retain the last rotation to avoid complications from new rotations on auditor switching. We also drop cases involving MAFRs, which are naturally driven by regulations. The dependent variable, Switch, equals one if the client switches its audit firm, and zero otherwise.  $ \lambda_t $ and  $ \mu_k $ are time and industry fixed effects, respectively.  $ Tie_{PS} $, capturing the connections between the rotated-off and the successor partners, is defined as before. After prior research (Schwartz and Menon 1985; Williams 1988; Krishnan et al. 1996; Chaney et al. 1997), we include the following client and auditor characteristics in X: Prior MAO (a dummy for the presence of MAOs in the most recent three years), Firm Size (the natural logarithm of client total assets), Growth (the change of sales deflated by sales in last year), Distress (a dummy for the incidence of financial distress $ ^{22} $), Auditor Tenure (the number of years in which the auditor has continuously served the client), Partner Tenure (the number of years in which the rotated-in partner has served as the signing partner since the rotation year), and Big 4 (a dummy for Big 4 audit clients). These characteristics are measured at the end of the prior year, and their descriptive statistics are presented in panel A of Table 8.

The regression estimates are reported in panel B. Evident in the significantly negative coefficients on  $ Tie_{PS} $, a one-standard-deviation increase in this variable reduces the post-rotation probability of an auditor switch by 0.82%, representing a 12.29% decrease relative to the 6.7% sample mean of Switch (panel A of Table 8). This evidence supports the importance of predecessor-successor ties to reducing client defections after mandatory rotations.

#### Partitioning rotation by the roles that partners play

#### Reconciling with related research

Two recent studies also examine collaborations among audit partners. Analyzing how homophily shapes the pairing of engagement partners in audit teams, Downar et al. (2021) report that gender and ethnicity homophily contributes to auditor dyad formation. However, as they highlight, auditors may fail to exercise sufficient levels of professional skepticism by questioning the work performed by homophilious colleagues such that the sharing of the same ethnicity by auditor dyads is associated with lower audit quality. Different from Downar et al. (2021), we focus on workplace ties forged through prior collaborations. More critically, they evaluate whether homophily matters when the auditor dyads work in the same engagement, whereas we examine how partner connections influence auditor performance when the engagement is rotated from one partner to another. Reflecting the role that interpersonal ties play in smoothing the transition process, trust between partners does not undermine audit quality but may engender positive outcomes such as reducing post-rotation client defections. Accordingly, different forms of networks may function differently in different contexts.

Taking previous working experience between partners as given, Zhang et al. (2017) document that such experience reduces post-rotation audit quality. As we show in section 3, choosing a partner with a close working relationship with the incumbent as rotation successor can be a strategic outcome. To mitigate the endogeneity concern, we apply a rigorous DiD design to examine the effects of the successor-incumbent relationship on audit quality. In contrast to Zhang et al.'s (2017) design choice to gauge audit quality strictly with DAC, we comprehensively analyze a spectrum of audit-quality measures and triangulate the analysis by examining post-rotation client defections. Zhang et al. (2017) conclude that working experience with the incumbent can compromise the successor's independence and, in turn, audit quality. As we find no evidence suggesting that successor-incumbent ties impair auditor performance, it is important to exercise caution in interpreting this conclusion. $ ^{23} $

## 5. Conclusions

In this paper, we examine the role that familiarity between audit partners plays in successor selection in the context of mandatory audit partner rotation. To promote efficient information transmission between the partners, audit firms and their clients may prefer successors familiar with the

outgoing partners. However, such a strategy may sacrifice successor independence and competence, engendering worse post-rotation auditing performance. Analyzing Chinese data from 2003 to 2015, we provide empirical evidence on several fronts.

First, capturing familiarity between partners with connections formed from prior jointly conducted audit engagements, we document that connections are a major determinant in the selection process—a candidate partner's probability of being selected as a successor rises by 35.16% with a one-standard-deviation increase in the number of engagements on which they collaborated with the outgoing partner. Second, supporting the information transfer explanation, engagements are more likely to be rotated to well-connected successors when the audit engagements are more complex, client-specific knowledge is not readily available to the succeeding partners, and the engagements are more valuable to the audit firms. Finally, we find that connections between the rotated-off and successor partners are associated with more frequent MAO issuance, more accurate audit opinion reporting, and fewer client defections during the post-rotation period. However, connected successors exhibit no perceptible impact on the quality of audited earnings. These results imply that, in network-based successor selection, the benefits of enhancing knowledge transfer may outweigh the costs of undermining successor independence or competence.

By introducing the concept of networks within accounting firms into auditing research, we demonstrate that workplace ties can shape partner-client matching and audit performance. These findings enrich our understanding of how public accounting firms organize their production of audit services. We also extend the auditor rotation literature, where researchers typically assume that successor partners on rotated audits are exogenously selected when examining the consequences of rotations. Given our evidence that partner networks can influence the rotation decision and attenuate client-specific knowledge loss stemming from rotations, future work in this research stream may consider controlling for incumbent-successor connections or discussing the possible effects of such connections on the results if the data are not available.

It is important to stress that our research is subject to several limitations. First, our data includes public clients only, which may create noise in the familiarity measure by neglecting collaborations involving private clients (Kinney 2015; Lennox and Wu 2018; Aobdia 2019). Second, the unique nature of China's audit market structure and development may limit generalizability to other settings. For instance, audit firms with better internal knowledge accumulation and sharing systems may rely less on the familiarity between partners in the transition process. Third, given that we cannot fully dispel the endogeneity threat stemming from the rotation decision, we urge caution in interpreting the evidence on post-rotation consequences.

Keeping these caveats in mind, our research contributes to the public policy discourse. Specifically, our evidence implies that, by facilitating information transfer, workplace ties between predecessor and successor partners can help the latter better understand clients' auditing environment and maintain or even improve audit performance. From this standpoint, regulators may prefer a partner-rotation regime over the more drastic step of imposing mandatory audit firm rotation. Our evidence is also informative to auditing practitioners. Drawing from the finding that predecessor-successor connections can reduce knowledge loss during the transition, audit firms may consider partners' prior teamwork experience when developing rotation policies.

Our choice of the proper distance is based on the trade-off between pool size and accuracy. Specifically, we compare the percentages of actual successors that are in the networks defined by the various distance thresholds. In defining the networks, we use the collaborations among partners from t - 4 to t rather than t - 5 to t - 1. This approach reflects the most updated information about the accessibility of partners in t, the rotation year. For example, if partner B1 is connected to the rotated-off partner A for the first time in year t, B1 is available for selection when A is rotated off an engagement. Defining the networks by collaborations between t - 5 and t - 1 essentially excludes B1 from the pool, rendering the data inaccurate.
