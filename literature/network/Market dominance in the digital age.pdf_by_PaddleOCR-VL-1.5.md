## Abstract

I document that the network structure of the online economy significantly contributes to rising industry concentration. Firms that are central in the online economy benefit more from increased economies of scale, decreased search costs, and network effects resulting from digitalization. Industries with firms that are more central become more concentrated and central firms have larger increases in market share. These results are driven by firms' ability to generate revenue, as evidenced by central firms earning higher risk-adjusted returns and having more positive earnings surprises. Centrality is also associated with increasing productivity, but profitability only increases for central firms in business-to-consumer industries.

Keywords: concentration; internet; network; digitalization.

JEL classifications: D22, E22, G32, O33, L1, L86.

## 1. Introduction

The US economy has become increasingly concentrated in fewer firms since the 1990s (Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely 2019). At the same time, these firms appear to have increased their market power (De Loecker, Eeckhout, and Unger 2020), creating concerns about the state of competition in the economy (Covarrubias, Gutiérrez, and Philippon 2020). Evidence points to the possibility that information technology plays an important role in this phenomenon. Autor et al. (2020) discuss how technological changes have the potential to give rise to “winner-take-most” markets, where only the most efficient firms are able to survive. Measuring firms’ use of information technology has proven difficult, however, with empirical research primarily focusing on industry-level investment in information technology (Bessen 2020; Kwon, Ma, and Zimmermann 2024). $ ^{1} $

In this article, I investigate how recent technological changes, namely digitalization and the online economy, contribute to rising industry concentration. Prior research has empirically documented that online activity is highly concentrated (Huberman and Adamic 1999). Search engines and web page links create network effects, driving web traffic to the already most popular websites (Barabási and Albert 1999; Cho and Roy 2004). These benefits are further calcified by Internet users' sticky web-browsing habits (Stigler Committee

on Digital Platforms 2019). Increased web traffic allows firms to further benefit from other types of economies of scale (Hsieh and Rossi-Hansberg 2023; Kwon, Ma, and Zimmermann 2024; Lashkari, Bauer, and Boussard 2024), which are particularly strong for digital technology and further contribute to the “winner-take-most” nature of the online economy. Increased web traffic also gives firms access to more consumer data, which is an emerging source of market power (Eeckhout and Veldkamp 2024). All of these channels combine to create strong advantages for dominant firms in the online economy. Motivated by these studies, my analysis proceeds in two parts: first I investigate the relation between the online economy and industry concentration, and second I explore the asset pricing implications.

Given that the Internet is structured as a network of websites, the extent to which a firm benefits from these channels is captured by the firm's centrality in the network. To first illustrate this mechanism, I present a toy model in which consumers on a Hoteling line decide which of two products to purchase in the presence of network effects and search costs. The model demonstrates that increases in network effects and decreases in search costs, such as those resulting from digitalization, benefit central firms via increased market share, resulting in higher levels of market concentration.

Empirically testing this hypothesis, however, requires a way to identify a firm’s position in the online economy. To do so, I use a unique data set to model the online economy as a network of over 200,000 public and private firm websites. Linkages in the network reflect the extent to which website audiences overlap. I then calculate the eigenvector centrality (henceforth centrality) of firms in the network. Centrality measures the flow of web traffic to a firm due to its connections in the network (i.e., the network effect). This flow of web traffic also reflects the search costs associated with finding the firm online. $ ^{2} $ Moreover, centrality enables firms to benefit from aspects of the online economy that foster concentration via economies of scale and the collection of consumer data.

I begin the empirical analysis by documenting strong feedback effects in web traffic and lower search costs for central firms. More-central firms appear higher in search engine results, receive more web traffic, and have higher web-traffic growth rates. A 1 SD increase in centrality is associated with a 4.06 percentage point greater web-traffic growth rate. To investigate this more directly, I examine the shock to web traffic associated with the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) shutdown. I find that the concentration of web traffic increased significantly in March 2020. This result is robust to the inclusion of control variables in a difference-in-difference setting and focusing on firms with a stronger online presence. The evidence therefore supports the hypothesis that central firms benefit from network effects and lower search costs, which results in concentrated online activity.

I next investigate the relation between centrality and the concentration of economic activity. Industries with more-central firms (i.e., industries that rely more on operating in online spaces) become more concentrated in the following year by an additional 5.6 per cent. Central firms also increase their market share by an additional 1.3 percentage points in the following year. These results indicate that centrality is associated with increased economic concentration at both the industry and firm level.

The phenomenon of increasing concentration has sparked a debate on whether it reflects decreasing competition or increasing productivity. Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely (2019) find that firms in industries with the largest increase in concentration over the past two decades are more profitable and have higher profit margins (PMs). The authors argue that this reflects a potential decrease in competition. In contrast, Autor et al. (2020) argue that increases in concentration are the result of highly productive “superstar” firms that benefit from new technology.

In the context of the online economy, central firms likely benefit from information technology that improves productivity via economies of scale. For example, all online visitors can use the same website, rather than firms needing to open stores in multiple locations. At the same time, these benefits may create barriers to entry, allowing central firms to charge higher markups (Stigler Committee on Digital Platforms 2019). I therefore examine changes in profitability, as measured by return-on-assets (ROAs), to investigate these two possibilities in the context of online centrality. Furthermore, the strength of these channels may depend on the type of industry the firm is in. For example, business-to-consumer (B2C) industries may experience stronger economies of scale via the aforementioned website example than business-to-business (B2B) industries. I therefore separately investigate the profitability of central firms in B2C and B2B industries.

I find that central firms in B2C industries become more profitable over the following year, but central firms in B2B industries do not. Furthermore, I decompose ROAs into PM, which is more strongly associated with markups, and asset turnover (AT), which is more strongly associated with productivity. Centrality is not significantly related to changes in PM, but central firms do have larger increases in AT. The evidence therefore suggests that the increase in sales for central firms is a function of increased scale rather than increased markups. While I cannot exclude the possibility that central firms increase their PMs over longer horizons, this result indicates that increased concentration attributable to the online economy is more strongly associated with increases in productivity, consistent with Autor et al. (2020).

To better understand how increased AT results in increased profitability for central firms in B2C industries but not in B2B industries, I examine how centrality is related to changes in a variety of costs. I find that central firms in B2C industries do not increase their costs commensurate with the increase in sales, which suggests strong economies of scale but also nontrivial markups. In contrast, central firms in B2B industries significantly increase their cost of goods sold (COGS) and selling, general, and administrative expenses (SG&A), which account for the lack of changes to profitability. Thus, B2C industries appear to have stronger economies of scale associated with online centrality, but B2B industries appear to charge lower markups for the increase in scale associated with online centrality.

While the results suggest a significant relation between centrality and concentration, it is also possible that both variables are driven by unobserved firm characteristics. To mitigate this concern, I examine the component of centrality that is determined by higher-order connections. $ ^{3} $ I find that results remain significant when considering only the higher-order component of centrality, indicating the relation between centrality and concentration is not fully explained by omitted firm characteristics.

I next turn to investigating the asset pricing implications of centrality. Forming centrality-sorted portfolios, I find that central firms earn higher risk-adjusted returns at approximately 0.90 per cent per month. This difference is primarily a function of the negative alphas earned by peripheral firms. Several robustness tests confirm the relation between centrality and stock returns, including double-sorted portfolios and examining firms with a stronger correlation between sales and web traffic.

I also document that the inclusion of private firms in the network significantly improves the ability of centrality to predict stock returns for public firms. This result highlights an important advantage of the data (i.e., incorporating private firms into the network) and suggests that it is more difficult for market participants to price the impact of private firms.

Given that the network structure of the online economy drives users to central firms, it is likely that centrality is related to future stock returns through providing information about

future cash flows. I therefore examine earnings surprises to assess the source of the return differential. Central firms have significantly more positive earnings surprises and peripheral firms have more negative cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) around their earnings announcements. This result suggests that the difference in returns reflects peripheral firms' difficulty in meeting expected cash flows. It therefore seems that market participants, namely investors and analysts, are aware of the advantages of central firms, but are not fully accounting for the significant negative effects on peripheral firms. This is plausible due to the relative difficulty of sorting through peripheral firms, which are smaller and less visible, whereas central firms are relatively easier to identify ex-ante. The fact that results strengthen when the network includes private firms, which especially suffer from these market frictions, further supports this interpretation. Furthermore, these results demonstrate that the effects of centrality extend beyond the growth of central firms that are already large and productive. Peripheral firms are significantly affected as well—but negatively.

Throughout my analysis, I find that the effects of website centrality are distinct from traditional industrial organization variables. Centrality is not significantly related to economies of scope or product differentiation and centrality does not appear to affect innovation efficiency. The effects of centrality are more driven by complementary, compared to substitutive, product market relations in the network of firm websites, which suggests possible benefits for both producers and consumers. $ ^{4} $ In contrast to concerns of large technology firms making anti-competitive acquisitions (Kamepalli, Rajan, and Zingales 2022), centrality is negatively related to acquisition activity, possibly indicating that online advantages allow central firms to grow without resorting to acquisitions. The fact that private firms substantially contribute to the informativeness of centrality reflects the importance of a holistic assessment of a firm's position in the economy, rather than just relative to product market competitors. These results highlight that the online economy uniquely contributes to increasing concentration in a way that is important for scholars, regulators, and policy-makers to take into account. Lower search costs and stronger network effects interact with economies of scale to create abnormal increases in market share for central firms. These advantages appear to be persistent and are exacerbated by the way the Internet is indexed and accessed (i.e., via powerful search platforms). Further study on the long-term effects that this phenomenon has on competition is an important avenue for future research.

Finally, while the analyses presented in this article help shed light on the role of digitalization in rising industry concentration, there are important limitations that should also be addressed in future research. First, the analysis on higher-order connections partially mitigates concerns of omitted variables, but cannot completely rule them out. Additional sources of exogenous variation in network position could help future research make stronger causal conclusions. Second, the data that enable the measurement of network position in this setting are only available for a four-year period, which restricts the set of tests that can be used due to limited within-firm variation in centrality. Furthermore, this period occurs well after the initial stages of digitalization in the 1990s and 2000s, and so may reflect near-equilibrium quantities. It is therefore important to consider the results within the limitations of the empirical setting. Nonetheless, the results paint a picture of central firms in the digital economy that should be taken into account when considering the role of digitalization in recent macroeconomic trends.

### 1.1 Related literature

This article contributes to the literature on rising industry concentration in the US economy. A number of related macroeconomic trends have also been documented: markups have increased (De Loecker, Eeckhout, and Unger 2020; Pellegrino 2025), labor share of output has declined (Karabarbounis and Neiman 2014; Barkai 2020), firm entry rate has declined (Decker et al., 2016), the relation between investment and profitability has weakened (Covarrubias, Gutiérrez, and Philippon 2020), and patent concentration has increased (Akcigit and Ates 2023). A subset of this literature focuses on the role of information technology in driving these trends. Autor et al. (2020) present a model of “superstar” firms to explain several macroeconomic trends. The authors note that the key mechanism in their model can be represented by “any force that makes the industry more concentrated—more ‘winner take most’—such as an increased importance of network effects or scale-biased technological change from information technology advances.” The model implications are therefore directly applicable to the online economy. Ganapati (2021) and Hsieh and Rossi-Hansberg (2023) document empirical evidence consistent with concentration being driven by increased firm scale and productivity, and present models invoking technological changes to rationalize the observed trends.

Empirical evidence directly measuring information technology has mainly relied on industry-level data. Bessen (2020) investigates the relation between industry-level investment in information technology and industry concentration. Kwon, Ma, and Zimmermann (2024) examine industry concentration over 100 years and find that rising concentration is significantly correlated with industry-level investment in research and development (R&D) and information technology. At the firm level, Lashkari, Bauer, and Boussard (2024) find that falling information technology prices explain roughly half of the increased concentration in France from 1990 to 2007, and Babina et al. (2024) find that investment in artificial intelligence reinforces “winner-take-most” dynamics. This article documents direct firm-level evidence that the online economy significantly contributes to rising industry concentration.

This article also contributes to the literature on the potential welfare implications of rising industry concentration. Several papers argue that rising concentration reflects declining competition (Bessen 2016; Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely 2019; Covarrubias, Gutiérrez, and Philippon 2020; Akcigit and Ates 2023), which could negatively impact welfare. Research focusing on information technology and industry concentration, however, has generally argued that technology has increased productivity, and thus welfare (Autor et al. 2020; Bessen 2020; Babina et al. 2024; Kwon, Ma, and Zimmermann 2024; Lashkari, Bauer, and Boussard 2024). A related strand of the literature has focused on the role of growing intangible capital, of which information technology is one form. Intangibles exhibit both barriers to entry, which weaken competition, and economies of scale, which increase productivity, and so can accommodate both hypotheses (Crouzet and Eberly 2019; Ayyagari, Demirgüç-Kunt, and Maksimovic 2024). This article documents nuanced facts regarding these trends in the context of the online economy. In particular, central firms have greater increases in scale but not markups. Central firms in B2C industries benefit from stronger economies of scale but also charge higher markups compared to central firms in B2B industries. Thus, while the evidence supports an increase in productivity for central firms due to economies of scale, the benefits of those economies of scale do not appear to be fully passed on to consumers.

## 2. Model

The digital economy has stronger network effects and lower search costs compared to the analog economy. Therefore, the transition to a more digitalized economy implies an increase in network effects and a decrease in search costs. To illustrate how this transition

creates larger benefits for more-central firms, I present a simplified version of the model from Cong and Mayer (2023), modified to examine firm centrality.

In the model, Firm A and Firm B compete on a unit Hoteling line for atomistic consumers in the presence of network effects and search costs. Firm A is located on the line at point  $ X_a $ and Firm B is located on the line at point  $ X_b $, such that  $ X_a \leq X_b $. This is the key difference between this model and the model described in Cong and Mayer (2023), where the firms exist at fixed locations on either side of the line. Consumers decide to purchase either Firm A's product or Firm B's product. The value  $ V_i $ of the product sold by Firm i is given by

 $$ V_{i}=Q_{i}+\gamma N_{i}, $$ 

where  $ Q_{i} $ is the quality of the product,  $ \gamma $ is the strength of network effects, and  $ N_{i} $ is the fraction of consumers that purchase the product.

A consumer faces search costs to purchase a product i based on the consumer’s distance from the firm on the line. The strength of these costs for the market at-large is given by  $ \kappa $. I assume that  $ \kappa > \gamma $ as otherwise the network effects dominate the search costs and the market collapses to the “winner-take-all” equilibrium where all consumers purchase products from the same firm. The search costs are thus

 $$ \kappa_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\kappa|X_{a}-x|&for i=A\\\kappa|X_{b}-x|&for i=B,\end{cases} $$ 

where  $ x $ is the location of the consumer,  $ x \in [0, 1] $. When a consumer purchases product  $ i $, they derive utility:

 $$ u(x)=V_{i}-p_{i}-\kappa_{i}(x), $$ 

where  $ p_{i} $ is the price of the product sold by Firm i.

In equilibrium, consumers observe the quality and price set by firms as well as the endogenously determined network effects. The marginal consumer, located at z, is indifferent between the two products, where  $ X_a < z < X_b $, since  $ X_a > z $ or  $ z > X_b $ implies the “winner-take-all” equilibrium. Thus, the fraction of consumers who purchase from Firm A is  $ N_a = z $, and the fraction of consumers who purchase from Firm B is  $ N_b = 1 - z $.

I define the difference in product quality and price as  $ \Delta Q \equiv Q_a - Q_b $ and  $ \Delta p \equiv p_a - p_b $, respectively, and the centrality of Firms A and B as  $ C_a = X_a $ and  $ C_b = 1 - X_b $, respectively. In this setting, centrality measures how close each firm is to the center of the Hoteling line, which is the optimal location to attract as many consumers as possible, and so reflects the strength of each firm's position in the economy. Solving for z gives

 $$ z=\frac{\Delta Q-\Delta p+\kappa(1+C_{a}-C_{b})-\gamma}{2(\kappa-\gamma)}. $$ 

The quantity  $ C_{a}-C_{b} $ reflects the strength of Firm A's position relative to Firm B. If  $ C_{a}>C_{b} $, then Firm A is in a more dominant position and, all else equal, will attract more consumers.

The quantity z is equivalent to the market share of Firm A. Examining the sensitivity of Firm A's market share to the strength of network effects ( $ \gamma $) gives

 $$ \frac{\partial z}{\partial\gamma}=\frac{\Delta Q-\Delta p+\kappa(C_{a}-C_{b})}{2(\kappa-\gamma)^{2}}. $$ 

Notably, this quantity is increasing in the centrality of Firm A ( $ C_{a} $). This means that increased network effects benefit Firm A more (i.e., larger market share) when Firm A is more central. Therefore, increased network effects, such as those resulting from digitalization, serve to further concentrate markets in firms that are more central.

Performing a similar analysis of the sensitivity of Firm A’s market share to the strength of search costs ( $ \kappa $) gives

 $$ \frac{\partial z}{\partial\kappa}=\frac{-\Delta Q+\Delta p-\gamma(C_{a}-C_{b})}{2(\kappa-\gamma)^{2}}. $$ 

This quantity is decreasing in the centrality of Firm A ( $ C_a $). This means that decreased search costs benefit Firm A more (i.e., larger market share) when Firm A is more central. Therefore, as in the case of network effects, the lower search costs resulting from digitalization serve to further concentrate markets in firms that are more central. $ ^{5} $

## 3 Data and variables

The primary data used for this article come from Alexa Internet, Inc. (henceforth Alexa). Alexa is an Amazon.com, Inc. subsidiary that provides commercial web-traffic data and analytics for the vast majority of active websites. They report a wide range of statistics covering web traffic aggregated at the domain level, $ ^{6} $ including the extent to which two website audiences overlap.

Alexa describes their procedure for collecting data to estimate these statistics as such:

“Alexa’s traffic estimates are based on data from our global traffic panel, which is a sample of millions of Internet users using one of many different browser extensions.” They also gather data from “a large number of 3rd party providers, representing a diverse panel of web surfers with a broad mix of interests. In addition, we gather much of our traffic data from direct sources in the form of sites that have chosen to install the Alexa script on their site and certify their metrics. As people in our panel visit websites, we count their visits and pageviews for each site and apply data science to estimate what the total traffic and engagement for each site might be.”

Alexa also uses search engine results to measure website popularity and exposure to key words, that is, the likelihood of a website being one of the first links returned when certain key words are put into a search engine.

The overlap score of website audiences is estimated from the previous 6 months of web-traffic data and defined at the website-pair level. It is calculated as the Jaccard similarity of the two website audiences, that is, the number of people who visit website A and website B divided by the number of people who visit website A or website B:

 $$ \mathrm{O v e r l a p}(A,B)=\frac{\left|A\cap B\right|}{\left|A\cup B\right|}. $$ 

Based on a queried website, Alexa reports overlapping websites with an overlap score between 1 and 100, with 100 being two websites that have identical audiences. I obtain overlap score data for over ten million websites every 6 months from June 2017 through June 2021.

To identify firm websites, I begin with the list of websites reported by public firms in Compustat as of the month in which the overlap data are collected. Some of the websites reported are corporate websites (e.g., aboutmcdonalds.com), while others are conglomerate websites (e.g., Yum! Brands, which owns KFC, Pizza Hut, and Taco Bell, reports its website as yum.com). I therefore expand the list of firm websites using Clearbit, Inc.'s Data Enrichment API, which identifies a firm based on a given website and reports alternative websites associated with that firm. Finally, I also include private firm websites reported in Capital IQ. These steps result in a list of 411,584 unique websites corresponding to 354,083 public and private firms headquartered in the USA, of which 231,561 appear in the overlap data. $ ^{7} $ The average public firm in the sample overlaps with between eleven and twelve other firms, although the median public firm only overlaps with 5 other firms and the most overlapping public firm overlaps 144 other firms. To provide intuition for the audience overlap of firm websites, Figure 1 shows four illustrative examples. The figure plots the top overlapping firm websites for Walmart (a large retailer), Alcoa (an aluminum manufacturer), Google (an information technology company), and Bank of America (a financial company).

I use the pairwise audience overlap scores to model the online economy as a network of firm websites, where linkages in the network reflect the extent to which website audiences overlap. Figure 2 presents a visual representation of the graph for public firms, corresponding to data in June 2017. Given the size of the full network, it is difficult to visually represent the network, so I present a sub-network of only public firm websites with connections to other public firm websites. The sub-network contains 3,850 public firm websites and 21,980 overlap scores. $ ^{8} $ Firms within the same cluster, or group of closely connected websites statistically identified within the network, are similarly colored to identify closely related firms.

### 3.1 Centrality

I calculate the centrality of firms within the network using eigenvector centrality. Network centrality is a commonly used characteristic in the network literature, and is often used in the finance literature concerning networks. $ ^{9} $ There are a number of alternative ways to calculate network centrality, the most common being degree, closeness, betweenness, and Katz centrality. $ ^{10} $ The hallmark of eigenvector centrality is that a firm’s centrality is based on the number and strength of its connections as well as the centrality of the firms it is connected to. This means that a firm can be central in the network even if it has relatively few connections, provided it is connected to other central firms.

Eigenvector centrality is calculated as the principal eigenvector of the network's adjacency matrix (Bonacich 1972). A more complete description of network terminology and the mathematical calculation of centrality is detailed in Supplementary Appendix A. $ ^{11} $

Conceptually, the calculation of eigenvector centrality is equivalent to the following. First, assume a uniform shock to web traffic across websites. Then allow that web traffic to pass from one website to another, or propagate, corresponding to the overlap score of the two websites. One could allow this propagation to happen any specified number of times. For example, degree centrality corresponds to the distribution of web traffic following a single propagation of the shock, thereby reflecting only first-order connections. Eigenvector centrality, however, corresponds to the equilibrium distribution of web traffic after propagating an infinite number of times. A firm's eigenvector centrality therefore measures the flow of web traffic to that firm's website due to the network structure.

In this way, centrality captures the benefits of network effects and reduced search costs, especially because search engines employ a similar analysis to determine search rankings. This means centrality can identify the firms that are best positioned to benefit from increasing concentration due to digitalization. However, the connections that determine centrality are formed via many different economic processes, which differentiates website centrality from traditional industrial organization variables such as product market competition. Centrality within a network of more-directly defined product market competition links would reflect the overall strength of competition faced by the firm (Hoberg and Phillips 2016; Pellegrino 2025). By comparison, website audience overlap can be due to product market competition, but it can also result from firms selling complementary products, or even simply having large audiences due to being a part of the broader online ecosystem (e.g., the overlap among google.com, instagram.com, and amazon.com). Centrality within the online network should therefore not be thought of as directly capturing the extent of competition faced by each firm, but rather as capturing the benefits (i.e., network effects) that result from the structure of the online network. Nonetheless, to compare centrality in the network of firm websites to centrality in a product market competition network, I also construct a network of firms where linkages are the product–market–similarity (PMS) scores developed by Hoberg and Phillips (2016). I then calculate eigenvector centrality for the product market network (PMS Centrality) and include it as a control variable in all analyses.

### 3.2 Characteristics of central firms

To better understand the characteristics of central firms, Table 1 presents summary statistics for public firm centrality calculated from the full network of public and private firms, averaged across the sample period. Centrality is defined between zero and one, with a mean of  $ 0.123 \times 10^{-2} $ and a median of  $ 0.041 \times 10^{-8} $ (first row of Panel A). ### 3.3 Centrality and web traffic

To more rigorously test the conjecture that central firms benefit from feedback effects on-line, I examine the Search Power and Web Traffic of firms in a regression setting. Search Power is an estimate of how competitive a website is for key words on search engines, provided by Alexa for one cross-section in January 2020. Being more competitive for key words means that a website is more likely to appear higher on the list of search results when users search for phrases or key words. $ ^{14} $ Web Traffic is the estimated percent of all Internet users who visited the website, provided daily by Alexa from 2014 through 2021. $ ^{15} $

I run regressions of Search Power and Web Traffic on centrality controlling for a variety of firm characteristics. Web Traffic is measured as a monthly average of daily observations, while the most recent observation of centrality, measured semi-annually, is applied to each month. Control variables including Web Traffic in month m and a measure of website engagement provided by Alexa reflecting how much time visitors spent on the website (Time on Site), as well as proxies for firm size (market capitalization and sales), advertising (advertising expense and a dummy variable for firms that do not report advertising expense), investor attention (returns, volatility, and turnover), and alternative firm centrality (PMS centrality). Each variable and the respective data source are discussed in Appendix A. Firm centrality and accounting variables are winsorized at the 1 and 99 per cent levels, standard errors are clustered by industry, and independent variables are standardized to facilitate interpretation. I also include industry-time fixed effects for regressions involving Web Traffic to control for time-varying differences in the level of web traffic received by different industries. $ ^{16} $

### 3.4 COVID-19

To further investigate these potential feedback effects, I examine web traffic during the COVID-19 shutdown. In early 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic caused much of the analog economy to shut down. This created a surge in web traffic as economic activity moved online. As consumers sought online substitutes for their consumption needs, their web browsing may have been naturally driven to the most central websites. In the context of the example of propagating web traffic discussed in Section 3.1, the COVID-19 shutdown therefore presents a compelling setting in which to examine how web traffic distributes across websites when there is a shock to overall web traffic (i.e., the mechanism by which the digital economy concentrates in the central firms).

I first examine the evolution of web traffic during the COVID-19 shutdown. Figure 3(a,b) reports weekly web traffic for central and non-central firms, respectively, from February 2020 through May 2020. Central firms are defined as the most central decile of firms within each two-digit SIC industry as of December 2019. Web traffic is then cumulated for central and non-central firms each week. Central firms received significant increases in web traffic during March 2020, coinciding with stay-at-home orders issued by states, led by California on March 19th, 2020. Non-central firms also received a slight increase in web traffic during this period; however, it is much smaller in magnitude compared to the traffic received by central firms. Overall, these results indicate that the COVID-19 shutdown coincided with an increase in total web traffic; however, that increase was larger for central firms. $ ^{18} $

Overall, the results in this section are consistent with the structure of the online economy driving users to central firm websites. First, centrality appears to capture the strength of a firm's position online, as evidenced by its strong association with Search Power and Web Traffic. Second, central firms have higher future web-traffic growth rates, indicating web-traffic feedback effects. Third, central firms appear to receive a greater portion of new web traffic, as evidenced by the shock to web traffic associated with the COVID-19 shutdown.

## 4. Centrality and economic concentration

I next investigate whether this concentrated online activity translates to concentrated economic activity. To begin, I examine the relation between centrality and industry

concentration, measured as the HHI for each industry (following Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely 2019):

 $$ HHI_{k,q}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{Sales_{i,q}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}Sales_{j,q}}\right)^{2}, $$ 

where N is the number of firms in the two-digit SIC industry k and  $ Sales_{i,q} $ is the sales for firm i from industry k in quarter q as reported in the Compustat Segments database. Industries with a larger HHI are considered more concentrated, with the squared market share in Equation (4) placing an emphasis on industries with a greater skewness in sales.

I then run quarterly regressions from July 2017 through December 2021 using the following regression specification:

 $$ \Delta H H I_{k,q+\tau}=\beta\ a v g_{k}\left(\ln(C e n t r a l i t y_{i,q})\right)+\lambda\mathbf{X}_{k,q}+\gamma_{q}+\varepsilon_{k,q}, $$ 

where

 $$ \Delta H H I_{i,q+\tau}=H H I_{i,q+\tau}-H H I_{i,q}. $$ 

The main variable of interest,  $ avg_k(\ln(\text{Centrality}_{i,q})) $, is the sales-weighted-average natural logarithm of centrality of firms in industry  $ k $ in quarter  $ q $. I take the logarithm before calculating the average because of the skewness in centrality, and use the sales-weighted average because industry HHI is impacted more by firms with greater sales. $ ^{21} $  $ X_{k,q} $ is a vector of control variables that includes web traffic, market capitalization, book-to-market ratio, sales, advertising expense, intangible share, industry HHI in quarter  $ q $, the number of firms in the industry, the number of mergers and acquisitions by firms in the industry, industry returns, turnover, and the average natural logarithm of PMS centrality of firms in the industry.  $ \gamma_q $ is time fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered by industry and all variables are standardized to facilitate interpretation.

The variable Traffic measures total industry web traffic, which I control for to show that centrality reflects more than just the level of web traffic. Intangible Share measures the industry average of the portion of a firm's capital that is intangible, which Crouzet and Eberly (2019) find has coincided with changes in market share over the past two decades. N (Horiz) M&A is the number of (horizontal) mergers and acquisitions that took place in the acquirer's industry over the past four quarters, controlling for increases in HHI that are the result of M&A activity. The number of public firms, which has declined simultaneously with the increase in industry concentration (Doidge, Karolyi, and Stulz 2017), can also affect HHI, so I control for the number of firms in the industry. Finally, I include industry HHI as of quarter q to control for the possibility that concentration itself leads to more concentration in the future. I also remove industries with less than ten firms and winsorize dependent variables at the 1 per cent and 99 per cent levels. The remaining control variables are described in Appendix A.

### 4.1 Market share and profitability

I next investigate the relation between centrality and economic concentration at the firm level. As the concentrating effects of the online economy provide benefits to central firms, these firms may be able to capture a larger portion of the market. I therefore examine how market share growth relates to centrality. Moreover, Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely (2019) find that firms in more concentrated industries are more profitable on average. To investigate if central firms follow a similar pattern, I examine how profitability growth relates to centrality.

I calculate market share as the ratio of sales to total industry sales, where industries are defined at the two-digit SIC level. Following Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely (2019), I measure profitability with ROAs (net income divided by total assets). I further decompose ROA into PM (net income divided by sales) and AT (sales divided by total assets) to investigate how centrality relates to these two aspects of profitability. Autor et al. (2020) suggest that rising industry concentration may be the result of “scale-biased” technology improving firms’ productivity, which is more likely to be reflected in AT. The authors further argue that such increases in productivity are generally beneficial for consumers. In contrast, the evidence in Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely (2019) suggests that increased concentration is associated with increased PMs and is the result of weakening competition, an effect that would be generally harmful to consumers. Given that concentrated online activity allows central firms to benefit more from economies of scale, it is plausible that central firms are indeed becoming more productive. At the same time, the increased concentration may create barriers to entry, insulating central firms from competition and allowing them to charge increased markups (Stigler Committee on Digital Platforms 2019).

It is possible that centrality was more strongly associated with changes in profitability during those years, and current profitability levels represent a stable equilibrium. However, the results during this sample period suggest a continued increase in economic concentration, and thus it is important to assess whether there are changes in profitability correlated with that change in concentration. Nonetheless, the results characterize the current relation between centrality and profitability, which may change over time.

### 4.2 Income and costs

The results reported in Panels B and C of Table 4 indicate that central firms in both B2C and B2B industries are increasing their scale; however, only those in B2C industries become more profitable. Thus, the implications of increasing concentration may be different for B2C and B2B industries. For instance, it may be that the economies of scale resulting from digital technology are more beneficial for B2C industries, perhaps because the breadth of their customer base is typically larger compared to B2B industries. In this section, I investigate this possibility by examining various measures of income and costs to assess how various types of costs change with the increasing scale of central firms.

I first examine different measures of income (scaled by total assets) to track how the observed increase in scale, as captured by AT, for both types of industries evolves into different results for profitability, as captured by ROAs. Specifically, I examine sales, operating income before depreciation (OIBDP), operating income after depreciation (OIBAP), pre-tax income (PI), income before extraordinary expenses (IB), and net income (NI). Then, I directly examine changes in costs (scaled by total assets) to pinpoint which costs can explain the income results. Specifically, I examine COGS, SG&A, R&D expenditure, depreciation expense (Dep), and interest expense (Int). I also examine the sum of all five costs (Total Costs). The results are reported in Table 5, with Panels A and B examining B2C industries and Panels C and D examining B2B industries. The regressions follow the same structure as those from the previous section.

For B2C industries, in Panel A, the relation between centrality and changes in AT is positive but not statistically significant. However, as each additional category of costs is subtracted (i.e., moving across the columns of the table), the relation becomes statistically significant. It therefore seems that central firms in B2C industries increase their sales without a commensurate increase in costs, and thus the additional revenue ends up as additional income. This conclusion is further supported by the evidence in Panel B, which shows that none of the costs considered significantly change for central firms.

These results are consistent with central firms in B2C industries increasing their scale at little marginal cost, which suggests strong economies of scale. $ ^{26} $ However, if these firms are fully passing on the benefits to consumers, then profitability would not change as sales increase because prices would equal marginal cost. The increase in profitability therefore implies that consumers do not fully benefit from the strong economies of scale. Nonetheless, the increasing concentration can still be beneficial for consumers if central firms charge lower markups than their industry peers. I investigate this possibility in the next section.

The results for B2B industries convey a notably different story. The first column of Panel C replicates the relation between centrality and AT documented in Table 4, which is positive, statistically significant, and slightly larger than the corresponding relation for B2C industries. However, this increase in scale for central firms in B2B industries appears to also result in a commensurate increase in operating costs, as evidenced by the statistically and economically insignificant relation between centrality and changes in operating income. Panel D shows that the increased costs are specifically attributable to COGS and SG&A and result in a significant increase in total costs.

It therefore appears that central firms in B2B industries increase their scale, but the increase in revenue is offset by a commensurate increase in costs. One possible interpretation is that prices are approximately equal to marginal costs for these firms, which implies relatively small markups. Furthermore, comparing the results between B2C and B2B industries, the fact that increased scale creates increased costs for B2B industries but not for B2C industries is consistent with stronger economies of scale in B2C industries. ### 4.3 Additional firm outcomes

In Supplementary Appendix D, I investigate how centrality relates to several additional firm outcomes. I provide a shortened summary here. The annual frequency of the data for these analyses exacerbates the limitations of having a sample period of 4 years, namely limiting the statistical power of the tests. Nonetheless, the results provide a more complete picture of central firms.

With respect to markups and productivity, it is possible that the increased scale of central firms improves welfare because it represents a shift to firms that are more productive or charge lower (but still positive) markups relative to their industry peers. For markups, I use the estimate developed by Pellegrino (2025), who structurally estimates a model incorporating network effects in the product market. Given the importance of network effects for central firms, I investigate the relation between centrality and this estimate of markups to provide further evidence on whether central firms charge relatively higher markups compared to their industry peers. Interestingly, I find weak evidence that central firms in B2C industries charge relatively lower markups. Thus, while the previous section documented that these firms do not fully pass on the benefits of their economies of scale to consumers, this shift in economic activity to central firms could still be welfare-improving. In contrast, I find no significant relation between centrality and markups for B2B industries. This evidence also suggests that increasing concentration in central firms does not contribute to the rise in aggregate markups documented by De Loecker, Eeckhout, and Unger (2020) via a redistribution of market share to highly productive firms that charge higher markups, as argued by Autor et al. (2020).

To investigate the relation between centrality and productivity in the cross section, I use the firm-level measure of total factor productivity developed by Imrohoroglu and Tuzel (2014). While this measure allows the investigation of cross-sectional differences in productivity, it is only available through 2020, and thus statistical power is particularly limited. I find that central firms do not significantly differ in their productivity compared to their industry peers in either type of industry. Taken at face value, these results suggest that there are no welfare benefits via increased aggregate productivity resulting from the redistribution of market share to central firms. However, given the limitations of the sample period, these results should be viewed as suggestive.

I also investigate the relation between centrality and changes in scope (Hoberg and Phillips 2025) and product market similarity (Hoberg and Phillips 2016). These relations are both economically and statistically insignificant during the sample period. This fact demonstrates that website centrality is distinct from traditional industrial organization concepts like economies of scope or product differentiation. I then investigate innovation efficiency by examining the number and value of patents granted, scaled by R&D expenditure (Kogan et al. 2017). Again, neither variable appears significantly related to centrality. Finally, I investigate changes in central-firm acquisition activity. There are recent concerns that the acquisition activity of dominant firms is potentially anticompetitive (Cunningham,

Ederer, and Ma 2021; Kamepalli, Rajan, and Zingales 2022). It is also possible that central firms substitute acquisitions for innovation activity (Phillips and Zhdanov 2013). However, I find that centrality is, if anything, negatively related to acquisition activity. One possible interpretation is that digital feedback effects allow central firms to grow without resorting to traditional means like acquisitions. If this is the case, it further demonstrates the uniqueness of website centrality compared to other industrial organization quantities.

### 4.4 Within-industry centrality

Another unique aspect of website centrality is that it can reflect both complementary and substitutable product market relations. $ ^{27} $ In this section, I investigate which of these types of connections in the network more strongly contributes to the information contained in website centrality.

To distinguish between substitutable and complementary product market relations, I use varying levels of firms' SIC codes. For example, the website audience for Delta Airlines (SIC 4512) most significantly overlaps with that of American Airlines (SIC 4512), United Airlines (SIC 4512), and Southwest Airlines (SIC 4512). These firms are direct competitors of Delta, as reflected in their shared SIC codes. However, Delta's website audience also overlaps with travel services like Expedia (SIC 4700) and Orbitz (SIC 4700), financial services like Chase (SIC 6020) and Bank of America (SIC 6020), and other local services like Yelp (SIC 7370) and OpenTable (SIC 7370). These firms provide complementary services for Delta customers and span a broad range of SIC codes.

To assess the information contained in these two types of connections, I create sub-networks of firm websites corresponding to increasingly granular SIC industries and measure centrality within these sub-networks. Specifically, I create sub-networks of all two-, three-, and four-digit SIC industries. A firm's centrality within increasingly granular sub-networks reflects its centrality relative to its increasingly direct competitors (Hoberg and Phillips 2010). I then use these various levels of centrality, including centrality in the complete network, to predict future changes in market share as in Column (1) of Table 4. To control for differences in the normalization of centrality across industry sub-networks, industry-time fixed effects are included with industries measured at the four-digit SIC level. If connections among direct competitors drive the relation between centrality and changes in market share, then centrality within more-granular industry sub-networks should subsume centrality in the complete network.

The results of this analysis are reported in Table 6. Each column introduces centrality within more narrowly defined industry sub-networks, standardized to mean zero and unit standard deviation, to facilitate interpretation of the relative strength of each level of centrality. In untabulated results, I find that each level of centrality positively and significantly predicts future changes in market share when the other measures of website centrality are excluded. When included together, however, I find that centrality in the complete network has the strongest correlation with future changes in market share. Centrality within two- and four-digit SIC industries also holds some predictive power, although the coefficients are not statistically significant when included together.

Overall, it appears that both complementary and substitutable connections contribute to the informativeness of website centrality. However, to the extent that controlling for centrality within increasingly granular industry sub-networks isolates the component of centrality in the complete network that is attributable to complementary connections, the evidence suggests that most of the information contained in centrality comes from complementary connections. ### 4.5 Centrality from higher-order connections

One concern with identifying the relation between centrality and concentration is that unobservable firm characteristics may be driving both variables. For example, there are many dimensions along which central firms may already be large and productive, which could be contributing to increased concentration. This issue complicates the identification of concentration effects more broadly, and highlights a significant advantage of the network approach taken in this article.

To mitigate this concern, I exploit the fact that eigenvector centrality is a function of both the direct connections of a firm and the centrality of other firms in the network. A firm's individual characteristics have a significant impact on its direct connections, but this impact quickly decreases when considering increasingly higher-order connections. The component of eigenvector centrality derived from higher-order connections is therefore less subject to concerns of being driven by unobservable firm characteristics.

This strategy follows those developed by the network econometrics literature to identify peer effects in networks. $ ^{28} $ In the absence of correlated effects, intransitivity in the network (i.e., the existence of peers-of-peers that are not direct peers) can be used to identify the endogenous effect of peers. The largest threat to identification is thus the existence of correlated effects, which result from network connections being endogenous. In the context of audience overlap, it is unlikely that network connections can be significantly influenced by firms. Audience overlap is determined by flows of web traffic, and like sales, web traffic is an equilibrium outcome in which firms compete to get as much as possible relative to the associated costs. The biggest threat to identification is therefore correlated characteristics that determine peer-of-peer connections. This is perhaps less of a concern with regard to audience overlap, given that overlap reflects similarity. If a peer-of-peer has characteristics that are significantly correlated with the focal firm, then the peer-of-peer would likely be a direct peer. However, given that similarity also accounts for website audience size, it is possible that these two firms have other correlated characteristics but are significantly different in size such that overlap is negligible. This concern is perhaps less severe given that the potentially omitted variable of most concern correlates with some dimension of firm size, but these results should nonetheless be considered with the appropriate conditions associated with being unable to precisely control for correlated effects.

To measure the higher-order component of eigenvector centrality, I first measure degree centrality in the network. As discussed in Section 3.1, degree centrality is equivalent to the distribution of web traffic after allowing a uniform web-traffic shock to propagate through first-order connections in the network. By contrast, eigenvector centrality allows the shock to propagate an infinite number of times. In this way, degree centrality reflects the component of eigenvector centrality corresponding to first-order connections. I therefore subtract the natural logarithm of degree centrality from the natural logarithm of eigenvector centrality. The remaining component of eigenvector centrality therefore reflects traffic that is driven to the website due to higher-order connections. An extended discussion of the calculation of degree and eigenvector centrality is in Supplementary Appendix A.

Using the higher-order component of eigenvector centrality, I rerun the analyses of concentration at the industry and firm levels. These results are reported in Table 7, with the structure of Panel A corresponding to the structure of Table 3 and the structure of Panel B corresponding to the structure of Table 4. Panel A reports a significant correlation between average industry centrality and changes in industry concentration when considering only the component of eigenvector centrality that is determined by higher-order connections. $ ^{29} $ This result demonstrates that a firm’s position in the network is significantly associated with increases in concentration over the following several quarters.

Panel B reports the relation between the higher-order component of eigenvector centrality and market share and profitability. Once again, I find similar results with this alternative measure of centrality, including when decomposing profitability into PM and AT. Therefore, it does not appear that the results from the previous sections are entirely driven by omitted firm characteristics.

To further demonstrate that the variation remaining in eigenvector centrality after subtracting degree centrality is driven by higher-order connections, I replace degree centrality with various measures of Katz centrality (Katz 1953). In concept, Katz centrality is calculated in a manner similar to eigenvector centrality, but each successive propagation is weighted by a given parameter. When this parameter equals zero, Katz centrality is equivalent to degree centrality, that is, zero weight is given to the successive propagations. When this parameter equals one, Katz centrality is equivalent to eigenvector centrality. Further discussion of the calculation of Katz centrality is provided in Supplementary Appendix A. I calculate four measures of Katz centrality using parameters equal to 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8. These measures therefore place increasing weight on higher-order connections. I then separately subtract the natural logarithm of each of these measures of Katz centrality from the natural logarithm of eigenvector centrality. The remaining component of eigenvector centrality is therefore derived from increasingly higher-order connections.

I rerun the analyses of concentration at the industry and firm levels using these increasingly higher-order components of eigenvector centrality. The results are reported in

Table 8. For brevity, results are presented for changes over the following year (i.e., four quarters). $ ^{30} $ In both panels, the results are remarkably similar when subtracting Katz versus degree centrality from eigenvector centrality. Moreover, increasing the weighting parameter for Katz centrality from 0.2 to 0.8 does not change the relation between centrality and concentration. This result demonstrates that the results in Table 7 are attributable to centrality derived from higher-order connections (i.e., firms with which the focal firm has no audience overlap).

Overall, the evidence in this section documents a strong correlation between centrality and rising industry concentration. Central industries become more concentrated and central firms gain additional market share over the sample period. This increase in scale results in increased profitability for central firms in B2C industries but not in B2B industries, which suggests stronger economies of scale for B2C industries but also higher markups. The implications for consumer welfare are therefore mixed, although centrality in B2C industries is not generally associated with increased markups either across or within firms. Network analysis identifying variation in centrality coming from higher-order connections shows that these results are significantly driven by the firm's network position, as opposed to omitted firm characteristics.

## 5. Market outcomes

I next investigate the asset pricing implications of website centrality. Network effects and search costs result in benefits for central firms, and barriers for peripheral firms, that have significant implications for sales. Centrality may therefore contain information about future cash flows that are relevant for stock prices. Thus, I examine the relations between centrality and future stock returns and earnings surprises. Moreover, if investors and analysts are already accounting for the size and productivity of central firms, then these quantities can further mitigate concerns that the effects of centrality are attributable to omitted variables.

A series of robustness tests, discussed in Supplementary Appendix E, confirm the relation between centrality and stock returns. First, centrality is a significant determinant of returns in Fama and MacBeth (1973) regressions controlling for other common determinants of returns and adjusting standard errors following Newey and West (1987). Second, the spread portfolio returns are significant when excluding firms with a low correlation between web traffic and sales, which suggests that the results depend on the relation between web traffic and economic activity. Third, double-sorted portfolios, sorting first on market capitalization, sales, or market share, demonstrate the difference between central and peripheral firms is particularly significant for “small” firms, which is consistent with the difficulties of assessing centrality for these firms.

Finally, the data and network used in this article provide important advantages when considering a firm's position in the economy. One such advantage is the inclusion of private firms. This is especially beneficial when considering a statistic that is measured relative to the aggregate, such as centrality, or relative to a particular group, such as market share. Centrality measured in a network with missing firms (e.g., a network of only public firms) could potentially be biased, or at least omitting valuable information.

To demonstrate this, I create a network of public-firm websites using data on the audience overlap between public firms. I then measure centrality within the public-firm network and run a similar analysis on returns. The results are reported in Panel D of Table 9. The spread-portfolio returns are noticeably weaker than those reported in Panel A, both economically and statistically, ranging from 0.31 per cent to 0.66 per cent compared to 0.65 per cent to 1.11 per cent, respectively. The inclusion of private firms in the network therefore improves the informativeness of centrality about public-firm returns, which indicates that investors are particularly inefficient at pricing the impact of private firms. This underscores the importance of a holistic view of the economy that takes into account all firms competing for market share.

### 5.2 Earnings

The stock return evidence suggests that centrality provides information about either future revenue or investors' discount rates. Given the evidence presented thus far connecting centrality to economic activity, it seems more plausible that the stock return results are a function of revenue. If this is the case, the difference between central and peripheral firms, and the relatively larger surprise for peripheral firms, should be reflected in firms' earnings.

To investigate this, I examine the standardized unexpected earnings (SUEs) for firms based on their centrality. Data on SUE scores are taken directly from the Institutional Brokers Estimate System (IBES) database. IBES calculates SUE scores as the actual earnings minus the average earnings forecast, divided by the standard deviation of earnings surprises among analysts. At the end of each March, June, September, and December, I sort firms into quintiles based on centrality. I then calculate the value-weighted SUE score of firms in each quintile. I also measure the three-day CAR (3-CAR), controlling for the CAPM or three-factor FF model, around the earnings announcement, following La Porta et al. (1997). This value reflects the stock price reaction to firms announcing their earnings, controlling for common risk factors. To create a portfolio-level measure, I value-weight the CARs within centrality-sorted portfolios.

The average SUE scores and earnings announcement CARs for centrality-sorted portfolios are reported in Panel A of Table 10. The sample period is from July 2017 through December 2021. As seen in Column (1), central firms have more positive earnings surprises. Columns (2) and (3) demonstrate that peripheral firms also have significantly negative announcement CARs, indicating that peripheral firms underperform investor expectations. In general, these results offer an explanation for why central firms earn higher stock returns, namely that a firm's position in the network provides information about its ability to generate revenue.

## 6. Conclusion

This article documents how the online economy contributes to rising industry concentration. Reduced search costs and the network structure of the Internet create network effects that are further calcified by platforms locking in customer attention through habit-based web-browsing patterns. These scale-based advantages combine with economies of scale inherent in digital technology to create abnormal increases in market share for firms that are central in the online economy.

I use a unique data set to model the online economy as a network of over 200,000 public and private firm websites, where linkages in the network represent overlapping website audiences. I then measure firm centrality in the network, which represents how web traffic flows to firm websites based on the network structure.

Central firms appear higher in search engine results, receive more web traffic, and have higher web-traffic growth rates, consistent with the network driving traffic to central firms. Industries with more-central firms become more concentrated and central firms have larger increases in market share over the following year. Furthermore, centrality is more strongly associated with changes in AT than changes in PMs. Central firms in B2C industries benefit from stronger economies of scale but charge higher markups than central firms in B2B industries. This result is consistent with increases in scale, and thus productivity, rather than decreases in competition, being the driver of increased concentration resulting from the online economy. Importantly, these effects are distinct from traditional industrial organization variables, such as product differentiation or economies of scope, and I examine the component of centrality that is determined by higher-order connections (i.e., firms with a negligible audience overlap) to mitigate concerns that these results are driven by omitted firm characteristics.

Centrality also predicts future returns and earnings, suggesting it contains information about future cash flows that is not already accounted for by investors and analysts. Additionally, the inclusion of private firms in the network substantially improves this predictive power, highlighting an important advantage of the data used to measure website centrality.

Overall, the results indicate that the network structure of the online economy significantly contributes to rising industry concentration. The advantages created for central firms appear to be long lasting and highlight the power of large search platforms to entrench other firms in the economy. These results are important for scholars, regulators, and politicians as the conclusions expand the debate on rising market power beyond traditional industrial organization arguments. Finally, while the negative competitive effects appear marginal in the short-run, studying the long-run competitive effects of online market dominance is an important avenue for future research.
