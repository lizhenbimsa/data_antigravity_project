# ARE THE FUTURES COMPUTABLE? KNIGHTIAN UNCERTAINTY & ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

## ABSTRACT

The growing sophistication of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in entrepreneurship is transforming how new ventures identify, gather, analyze, and utilize information from their internal and external operating environments to automate critical choices, decisions, and tasks. For many startups and corporate ventures, prior research suggests that AI provides important task performance advantages to entrepreneurs in addressing the problem of uncertainty, in part, through enhanced predictive capabilities. What is less clear, however, is whether AI tools enable entrepreneurs to address the problems of Knightian uncertainty – a fundamental type of uncertainty that manifests in entrepreneurship through a set of four inter-related problems: actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion. Building on these arguments, our central premise in this study asserts that the predictive capabilities and task performance advantages of AI in entrepreneurship are contingent upon the ability of these systems to grapple with the problems of Knightian uncertainty. We investigate the logic of this approach through an in-depth analysis of the limits of foundational and emerging types of AI to address these problems, identifying fundamental areas of computational irreducibility inherent in each. In doing so, we offer novel theorization to explain why and how the problems of Knightian uncertainty generate critical boundary conditions governing the use of AI in entrepreneurship.

Keywords: Entrepreneurship; Entrepreneurial Action; Knightian Uncertainty; Artificial Intelligence

## INTRODUCTION

In the field of entrepreneurship, artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming both theory and practice (Chalmers et al., 2020; Lévesque, Obschonka, Nambisan, 2022; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2019; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019), in large part, by altering the “calculus of the actor-environment nexus, particularly as it pertains to entrepreneurial action under conditions of uncertainty” (Townsend & Hunt, 2019: 2). New firms must grapple with a wide array of uncertainties during the business venturing process (Foss & Klein, 2012; Klein, 2008, McMullen, 2015; McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Packard, Clark, & Klein, 2017; Sarasvathy, 2001; Townsend, Hunt, McMullen, & Sarasvathy, 2018). The implementation and use of AI in new ventures offer the potential to provide a novel, functionally superior means to address these

uncertainties since “…the new wave of artificial intelligence (brings about) a critical component of intelligence—prediction…(and) better prediction reduces uncertainty” (Agrawal et al., 2018: 2; Agrawal, Gans, & Goldfarb, 2019b). However, other scholars argue that AI systems are fundamentally limited in their ability to address the problem of uncertainty (e.g., LeCun et al., 2006; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Townsend & Hunt, 2019;). For example, Gigerenzer (2022: p. X) asserts that “when the environment is stable, AI can surpass humans. If the future is like the past, large amounts of data are useful. However, if surprises happen, big data – which is always data from the past—may mislead us about the future.” The critical issue at the heart of this debate involves questions as to whether the use of AI in entrepreneurship is useful to entrepreneurs in their efforts to address the problems of Knightian uncertainty (Knight, 1921; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Townsend & Hunt, 2019).

In the field of entrepreneurship, Knightian uncertainty is endemic to the business venturing process (Knight, 1921). It manifests as an entangled set of knowledge and action problems that occur when entrepreneurs do not know whether something will be possible or impossible in the future and, thus, cannot know or predict future states of a decision environment (Arikan, Arikan, & Koparan, 2020; Foss & Klein, 2012; McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Sarasvathy, 2001; Townsend et al., 2018; Venkataraman, 1997). Knightian uncertainty encompasses four interrelated problems, representing distinct obstacles entrepreneurs face in predicting the future: actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion (Knight, 1921). The combined impact of these problems creates a cascading set of challenges that entrepreneurs must address when pursuing new possibilities through entrepreneurial action (Ramoglou, 2021; Ramoglou & McMullen, 2023; Townsend & Hunt, 2019).

Building on this approach, the central premise motivating our study asserts that the problems of Knightian uncertainty create fundamental boundary conditions governing the predictive capabilities and task performance advantages of AI in entrepreneurship. Our analysis of Knightian uncertainty’s contingent role in shaping the performance advantages of AI contributes to entrepreneurship and organization theory in three critical respects. First, we build novel theory to explain how and why the foundational problems of Knightian uncertainty play a central role in determining the fundamental limits of AI’s predictive capabilities in entrepreneurship (e.g., Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Townsend & Hunt, 2019; Townsend et al., 2018). Given the number of areas in which AI systems have equaled or even surpassed human-level task performance (e.g., Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, & Rock, 2023), theorizing the boundary conditions its predictive capabilities in entrepreneurship may seem to be premature. However, a fundamental implication of our theorizing contends that even if AI systems eventually develop capabilities that are superior to humans across all relevant tasks – ostensibly through the emergence of artificial general intelligence (AGI, see Bubeck et al., 2023) – important dimensions of business venturing will remain computationally irreducible (cf. Wolfram, 2008) since even intelligent machines are subject to the problems of Knightian uncertainty. $ ^{1} $

Second, our study builds on extant research to provide much-needed construct clarity (i.e., Suddaby, 2010) regarding the nature and impact of Knightian uncertainty in entrepreneurship (Townsend et al., 2018), especially with the emergence of AI (Townsend & Hunt, 2019). Prior studies have tended to define Knightian uncertainty as being impervious to prediction without

making it clear whether this purported unpredictability arises due to an entrepreneur's inherent lack of information, capabilities, or tools to develop accurate predictions. If so, then AI has the potential to solve the problem of Knightian uncertainty through functionally superior technological capabilities (Agrawal et al., 2018), thereby creating a serious disconnect between the prevailing notions of Knightian uncertainty and the observable realities of AI-assisted entrepreneurial action (Townsend & Hunt, 2019). Our study addresses these problems head-on through an in-depth analysis of the cascading set of problems that define the nature and substance of Knightian uncertainty (Townsend, et al., 2018). Through this, we explain how and why some decision environments are inherently unpredictable, even in the age of intelligent machines.

Third, our study contributes to both entrepreneurship and organizational research by identifying and cataloguing the benefits and limitations of AI in entrepreneurship and organizational research. As computational theorists debate the merits of developing fully autonomous machines that operate without human oversight or control (Berente, Gu, Recker, Santhanam, 2021; LeCun, 2022), such pursuits illuminate a whole host of important technical, ethical, and organizational problems for future entrepreneurship and organizational research (Jia, Luo, Fang, & Liao, 2023). Building upon our novel theorization, we explore the critical implications of these topics by posing fundamental questions about the possibilities (or impossibilities) of machine agency and agentic AI (van Rijmenam & Logue, 2021) and the role of autonomous AI systems in new and established organizations.

In the next section, we review and critique existing literature concerning both the foundational and emerging types of AI in entrepreneurship to lay the groundwork for a reconceptualization of AI's roles, impacts, and limitations when addressing entrepreneurial action in the context of uncertain decision environments.

##### ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE, TASK PERFORMANCE & ENTREPRENEURSHIP

The explosive growth of investment in and implementation of computationally complex systems of artificial intelligence (AI) heralds the emergence of a new era of machine intelligence in entrepreneurship (Chalmers et al., 2020; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). In fundamental ways, the transformative potential of “...creat(ing) and understand(ing) intelligence as a general property of systems, rather than as a specific attribute of humans” (Russell, 1997: 57; 2016) promises to aid the efforts of entrepreneurs to tackle key problems in the business venturing process (Townsend & Hunt, 2019) in ways that will fundamentally transform the relationship between humans and technology (Murray et al., 2021).

Yet, despite the transformative possibilities of deploying AI in both established and new ventures, entrepreneurship scholars are increasingly grappling with questions regarding the effective use and role of AI in entrepreneurship (Chalmers et al., 2020; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). While this emerging stream of scholarly research has done much to identify and describe the significant speed, scaling, and task performance advantages of AI (Chalmers et al., 2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019), questions persist over whether it is simply ‘a matter of time’ before AI utterly transforms entrepreneurship or whether the capabilities of AI systems are simply being overhyped (Lévesque, Obschonka, & Nambisan, 2022; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020). These debates are complicated by recent breakthroughs in generative AI and related models that amplify the inflated optimism amidst acclamations that “scale is all you need” (Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023). Although optimism that ‘more data and better algorithms’ will continue to empower entrepreneurial ventures to tackle seemingly ‘impossible’ computational tasks is not entirely unfounded (Chalmers et al.,

2020; Giuggioi & Pellegrini, 2022; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022), AI skeptics are quick to point out a generation’s worth of overhyped predictions of AI ascendency, which in their view, has always culminated in a predictable series of ‘AI winters’ (cf. Duan, Edwards, & Dwivedi, 2019).

Parsing and adjudicating the differences between AI advocates and skeptics is difficult because, in some sense, both groups are correct – recent breakthroughs in AI systems, such as OpenAI’s GPT-4, are clearly transformative technologies that are, nonetheless, overhyped – both favorably and unfavorably – in public discourse (Vinsel, 2023). Analyzing the capabilities, tradeoffs, and limitations of AI technologies (Nilsson, 1998) is critically important in entrepreneurship research to chronicle not only the transformative organizational impacts but also to identify the limitations of AI (e.g., Chalmers et al., 2020; Lévesque et al., 2022; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). In the following section we take steps to specify these lines of demarcation and the definitional work surrounding them.

##### Humans versus Machines? The AI Task Performance Debate

Contemporary research in the field of AI draws on the foundational work of Gödel, Church, Turing, and others, “…to create and understand intelligence as a general property of systems, rather than as a specific attribute of humans” (Russell, 1997: 57). Although AI, as a scientific field, has (in)famously eluded adoption of a single, precise definition (Mitchell, 2019), leading theorists generally accept that AI is "(technology) that seeks to make computers do the sorts of things minds can do" through the development of computational processes that "(…) involve psychological skills – such as perception, association, prediction, planning, motor control—that enable humans and animals to attain their goals" (Boden, 2018: 1). Thus conceived, the field of AI is united by a common goal to create systems of 'machine intelligence' that simulate or surpass the task performance capabilities of humans (Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb, 2019b; Russell & Norvig, 2016).

Progress towards these goals has been rapid in recent years. Across numerous other business functions, the use of AI in both new and established ventures provides significant performance advantages over humans to tackle key tasks in a variety of areas. The accelerating improvement of the task performance of AI raises important questions for entrepreneurship scholars regarding the use of AI in both established and new ventures (e.g., Lévesque et al., 2022; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). At the heart of these decisions lie critical questions regarding what, how, why, and when AI systems will be able to deliver superior task performance (i.e., the success of machines in accomplishing job-related goals and objectives – Grace, Salvatier, Dafoe, Zhang, & Evans, 2018) relative to humans. Experts offer mixed assessments and competing forecasts concerning the rate of AI's task performance advancement (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). In a recent global survey, a large sample of AI experts has predicted that there is a 50% chance "unaided machines (will) accomplish every task more cheaply and better than human workers" by 2061, resulting in the full automation of human labor by AI over the next century (Grace et al., 2018). Consistent with this view, Shepherd and Majchrzak (2022: 8) cite a recent McKinsey & Company report, which predicts that by 2030, "30% of the activities [involving] 60% of all careers" will be impacted by AI. OpenAI researchers have recently forecasted that nearly one-fifth of all U.S. workers could have at least 50% of their tasks impacted by the introduction of large language models – an emerging type of generative AI (Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, & Rock, 2023).

At the same time, despite the growing number of accomplishments of various AI systems, other scholars remain skeptical regarding the ability of AI to match or surpass the reasoning abilities and task performance of human actors (e.g., Bishop, 2021; Floridi, 2023; Gigerenzer, 2022). These scholars often point to numerous prior examples of overblown prognostications about

the ability of AI technologies to replicate ‘human intelligence’ as a warning against over-

estimating the capabilities of AI systems (Shaw, Rudzicz, Jamieson, & Goldfarb, 2019). Bishop

(2021) provocatively argues that “AI is stupid,” predicting that even emerging causal AI systems

will only be as effective as the human engineering underlying its existence. He further argues that

future breakthroughs in algorithm development will, at best, incompletely address the underlying

incapacity of AI to replicate human thought (Bishop, 2021). The ongoing debate between AI

advocates and skeptics highlights the contingent role of AI task performance in entrepreneurship

– in some cases, AI systems provide superior capabilities, while in other cases, the capabilities of

AI systems are far more limited to tackling key problems in entrepreneurship (Chalmers et al.,

2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022). Toward this end, both views will be emphasized in detail.

### Benefits & Limitations of Foundational & Emerging Types of AI in Entrepreneurship

In recent years, key breakthroughs in AI algorithms have enabled companies to solve extraordinarily complex computational tasks (Agrawal et al., 2018). For example, Insilico Medicine, an AI drug discovery startup, utilizes an integrated set of AI systems to analyze a large volume of scientific research pertinent to the development of new therapeutics (Insilico, 2019). The integration of Insilico’s unique, AI-driven drug discovery model speeds up the process of drug discovery by orders of magnitude – in some cases, reducing the time it takes to identify new drug candidates from an average of three years to three weeks (Hale, 2020). ### Foundational Types of AI: Logical AI & Machine Learning

Since the birth of AI as a scientific field at the Dartmouth conference in 1956, foundational approaches to AI research have been dominated by two major approaches: logical AI and machine learning $ ^{2} $. For several decades, logic-based approaches to AI – essentially what AI scholars now describe as “Good Old-Fashioned AI” or GOFAI – were constructed using symbolic and propositional logic systems. Logic-based systems rely on deductive reasoning and require an enormous array of if-then statements to anticipate all the contingent possibilities to guide an AI’s decisions. As Boden (2014: 90) notes, such systems are designed to handle computational tasks “represented as a search space: a set of possibilities (defined by a finite set of generative rules), within which the solution lies – and within which it must be found.” Boden (2014) further notes that “in many cases, a systematic “brute force” search through the space is impractical because there are too many possibilities to be individually considered.”

Computing solutions to complex decision problems with logic-based systems require an exponentially increasing amount of data and computational power as each new bit of data increases the array of possibilities in a decision problem. IBM’s development of the Deep Blue system in 1997 to compete with Garry Kasparov – the World Champion chess grandmaster at the time (Hsu, 2004) – provides a useful example of the benefits and limitations of such a system. The Deep Blue system possessed a staggering computational advantage over Kasparov since it could evaluate 200M+ possible chess moves per second while Kasparov could, at best, evaluate three moves per second, relying “on computational power and simpler search and evaluation function” (Nilsson, 2009: 32). Even so, the purported superiority in computational prowess is not always an asset.

As noted above, part of the challenge is that logical AI systems are less suited to tackle other problems when the array of possibilities expands exponentially with each new decision by other actors in the decision environment. Such environments create a complex set of knowledge representation problems for logical AI systems since the algorithms do not know which information is relevant to the decision or task-at-hand (Levesque, 1986). These problems become intractable when logical AI systems attempt to use deductive reasoning that applies formally specified rules to specific cases that often defy simple classification and analysis (Marcus, 2020). In these situations, the amount of knowledge that must be encoded and stored expands exponentially as the complexity of the task increases (Levesque, 1986). Such knowledge representation problems remain a significant problem in autonomous robotics and other similar AI domains, where researchers are attempting to build intelligent machines capable of navigating uncertain environments where multiple agents interact in complex and unexpected ways (Belle, Bolander, Herzig, & Nebel, 2023).

Scholars have explored the role of heuristic AI methods that use commonsense ‘shortcuts,’ ‘rules of thumb,’ or hybrid approaches to reduce computational burdens (e.g., Marcus, 2020), but knowledge representation problems remain intractable for logical AI systems in uncertain task environments. IBM encountered the real-world equivalent of these knowledge representation problems when the company’s corporate entrepreneurial venture, Watson Health, struggled to adapt the venture’s automated reasoning capabilities to address complex medical problems in the healthcare industry (Hernandez & Fitch, 2021; Ross & Swetlitz, 2018). Although project researchers encouraged Watson Health executives to take a narrower, more measured approach when discussing the capabilities of the system, IBM leaders continued to over-hype the Watson system (Lohr, 2021). David Ferrucci, one of the key scientists who developed the platform, noted

in an interview that “Watson was engineered to identify word patterns and predict correct answers for the trivia game. It was not an all-purpose answer box ready to take on the commercial world…It might well fail a second-grade reading comprehension test” (Lohr, 2021).

Watson Health’s decision problems are apt illustrations of the knowledge representation challenges that logical AI systems would face if, for instance, they were tasked to compute solutions in the game of Go, where every possible move in the game expands the scope and size of the problem exponentially. For logical AI systems, the decision problem in Go is ‘uncomputable’ as there are more possible moves than stars in the known universe (DeepMind, 2019). Yet, nearly 20 years after Deep Blue’s victory over Garry Kasparov, Alphabet/DeepMind’s AI system AlphaGo accomplished exactly this feat by using a completely different set of AI algorithms leveraging new breakthroughs in deep learning systems (DeepMind, 2019). The AI system was not programmed with an explicit set of game rules or strategies; instead, AlphaGo ‘learned’ these rules by using a series of algorithms applying Bayesian reasoning to learn effective game strategies. AI researchers at Deep Mind built on the success of AlphaGo to develop new approaches to machine/deep learning that have been reportedly successful in solving one of the grand challenges of biology by solving the protein folding problem – a breakthrough that scientists believe will revolutionize drug discovery (Callaway, 2020).

Yet, in other cases, entrepreneurs have faced significant challenges in using deep learning systems to tackle important entrepreneurial tasks. Zillow Offers, a corporate venture of the real estate technology firm, Zillow, leveraged its parent company's massive 'big data' platform to automate the 'house-flipping process,' whereby the company purchased 'undervalued' homes from willing sellers and then tried to sell the homes for a quick profit to other buyers. In this situation, the corporate venture's knowledge representation system was built on a probabilistic

analysis of housing prices based on historical data. Although the ML system could model degrees of uncertainty based on historical frequencies of housing prices using massive data, such as spanning 15 years of transaction data, Zillow Offer's AI model failed because of the unexpected volatility of housing prices following the COVID-19 pandemic. In these situations, the probabilistic underpinnings of the corporate venture's ML models could not account for unexpected new possibilities in the decision environment.

Zillow Offer’s failure reflects the fundamental challenges machine learning systems face with ‘model brittleness’ (i.e., “their unpredictable errors and lack of robust generalization abilities”) when trying to apply ML models to new or dynamic decision environments (Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023). When real-life situations differ from the training data used to develop these systems, ML models can produce unpredictable errors (Heaven, 2019). For these reasons, a growing number of AI scholars and practitioners note that “there are no fixes for the fundamental brittleness of deep neural networks” (Chollet, 2019). The extent of this challenge is even evident in the increasingly sophisticated, emergent types of AI, which we delve into in the next section.

## Emerging Types of AI: Generative AI & Causal AI

The contrast between Zillow Offer’s failure to leverage ‘big data’ to automate the home buying process and Deep Mind’s success in using machine learning algorithms to solve the protein folding problem is at least partly a function of Deep Mind’s use and accurate allocation of emerging types of AI to augment traditional machine learning systems. In particular, Deep Mind used an emerging type of generative algorithm to create new, winning strategies for the game of Go. A growing number of other startups are creating and using generative algorithms and transform architectures to power high-profile creative breakthroughs in AI systems, such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion, among others. Generative AI systems are designed to

learn patterns from input data and then generate novel, yet statistically similar, output. These models leverage complex algorithms derived from probability theory and statistics to create new instances of data that mimic the distribution of the original training data.

The generative reasoning that serves as the foundation of these systems builds upon informational cues in the environment “that create possible solutions to problems that the machine encounters while interacting with its environment” (van der Zant, Kouw, & Schomaker, 2013: 113). At the same time, these systems “…do not think, reason, or understand…they have nothing to do with the cognitive processes present…in the human brain and mind, to manage semantic contents successfully” (Floridi, 2023). In this sense, generative AI systems lack the ability to understand semantic meaning, as reflected in the tendencies of these systems – particularly large language models – to ‘hallucinate’ concepts, ideas, and text in their model outputs (Wolfram, 2023). These tendencies stem from the use of stochastic functions built on next-word-prediction probabilistic models that are used to compute the output (Wolfram, 2023).

Although the capabilities of these systems are generating impressive breakthroughs across a variety of different computational tasks, there are significant limitations and risks with these approaches. In one simple, but notable, example, the startup Solid Gold Bomb created an automated, print-on-demand business on Amazon that sold variations of the "Keep Calm and Carry On" t-shirts. Instead of trying to predict which versions of the "Keep Calm" t-shirt would resonate with customers, the startup used a basic type of generative algorithm that would randomly combine different phrases to create new phrases. The startup was soon forced to close operations after considerable backlash from customers in response to a series of violent and misogynistic messages that the algorithm posted as possible t-shirt designs on the startup's Amazon store. The algorithm, of course, had no ability to understand the semantic meaning of the messages on the shirt; the

algorithms were simply randomly combining words and tracking whether t-shirt designs attracted the attention of potential customers. While the Generative AI algorithms that are emerging are certainly more sophisticated than the algorithms used by Solid Gold Bomb, the fundamental computational approaches still rely on ‘random’ variation to generate novel output. For these reasons, prominent AI scholars and practitioners argue that the problems with generative AI systems are “not fixable” due, in part, to the use of probabilistic models in environments where the future is only ‘partially predictable’ (LeCun, 2018; LeCun et al., 2006; Wolfram, 2023).

The reality that decision environments are only partially predictable provides important insights into the limitations of another emerging type of AI – Causal AI -- which combines causal inference with machine learning to address the challenges of predicting decision outcomes in complex and dynamic environments. Startups such as Insilico Medicine use causal AI systems along with an ensemble of generative AI systems to predict whether a new drug will be successful in completing clinical trials. Other startups like StitchFix use causal AI systems (i.e., Directed Acyclic Graphs – DAGs) to model new marketing campaigns before launch to help inform strategic decisions of where and how to invest their resources (Landesberg, Davies, & Yee, 2019). The effectiveness of these tools, however, is still contingent upon the extent to which different future possibilities are accounted for in the decision model. Sentient Investment Management, one of the first ‘automated’ hedge funds, used an ensemble of sophisticated AI genetic/evolutionary algorithms to manage the startup’s portfolio of investments. The startup closed down after 18 months due to poor investment returns. Despite the startup’s sophisticated use of AI, the investment strategy implemented by the AI failed to account for the recursive impact of its own trading activity on asset prices (Byrum, 2022).

In a competitive marketplace, the failure to account for the recursive impact of choices to intervene, or not, in decision environments amplifies the problem of an unknowable future. Although causal AI tools can help address some uncertainties in predicting the future consequences of interventions, the task performance of causal AI is limited when trying to infer the consequences of novel and unexpected actions. As Bishop notes (2021:17), "without genuine understanding, the ability to seamlessly transfer relevant knowledge from one domain to another will remain (elusive)." Bishop (2021) goes on to argue that "lacking phenomenal sensation (in which to both ground meaning and desire), even a system with a 'complete explanatory model' (allowing it to accurately predict future states) would still lack intentional pull, with which to drive genuinely autonomous teleological behavior." Though powerful and useful, these emerging systems represent a "new form of artificial agency" that lacks intelligence and understanding and, thus, the ability to fully govern the system's own behavior (Floridi, 2023). In this sense, both generative and causal AI systems neither possess fully proficient semantic awareness nor agentic control, thereby limiting the ability to address problems that are central to entrepreneurship.

##### The Problem of Uncertainty & the Fundamental Limits of AI Task Performance

As the foregoing makes plain, the capacity of both foundational and emerging types of AI to address the problems of knowledge representation, model brittleness, semantic meaning, and agentic control reflects a more fundamental and vexing paradox facing all AI systems – that for some computational tasks what is easy for humans can be difficult for AI (Autor, 2014; Minsky, 1987). At the heart of these challenges, Gigerenzer (2022) argues, is the problem of uncertainty: "When the environment is stable, AI can surpass humans. If the future is like the past, large amounts of data are useful. However, if surprises happen, big data – which is always data from the past—may mislead us about the future." Gigerenzer (2022: p. X) goes on to argue that "many of

the problems we face are not well-defined games but situations in which uncertainty abounds" and that "humans are the key source of uncertainty."

In similar fashion, a variety of scholars from other fields, including several prominent computational theorists, express a similar problem of uncertainty as a critical boundary condition on the use of different types of AI (e.g., Mathieu, Couprie, LeCun, 2016), and are increasingly calling for alternative approaches that extend beyond the use of probabilistic reasoning to contend with the problem of uncertainty (e.g., Matsuo, LeCun, Sahani, Precup, & Silver, Sigiyama, Uchibe, & Morimoto, 2022). Although the Bayesian logic at the heart of both traditional and emerging AI systems can address some facets of the problem of uncertainty, the advocacy for new approaches is rooted in both technical and philosophical concerns. On the technical side, in probabilistic approaches to AI, such as Bayesian networks, it is essential to ensure that the probabilities within a distribution sum to 1, a process referred to as normalization. These models achieve normalization by adding a constant to maintain a valid probability distribution. Unfortunately, the use of normalization constants can create numerous computational problems since these models require the integration of all possible outcomes (LeCun et al., 2006), leading, in some cases, to highly inaccurate models' predictions (Gelman & Meng, 1998). These technical issues are significant enough that even though the use of probabilistic approaches allows AI systems to compute solutions to uncertain decision problems, the technical limitations of these models still prevent accurate prediction – even probabilistically! – in environments that are only partially predictable.

The premise that decision environments will never be more than merely partially predictable reflects the fundamental philosophical skepticism held by many prominent AI theorists that all decision problems can be successfully solved through computational methods. The central issue in uncomputable decision problems stems from the computational irreducibility of some

tasks. As Wolfram (2002: 73) notes, “computational irreducibility leads to a much more fundamental problem with prediction…even if in principle one has all the information one needs to work out how some particular system will behave, it can still take an irreducible amount of computational work actually to do this.” Unfortunately, Wolfram (2002: 73-4) concludes, “there is in fact always a fundamental competition between systems used to make predictions and systems whose behavior one tries to predict.”

For entrepreneurship scholars researching the use and limits of AI in entrepreneurship, Wolfram's (2023) insights highlight the fundamental implications of the transformative use of AI in entrepreneurship. As much as AI transforms the practice of entrepreneurship in the coming years, the field itself will change in substantial but unpredictable ways. In the following section, we explore these linkages to analyze how the problems of Knightian uncertainty create important boundary conditions for the use of AI in entrepreneurship.

##### KNIGHTIAN UNCERTAINTY & ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

In entrepreneurship research, the problem of uncertainty refers to the challenges entrepreneurs face when “making investments today without knowing what the distribution of the returns will be tomorrow” (Venkataraman, 1997:120; see also Foss & Klein, 2012; Gans et al., 2019; McMullen, 2015; McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Packard et al., 2017; Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Sarasvathy, 2009; Townsend et al., 2018; Wood, Bakker & Fisher, 2021). For entrepreneurs, the problem of uncertainty complicates a vast array of fundamentally important and practical entrepreneurial tasks, including which products or services to develop (Gans, Stern, & Wu, 2019), which market segments to target (Gruber, MacMillan, & Thompson, 2008), or how to best design the venture's business model to realize future performance objectives (Demil, Lecocq, Ricart & Zott, 2015).

To address the problem of uncertainty, entrepreneurship scholars have explored a variety of decision tools and strategies that entrepreneurs use to navigate uncertain environments. $ ^{3} $ In recent studies, a growing number of scholars have speculated that emerging AI technologies will provide useful tools to help managers and entrepreneurs address the problem of uncertainty (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2018; Agrawal, Gans, & Goldfarb, 2019b; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). As Agrawal and colleagues (2018: 2) note, “…the new wave of artificial intelligence (brings about) a critical component of intelligence—prediction…(and) better prediction reduces uncertainty” (see also, Agrawal et al., 2019b). In contrast, other scholars argue that the problem of uncertainty presents an important set of obstacles for AI systems in entrepreneurship (Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Townsend & Hunt, 2019); since the future states of entrepreneurial decision environments are, to a significant degree, unpredictable and unknowable (Ramoglou, 2021; Ramoglou & McMullen, 2023; Townsend et al., 2018).

Building on these views, our central premise builds on the fundamental distinction between Knightian uncertainty and other types of uncertainty in the business venturing process to assert that the problems of Knightian uncertainty create important boundary conditions for the use of AI in entrepreneurship (McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Townsend et al., 2018; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). As new possibilities are actualized in the business environment through entrepreneurial action (Ramoglou, 2021), entrepreneurs confront the problems of Knightian uncertainty (Knight, 1921; McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Townsend et al., 2018) -- actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion – as a set of formidable obstacles when striving to navigate an unknowable array of future possibilities that are neither analyzable nor predictable using statistical analysis and probabilistic reasoning.

For AI systems, since both foundational and emerging types of AI rely on statistical analysis as the basis of computation, the ubiquitous presence of Knightian uncertainty in entrepreneurship illuminates the boundary conditions governing the use of AI in entrepreneurship (Townsend & Hunt, 2019). In this section, we explore and analyze these issues by bridging existing work on the limitations of foundational and emerging types of AI with the problems of Knightian uncertainty.

### Theoretical Background: What is Knightian Uncertainty?

For more than a century, Knight’s (1921) foundational work on the nature and role of uncertainty in entrepreneurship – Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit (RUP) – has been instrumental in shaping how economics (e.g., Raiffa & Luce, 1957; Ellsberg, 1961; Lawson, 1988), organizational (e.g., Duncan, 1972) and entrepreneurship (e.g., Busenitz & Barney, 1997; Sarasvathy, 2001; McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Townsend et al., 2018) scholars theorize the role of uncertainty in economic and social phenomena. In the field of entrepreneurship, Knight’s theory of uncertainty has substantially shaped the development of virtually all of the field’s most prominent theories, including foundational approaches to entrepreneurial orientation (Miller, 2011), entrepreneur cognition (Busenitz & Barney, 1997), effectuation (Sarasvathy, 2001), entrepreneurial action (McMullen & Shepherd, 2006), creation theory (Alvarez & Barney, 2007), and the judgment-based analysis (Foss & Klein, 2012).

Knight’s (1921) fundamental thesis in RUP is reflected in a now-classic dichotomization of risk and uncertainty wherein he differentiates ‘true uncertainty’ – an unmeasurable type of uncertainty – from other types of ‘uncertainty’ that are measurable through statistical analyses. In Knight’s view (1921), unmeasurable (true) uncertainties play a central role in creating the possibility of generating economic profits since, under these conditions, markets are unable to

price assets accurately. In contrast, when uncertainties are measurable, Knight (1921) argues that entrepreneurs can use the tools of probability theory or insurance to hedge against such measurable risks. Contemporary research in entrepreneurship builds on Knight's distinction between risk and uncertainty to examine decision problems where the use of probabilistic tools provides limited value to addressing the problem of uncertainty (e.g., Feduzi et al., 2022; Hertwig et al., 2019; Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Townsend et al., 2018).

To build his approach, Knight (1921) grounds the general categories of risk and uncertainty in the Kantian theory of judgment (Kant, 1800) to align three types of probability judgments (i.e., a priori, statistical, and estimates) with Kant's three modalities of judgment (i.e., apodeictic, assertoric, and problematic). In the Kantian framework, problematic judgments reflect the most fundamental type of uncertainty about whether something is possible or impossible (Kant, 1800). Contemporary decision theorists refer to this type of uncertainty as “modal uncertainty,” encompassing what Bradley and Drechsler (2014: 1229) describe as “uncertainty about what is possible or what could be the case…(and) concerns the make-up of the space of states and consequences, and hence what actions are possible.” Knight (1921: 243) draws on similar logic to argue that “judgment or foresight and the capacity for planning and the ability to execute action… (under conditions of uncertainty) are each the product of…(a) accuracy, (b) promptness or speed, (c) time range, and (d) space range.” Building on this logic, the baseline condition of Knightian uncertainty is a type of modal uncertainty that reflects the fundamental problems entrepreneurs face in developing ‘future knowledge’ about what will be possible or impossible in the future.

The critical distinction between Knightian uncertainty and the other types of uncertainty is reflected in the challenges entrepreneurs face in knowing what is possible (or impossible) in the future states of decision environments. In Knight’s view, however, this state of ‘modal uncertainty’

is not simply a function of the ignorance of the entrepreneurs or a limitation of the tools of probability theory. Instead, Knight (1921) argues that ‘true uncertainty’ is rooted in the dynamic and unpredictable nature of the decision environment itself: “It is a world of change in which we live, and a world of uncertainty. We live only by knowing something about the future; while the problems of life, or of conduct at least, arise from the fact that we know so little. This is as true of business as of other spheres of activity” (Knight, 1921: 199). For Knight (1921), the knowledge problems entrepreneurs face in ‘knowing something about the future’ are rooted in the indeterminism of the environment – the fact that future states of the world can evolve in dynamic and unpredictable ways (Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Townsend et al., 2018). In this sense, the ignorance of the entrepreneur about the future and the limitations of probability theory are not temporary states that can be resolved through inductive processes of knowledge acquisition or through the development of better probabilistic tools.

#### The Problems of Knightian Uncertainty & the Computational Limits of AI

For entrepreneurs, the problems of Knightian uncertainty are rooted in the problems actors face in knowing what will be possible or impossible in the future. Under these conditions, no matter how much information the entrepreneur collects or generates, computing whether a future state is possible or impossible will only ever be, at best, partially predictable due to the dynamic nature of decision environments. This dynamism is, in turn, rooted in the actions of entrepreneurs to actualize (or not) new possibilities in the system through acts of economic pioneering (McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Ramoglou & McMullen, 2023; Townsend et al., 2018).

Thus, the central premise of our theoretical approach contends that while the growing sophistication of both foundational and emerging types of AI will provide novel tools and methods for addressing the problems of natural and empirical uncertainty (i.e., risks), the problems of

Knightian uncertainty manifested through four interrelated problems confronting entrepreneurs – actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion – create the computational limits on the use of AI in entrepreneurship. ##### Actor Ignorance & Knowledge Representation

The first problem of Knightian uncertainty is the problem of actor ignorance. In Knight's theory, the problem of actor ignorance refers to the difficulties entrepreneurs face in specifying whether different possibilities are relevant or not in a decision environment: "we live only by knowing something about the future; while the problems of life, or of conduct at least, arise from the fact that we know so little." (Knight, 1921: 199). At the core of this problem are the intersecting premises that there are too many possibilities in the world for a finite intelligence to know all of the relevant factors in a particular situation and how these factors will interact with each other. As Knight (1921: 207) argues: "The world is made up of objects which are practically infinite in variety...and when we consider the number of objects which function in any particular situation, and their possible variety, it is evident that only an infinite intelligence could grasp all of the possible combinations."

For entrepreneurs, the problem of actor ignorance manifests in situations where they do not know which possibilities might exist (or not) in future decision environments. Actor ignorance is a knowledge problem (Townsend et al., 2018) that exists at the actor level as it reflects an entrepreneur's ignorance or lack of information about what is possible (or impossible). Entrepreneurs resolve actor ignorance by gathering information so they can determine what will

be possible or impossible for them in the future. In this sense, actor ignorance is similar to the problems of knowledge representation facing AI systems (e.g., Brundage, 2014; Martínez & Fernández-Rodríguez, 2015).

Resolving the problem of actor ignorance with AI requires the AI system to solve a variety of computational tasks relating to ‘model parameterization’ where the system must determine whether different bits of information are relevant (or not) to the task at hand (e.g., Eckstein et al., 2022). Resolving the problem of actor ignorance can be difficult for logical AI systems because the system requires rules and explicit guidelines for determining whether the information is relevant or not. In a recent article, Marcus (2020: 6) acknowledges the challenges logical AI systems face with regard to knowledge representation: “we must refocus, working towards developing a framework for building systems that can routinely acquire, represent, and manipulate abstract knowledge, using that knowledge in the service of building, updating, and reasoning over complex, internal models of the external world.”

Marcus (2020: 28) recognizes the challenges of pursuing this approach to addressing the knowledge representation problem, noting that “connecting abstract knowledge to specific real-world situations at scale in an efficient fashion is essentially an unsolved problem.” At the core of these problems is the recognition that much of the knowledge that is most useful to entrepreneurs is tacit (e.g., Polanyi, 1962), learned over time through experience. The rules-based computational processes used by logical AI systems lack the ability to understand ‘tacit knowledge,’ thereby creating what some scholars call the Polanyi paradox in AI (i.e., tasks that may be easy for humans are often hard for AI systems – Autor, 2014).

However, when knowledge can be represented and described using symbolic logic or quantifiable scientific laws, AI systems become much more effective in analyzing these bits of

information. As we have noted, Insilico Medicine utilizes a comprehensive set of AI natural language processing tools in the product development process to analyze millions of pages of scientific research to identify relevant new scientific discoveries regarding different diseases (Insilico, 2019). Insilico's success in using AI to analyze a large volume of scientific research in order to identify relevant scientific discoveries is not simply a function of using more sophisticated algorithms but is also aided by the type of scientific information that is being analyzed. Although the possibility space for new drugs is massively large, it is knowable in so far as the known laws of science can govern the search process.

In contrast, when entrepreneurs try to use AI technologies to contend with problems where the future possibilities are unknowable, the computational task becomes exponentially more difficult. These problems are aptly demonstrated by the challenges Argo.AI faced in trying to commercialize fully automated self-driving technologies. Despite the technological sophistication of the venture's self-driving AI, the fast-changing, dynamic environment inherent in everyday driving, while comparatively easy for humans to navigate, still remains too difficult for startups like Argo.AI to tackle successfully. Overall, both foundational and emerging types of AI vary in their capacity to address the problem of actor ignorance and knowledge representation. However, when these AI systems cannot determine what is possible or impossible in the future, these computational tasks will remain difficult for these systems to handle successfully.

##### Practical Indeterminism & Model Brittleness

While the limitations ‘finite intelligences’ face in comprehending the overwhelming complexity of the world are, of course, common to everyone, the problem of actor ignorance is further complicated by the problem of practical indeterminism. In Knight’s theory, practical indeterminism refers to the fact that, at the environmental level, multiple unknown future

possibilities are continually generated in the decision environment. In these situations, Knight (1921: 198-9) argues that “even though the businessman could not know in advance the results of individual ventures, he could operate and base his competitive offers upon accurate foreknowledge of the future if quantitative knowledge of the probability of every possible outcome can be had” (Knight, 1921: 198-9). In other words, if all the future possibilities are knowable, then the entrepreneur can still operate successfully by using probabilistic reasoning to determine the proper course of action.

In Knightian theory, the problem of practical indeterminism raises questions as to whether gathering enough information will ever enable actors to predict the future with complete certainty. Consistent with this view, Knight (1921) argues that even if an individual is able to access all of this information, each decision situation would be shaped by 'unknowable causes,' which would limit the ability of the entrepreneur to predict the future. Under these conditions, entrepreneurs act as if the environment is indeterministic; "The postulates of knowledge generally involve the conclusion that it is really determined in the nature of things which house will burn, which man die, and which face of the thrown die will come uppermost. The logic which we actually use, however, assumes that the result is really indeterminate, that the unknowable causes actually follow a law of indifference" (Knight, 1921: 219).

The problem of practical indeterminism plays an important role in entrepreneurial decision environments. As Knight (1921) argues, changes in the environment do not prevent entrepreneurs from predicting the future as long as the ‘law of change’ is known. For example, demographic changes play an important role in shaping the vitality and distribution of economic opportunities over the long run in countries around the world. Although these slow changes do require entrepreneurs to adapt their plans to these shifting demographics, as long as the rate of change falls

within the range of possible values, the startup can prepare for these environmental changes. In this sense, practical indeterminism is, like actor ignorance, a knowledge problem, but one that occurs at the environmental level instead of the actor level. Resolving practical indeterminism, thus, requires entrepreneurs to collect bits of information about the overall environment with the goal of determining what will be affordable or not in the future (Ramoglou, 2021).

However, this is every bit as difficult as it sounds, evidenced most recently by the sudden economic and social shocks borne out of the Covid-19 pandemic, which uncompromisingly exposed the brittleness of forecasting models when unexpected changes extend well beyond the range of possible outcomes any organization could have anticipated. The widespread failure of startups at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic illustrates the problem that practical indeterminism creates for entrepreneurs. For AI systems, the problem of practical indeterminism is likewise tied to the problem of model brittleness (Mitchell, 2021), since it is also subject to the significant challenges accompanying the need to account for unexpected, dynamic changes in the environment (Agrawal et al., 2018). In the aftermath of the failure of Zillow Offer, its CEO acknowledged that “we’ve determined the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated” (Clark, 2021). Although the venture possessed a massive database of prior transactions, unexpected volatility in the system exposed the brittleness of the AI models, thereby undermining the system’s ability to make accurate inferences about future real estate prices.

The use of Bayesian learning in machine learning algorithms addresses some of the problems encountered by ventures such as Zillow Offers by accounting for statistical uncertainty in a predictive model. For example, Ximantis utilizes a set of stochastic machine-learning models to forecast traffic congestion. The company offers AI as a service to help reduce fuel consumption for companies with large driving fleets with the stated goal of helping companies achieve their

sustainability goals. The startup’s success in using stochastic machine learning is directly tied to the nature of the computational task the venture is attempting to solve. Unlike Argo.Ai, which was attempting to solve the problem of actor ignorance by equipping individual self-driving cars with the technology to automate the driving process, Ximantis is solving a different problem through the inferential analysis of aggregated traffic data through machine learning. Even though the underlying problem is computationally complex, the use of stochastic machine learning allows the startup to predict traffic congestion probabilistically, even in a chaotic system, because the startup can aggregate the data together to analyze it statistically, especially during periods of relatively stable or statistically repetitive environmental conditions.

### Agentic Novelty & Semantic Meaning

The problem both entrepreneurs and AI systems face in contending with the problem of practical indeterminism is further complicated by the fact that, in many cases, practical indeterminism is actually caused by entrepreneurial action. On this point, Knight (1921: 221) relates, “if there is real indeterminateness, and if the ultimate seat of it is in the activities of the human (or perhaps organic) machine, there is in a sense an opening of the door to a conception of freedom in conduct.” Knight’s musings on the ‘human machine’ as the source of real indeterminateness illuminate the third problem of Knightian uncertainty – agentic novelty. In Knightian theory, agentic novelty refers to the actions entrepreneurs take to introduce new possibilities into the system that diverge from existing approaches. Entrepreneurs engage in agentic novelty, in part, to generate economic profits through the pursuit of unique opportunities (Ramoglou & McMullen, 2023) through acts of economic pioneering (Knight, 1942). In related work, Knight (1942: 128) argues that the “first and primary function (of entrepreneurs), in a

progressive society, is that of leadership or economic pioneering; it is to initiate useful changes or innovations. The incentive to new departures is profit in the corresponding sense."

In this sense, unlike the problems of actor ignorance or practical indeterminism, the problem of agentic novelty is an action problem in so much as the level of uncertainty is tied to the actions (or non-actions) the entrepreneur takes. However, similar to actor ignorance, agentic novelty occurs at the actor level as entrepreneurs enact ‘new departures’ to ‘initiate useful changes or innovations.’ In these cases, these agentic acts of economic pioneering often represent a willful departure from established norms and practices in order to produce new innovations that appeal to customers. At the heart of economic pioneering is the willingness to try something new that reconceptualizes customer needs and wants in novel ways. In doing so, agentic novelty challenges entrepreneurs to engage in counterfactual reasoning – to imagine they might be able to actualize the future through entrepreneurial action (Ramoglou & McMullen, 2022). Contemplating such actions requires empathetic judgment and an understanding of key customer problems, needs, and wants (McMullen, 2015). The rapid growth and development of generative AI tools, such as Open AI's ChatGPT, Stable Diffusion's text-to-image AI, and other recent generative AI startups, can certainly offer creative new ideas for entrepreneurs to explore (Townsend & Hunt, 2019). However, these technologies will always be limited because the underlying generative process ultimately relies on a stochastic function to generate new possibilities. There is no semantic awareness or empathetic accuracy underpinning these efforts – problems which are reflected in the tendency of these systems to “hallucinate facts” and data in model output.

To help mitigate these risks, Stitch Fix keeps ‘humans-in-the-loop’ to provide oversight and modify the creative output of the startup’s AI over the production and distribution of clothing items to customers (Sibley et al., 2016). Numerous startups are facing significant challenges in

addressing these problems of agentic novelty and semantic meaning in managing their AI systems.

OpenAI spent a significant amount of time in ‘adversarial testing’ and has implemented strict controls to try to limit the production and distribution of harmful content by ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2023). Startups building text-to-image/video AI tools have faced an ongoing battle to prevent users from abusing the system by producing illegal content such as child pornography or deep fake videos and pictures (e.g., Westerlund, 2019). In a fashion similar to Insilico Medicine, several drug discovery startups have implemented strict controls to prevent the technology from being used to create harmful chemical or biological weapons (e.g., Urbina, Lentzos, Invernizzi, & Ekins, 2022).

However, Yann LeCun, Meta’s head of AI Research, argues that these problems are not solvable algorithmically because the underlying model’s reliance on probabilistic reasoning will not produce semantic awareness in the AI of what it is producing (LeCun, 2018). These systems are powerful, creative, and useful for creating new possibilities through the production of novel products and services. At the same time, these AI systems are also vapid and blind, producing novelties through a stochastic mixing of different possibilities and lacking awareness of what is produced. In this sense, generative AI tools address dimensions of the agentic novelty problem through the production of novel things but without any understanding of the semantic meaning of what is being created. Overall, addressing the problems of agentic novelty requires entrepreneurs to understand and interpret the implications of their ‘new departures.’ For now, AI systems lack the ability to understand and interpret the meaning of the actions and choices the systems are making, and thus, AI systems will likely still struggle to address the problem of agentic novelty.

### Competitive Recursion & Agentic Control

These 'new departures' through acts of agentic novelty are an important source of new possibilities in the economic system but also lead to the fourth problem of Knightian uncertainty

– competitive recursion. In Knightian theory, the problem of competitive recursion refers to the consequences of the novel choices and actions of actors to intervene or not that recursively feed back into the decision environment. These actions or non-actions cascade and ripple through the business environment, amplifying the problems of knowing what will be possible or impossible in the future. These problems stem from the difficulty of 'twofold inference,' where entrepreneurs "must infer what the future situation would have been without our interference, and what change will be wrought in it by our action." Unfortunately, Knight (1921: 202) argues, "none of these processes is infallible, or indeed ever accurate and complete. As a result, entrepreneurs in both new firms and corporate ventures react and act in pursuit of profits, predicting future consequences of these ongoing, complex sequences of action and amplifying "the fundamental uncertainties of economic life (as) the errors in predicting the future and in making present adjustments to fit future conditions" (Knight, 1921: 259).

The actualization of new possibilities through acts of agentic novelty is an important source of new opportunities in the economic system (Ramoglou, 2021). Like the problem of agentic novelty, the problem of competitive recursion is an action problem in that as the choices and actions of actors to intervene (or not) ripple recursively through the decision environment, the problem of Knightian uncertainty is amplified in entrepreneurship. In this sense, unlike agentic novelty, the problem of competitive recursion manifests in the broader decision environment, encompassing an unknowable set of competitive actors who may or may not intervene in a marketplace. These actions subsume the challenges with 'double inference' highlighted in Knight's theory. When competitors choose to (or not to) intervene in decision environments, the dynamic nature of competition leads to future moves and countermoves by other actors.

To address the problem of competitive recursion, a growing number of startups are using causal AI tools to analyze the impact of interventions in the decision environment. Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and other causal AI tools enable startups to infer the future consequences of different possible decisions. CausaLens is developing a suite of causal AI tools for multiple industries that allow users to apply a combination of machine learning and causal inference tools (Pearl, 2018) to analyze a variety of decisions before they are implemented. In one particular use case, the startup has built a suite of causal AI tools to allow hedge funds and other financial entities to analyze the future impact of different trading strategies on asset prices. The startup recently reported that the use of their causal AI tools allowed client firms to accurately forecast foreign currency exchange prices 24 hours in advance of trading. Compare CausaLens’s success with the failure of Sentient Investment Management, one of the first ‘automated’ hedge funds, which used an ensemble of sophisticated AI genetic/evolutionary algorithms to manage the startup’s portfolio of investments. Despite the startup’s seemingly sophisticated use of AI, the investment strategy implemented by the AI failed to account for the recursive impact of its own trading activity on asset prices (Byrum, 2022).

In a competitive marketplace, the failure to account for the recursive impact of choices to intervene or not in decision environments amplifies the problem of competitive recursion. Although causal AI tools can help address some facets of the challenges of addressing these problems, causal AI is still limited when trying to infer the consequences of novel and unexpected actions. Pearl (2018) acknowledges the limitations of these tools, noting that causal AI requires information about possible counterfactuals in order to infer the consequences of different actions. Entrepreneurs face a variety of decisions regarding the feasibility and desirability of intervention tasks (i.e., should we enter this market or another? Which new product should we develop? How

will our customers respond to new products we are developing?). These challenges culminate in the problems entrepreneurs face in knowing how collective actions and choices of an unknowable set of competitors, both now and in the future, will impact the dynamics of any marketplace.

As Wolfram (2023) recently noted, AI systems are locked in an ongoing race with the systems they are attempting to navigate and predict. These challenges are particularly relevant in fast-changing, dynamic marketplaces. Competitors will respond, sometimes in unexpected ways, thereby transforming what was an already complicated situation into an unknowable one in the future. AI systems offer useful tools for addressing these problems, but the culmination of the four problems of Knightian uncertainty – actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion – creates clear boundaries on the use of AI entrepreneurship. In the following section, we build on these insights to identify important research opportunities at the intersection of AI and entrepreneurship.

##### DISCUSSION & FUTURE RESEARCH

The purpose of this study is to bridge existing research in the fields of entrepreneurship and artificial intelligence research to explore the limits of AI in the business venturing process (Chalmers et al., 2020; Lévesque et al., 2022; Obschonka & Audretsch, 2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). Our central premise is that the problems of Knightian uncertainty – actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion – create fundamental boundary conditions on the predictive capabilities of AI in entrepreneurship (Arikan, Arikan, & Koparan, 2020; Foss & Klein, 2012; McMullen & Shepherd, 2006; Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Sarasvathy, 2001; Townsend et al., 2018; Townsend & Hunt, 2019; Venkataraman, 1997). We ground the logic of our theoretical approach by examining the benefits and limitations of four foundational and emerging types of AI – logical AI, machine learning,

generative AI, and causal AI – to tackle key entrepreneurial tasks such as identifying target market segments or developing novel products and services through computational means. To illustrate our analytical approach, we highlight contemporary examples of how startups in a variety of industries are using AI to tackle these entrepreneurial tasks. Based on this approach, our analysis indicates that while both foundational and emerging types of AI provide important task performance advantages in entrepreneurship, AI systems are still subject to the problems of Knightian uncertainty.

#### Knightian Uncertainty & the Boundaries of AI in Entrepreneurship

The first contribution of our study emerges from our theorization concerning how and why the problems of Knightian uncertainty create fundamental boundary conditions on the predictive capabilities of AI in entrepreneurship. While each of these problems creates important obstacles for AI systems, a key opportunity for future research is to explore and analyze the nature of the paradoxical relationship between the knowledge (i.e., actor ignorance and practical indeterminism) and action (i.e., agentic novelty and competitive recursion) problems at the heart of Knightian theory. Future research will undoubtedly identify a growing number of areas where AI tools are used to solve important knowledge problems. The recent success of Deep Mind's AlphaFold AI in solving the protein folding problem – one of the ‘grand challenges’ of biology research and an important area in drug discovery) foreshadows these types of future breakthroughs.

However, the problems of Knightian uncertainty are not simply knowledge problems. Although knowledge (or perhaps ‘unknowledge’ – McVea & Dew, 2021) problems play an important role, when entrepreneurs act in novel ways through acts of ‘economic pioneering’ and ‘willful departures’ from the status quo in the pursuit of novel profit-generating opportunities, new possibilities are introduced into the decision environment. Emerging types of generative AI will

play an important role in assisting and complementing these efforts, but the role of agentic novelty in generating new possibilities involves more than just the random or stochastic ‘remixing’ of existing data and information. Imagined futures (Ramoglou & McMullen, 2022), entrepreneurial vision (Thiel & Masters, 2014), and profound beliefs (Blank, 2023a) all play an important role in entrepreneurial pioneering by human actors. Human entrepreneurs can imagine and pursue alternative futures (e.g., Kier & McMullen, 2018), but existing AI tools are not motivated by a vision of the future. These systems lack the semantic awareness and understanding (Bishop, 2021) and the ‘determined optimism’ (Thiel & Masters, 2014) to see the process of turning new possibilities into viable opportunities (Ramoglou & McMullen, 2022).

Yet, even without these agentic capabilities, generative AI systems can play a useful role in combination with human entrepreneurs to generate creative, new product and service ideas (Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019). At the same time, as the capabilities of these AI systems continue to grow and the systems are granted more autonomy, understanding the fundamental limitations of the predictive capabilities of these systems will help better inform organizational design choices. One important question for future research would be to explore important distinctions among different approaches to ‘automation’ and ‘iteration’ in designing AI task environments. For startups like Insilico Medicine, the use of AI tools speeds up the process of drug discovery by automating the review and analysis of a vast amount of scientific research through natural language processing models. These insights can then be tested and validated through a series of iterative steps involving automated hypothesis testing and validation.

Competitive environments create a different set of challenges for designing AI task environments. Although the growing use of game theoretic modeling (Qi, 2019) and generative adversarial networks (Creswell et al., 2018) in AI systems provide useful computational

approaches to automate decisions in these environments, the ongoing iterative sequences of moves and countermoves among competitors amplify the problems startups face in predicting the consequences of their competitive actions. These problems will be further exacerbated by the extent to which AI systems pursue novel strategies in pursuit of competitive advantages. Recent studies in the AI literature continue to document a growing number of emergent strategic capabilities and achievements of these AI systems (e.g., Makridakis, 2017; Davenport, 2018; Kemp, 2023). However, even as these increasingly sophisticated and powerful AI systems are deployed in these decision environments, future researchers will likely find that, in many cases, the growing influence of AI systems in various decision environments will likely decrease the predictability of these systems. Temporary advantages will emerge, but as long as the problems of competitive recursion remain unsolved, ongoing iterative cycles of the competitive process will continue to limit the predictive capabilities of these AI systems. Overall, a key premise of our theorizing thus asserts that no matter how sophisticated these systems might become, AI systems will still be subject to the paradoxical limits on predicting future environments outlined in Knightian theory.

### Theorizing the Paradoxical Problems of Knightian Uncertainty in Entrepreneurship

The second contribution of our study builds on both foundational and contemporary research to clarify the meaning and role of Knightian uncertainty in entrepreneurship research. Contemporary scholars have debated the meaning of Knightian uncertainty in recent studies that draw in part upon the logic of Knight's theory (1921) to identify important dimensions of the construct. What is less clear in these studies is why these decision environments are unpredictable in the first place? In this study, we answer this question by analyzing and discussing important dimensions of Knight's (1921) original theory that are often ignored in contemporary research.

Towards this end, we elaborate on and contrast three different types of probability judgments to clarify important dimensions of Knightian theory on the nature of ‘true unmeasurable uncertainty,’ which, as we note, refers to an entrepreneur’s inability to know if something is possible or impossible in the future. Consistent with this view, we also clarify the connection between Knightian uncertainty and contemporary approaches to theorizing modal uncertainty, which researchers define as “uncertainty about what is possible or what could be the case…(where the decision-maker is) unsure as to what the possible states of the world are or what possible consequences could follow from the choice of an action” (Bradley & Drechsler, 2014: 1229).

For the field of entrepreneurship, we analyze how the paradoxical relationship between knowledge and action problems in Knightian theory is the source of this unique type of uncertainty (Knight, 1921; 1942). Knight's theory (1921) emphasizes the use of disjunctive reasoning (i.e., how will the future be different from the past and present?) and highlights the problem that true uncertainty is not solvable through inductive methods because of the range of different future possibilities that is always in flux. Through entrepreneurial action, the indeterminism of business environments is shaped in fundamentally unexpected ways by the human values, preferences, and expectations that pervade entrepreneurial decision environments (Townsend et al., 2018). AI systems can augment and enhance these processes by helping entrepreneurs to imagine "what is possible," but the limitations of these systems to address the problems of Knightian uncertainty create boundaries on the use of AI in entrepreneurship.

Exploring the nature and impact of the paradoxical relationship between knowledge and action problems in future research will illuminate a broad set of new insights for entrepreneurship theory. Existing research on knowledge problems in entrepreneurship emphasizes both the agentic and structural dimensions of these problems (Townsend et al., 2018). At the same time, this study

expands on knowledge problems research to differentiate between knowledge and action problems in entrepreneurship, but in a way that preserves both the agentic and structural dimensions of these problems and highlights the fundamental paradoxical relationship. At the core of these problems of Knightian uncertainty is the fundamental paradox of future unknowability (Rescher, 2009). Future research exploring the impact of these paradoxical problems of Knightian uncertainty, accounting for both the agentic and structural dimensions of these problems will provide new fundamental insights to shape future entrepreneurship theory development. Future analyses of this paradox through the lens of paradox theory (e.g., Epstein & Wang, 2004; Sarin & Wakker, 1998) will identify important additional dimensions to the problems that have not been explored or discussed in existing research. From AI research, entrepreneurship scholars are likely to benefit from the extension and analysis of existing research in robotics research on hybrid forms of 'knowledge-producing actions' to address the problems of uncertainty (e.g., Thrun, 2002; Levesque & Lakemeyer, 2008; Beetz et al., 2018). In addition, existing theories of entrepreneurial action, along with complementary new theoretical perspectives on entrepreneurial work (e.g., Ramoglou & McMullen, 2022), are likely to benefit directly from ongoing analyses of the paradoxical relationships between knowledge and action at the core of Knightian theory. Overall, given the fundamental role of Knightian uncertainty in entrepreneurship theory, opportunities abound to expand upon and explore the role of these paradoxical problems in future research.

### Analyzing The Possibilities of Machine Agency & Agentic AI in Entrepreneurship

Given the extent to which the problems of Knightian uncertainty create boundaries on the predictive capabilities and task performance of AI in entrepreneurship, the third contribution of our study addresses the possibilities (or impossibilities) of machine agency or agentic AI (Abedin,

Meske, Junglas, Rabhi, Motahari-Nezhad, 2022; Murray et al., 2021). As we discuss in the study, AI systems currently lack the capacity for developing complete semantic awareness and enacting agentic control (Bishop, 2021). AI researchers are aware of these limitations (Mitchell, 2021). Current AI research notes that these AI systems operate with what is at best described as an 'apparent agency' where actions are enacted based on objectives specified by human developers (Sundar, 2020; Takayama, 2015). Moreover, despite the appearance of apparent agency, existing AI systems "still lack intentional pull, with which to drive genuinely autonomous teleological behavior" (Bishop, 2021).

The lack of intentional pull in existing AI systems generates several important disadvantages for these systems in entrepreneurship. Human entrepreneurs are subject to the same limitations on prediction as AI systems in the face of the problems of Knightian uncertainty (Sarasvathy, 2001). Given these limitations, instead of continuing to try to predict unknowable future decision environments, prior research has explored how entrepreneurs use a variety of agentic strategies, including shaping strategies (Pontikes & Rindova, 2020; Rindova & Courtney, 2020; Rindova & Martins, 2021), ‘worldmaking’ strategies (Sarasvathy, 2021; Sarasvathy, Dew, Read, & Wiltbank, 2008), or design strategies (Sergeeva, Bhardwaj, & Mansoori, 2022) to influence the decision environment in ways that are favorable to these entrepreneurs. The common objective motivating these approaches is built on the logic that instead of trying to predict unknowable future environments, entrepreneurs should strive to shape them (Sarasvathy, 2021; McMullen & Ramoglou, 2022).

Existing AI systems lack the ability to enact these types of strategies (Bishop, 2021). Although there are numerous scholars who believe that building true agentic capabilities in AI systems is an impossible task, others argue that building such capabilities is entirely possible.

Recent advancements in multi-agent systems suggest there are important new insights to be gained from exploring alternative computational approaches (e.g., Parker et al., 2003; Tesauro et al., 2004). In a recent study, Open AI researchers reported the results of an experiment where a group of self-supervised agents developed a set of six emergent strategies that were not pre-programmed by the research team (Baker et al., 2020). In several cases, these emergent strategies involved the manipulation of the local environment by agents to create more advantageous conditions. Although there are clear differences between artificial and real-world competitive environments, these studies suggest that multi-agent systems, among other approaches, might help train future AI systems to develop an array of agentic capabilities (McAurther et al., 2007). Overall, while significant questions remain regarding the possibilities of machine agency and agentic AI in entrepreneurship, exploring these possibilities in future research will raise new questions regarding the range and types of agentic strategies AI systems might develop in the future to address the problems of Knightian uncertainty.

##### Limitations & Directions for Future Research

As is the case with all novel theoretical approaches, ours is grounded in a set of assumptions, each of which we have sought to make clear from the outset. These assumptions – including the efficacy of Knight’s (1921) theorization of uncertainty and entrepreneurship – undergird a useful, novel platform for future study of the role, impact, and limitations of AI in the business venturing process. Like all assumptions, these predictions can and should be stress-tested against conditions arising in diverse empirical contexts. Our discussion of specific contemporary examples of both startup successes and failures is intended to lay a roadmap for future empirical studies to test the logic of our approach.

Second, although our study examines central questions concerning the relative task performance advantages of AI in entrepreneurship, emerging research on the role and impact of AI in organizations addresses some of the design challenges managers face in determining more effective configurations of human actors and AI tools in organizations (Raisch & Krakowski, 2020; Murray et al., 2021). Many of the tensions we raise and address in our theorization are reflected in a series of interrelated paradoxes between AI automation versus human-centric technology augmentation (Raisch & Krakowski, 2020; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022). Our discussion of failed startup examples illustrates the importance of the augmentation perspective. When entrepreneurs overestimate the capabilities of AI to address key problems of Knightian uncertainty in the startup process, the venture increases its likelihood of failing. By deepening our understanding of these problems, our research confirms the importance of alternative arrangements of human-machine networks where the capabilities of AI and entrepreneurs are designed in a way that maximizes the strengths and offsets the weaknesses of both types of actors (i.e., Chalmers et al., 2020; Puranam, 2021; Shepherd & Majchrzak, 2022; Townsend & Hunt, 2019).

Lastly, understanding why and how both humans and AI might intervene in processes of entrepreneurial action highlights the importance of developing a new theory to explore questions related to AI governance. Zillow’s failure to automate the home buying process using AI raises important questions about the role of human judgment and AI governance. In some cases, entrepreneur judgment provides the ‘stopping rules’ that allow new ventures to alter course in the face of Knightian uncertainty (Hunt, 2013). In other cases, however, AI systems might provide the ‘stopping rules’ to correct or illuminate human bias in their decision-making and choices (Murray et al., 2021). Future research exploring these questions will raise important, new insights into the

contingencies that shape the task performance advantages of AI and human entrepreneurs in processes of entrepreneurial action.

## Conclusion

With sixty-four spaces, thirty-two pieces, and a tightly prescribed set of movements, the game of chess contains  $ 10^{46} $ possible strategic positions. Though inconceivably large relative to the limited computational capacity of humans, even chess’s computational landscape is modest when compared to the  $ 10^{360} $ possible positions in Go. It is, therefore, a remarkable testament that AI systems have successfully conquered these computational tasks that were once thought to be impossible. At the same time, as Russell and Norvig (2016: 9) note, “the (real) world is an extremely large problem instance” that surpasses the computational power and capabilities of existing systems of AI. In the context of entrepreneurship, the business venturing process fuels a never-ending process of generating novel solutions to emerging problems that virtually guarantees that the futures which emerge will often deviate in important and unknowable ways from the present (Knight, 1921). Empowered by cutting-edge intelligent machines, the use of AI in entrepreneurship equips entrepreneurs with useful tools to address important problems of uncertainty throughout the business venturing process. And yet, the problems of Knightian uncertainty – reflected in the inability of entrepreneurs to determine what will be possible in the future – stand unique in the field of entrepreneurship, creating a dynamic boundary on the frontiers of AI in the field of entrepreneurship. In these situations, the effective design of new ventures requires a clear delineation of the problems of Knightian uncertainty to ensure new ventures can operate successfully to address the challenges of computing possible futures.
