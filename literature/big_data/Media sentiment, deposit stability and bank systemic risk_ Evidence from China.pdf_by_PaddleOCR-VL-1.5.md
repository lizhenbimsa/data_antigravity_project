## ABSTRACT

Using textual analysis techniques, we construct the unique bank-level media sentiment index in China with the data of news narratives to examine its driving effect on bank systemic risk with a distinction between systemic linkage and bank tail risk. We find consistent and robust evidence that negative media sentiment tends to increase market-data-based systemic risk measures. The withdrawal of deposits due to the negative media sentiment forces banks to raise interbank lending, which increases the cost of their liability and encourages them to engage in risky and similar business, in hence enlarging both bank tail risk and systemic linkage. We also find that the negative media sentiment of banks increases systemic risk, mainly by improving system linkage in the early stage, but mainly by improving bank tail risk in the later stage.

## 1. Introduction

Early studies, such as classic Diamond and Dybvig (1983), have created a research paradigm of how depositor emotional panic leads to bank run, which emphasizes the significant role of negative sentiment on bank deposit behaviors. Allen and Gale (1994) attributes depositors' panic to the fact that depositors receive negative information about the returns of depository banks' portfolios or suffer external liquidity shocks under the condition of information asymmetry. Subsequent researches discuss the systemic crisis in the banking sector from the perspective of the rapid contagion of depositor sentiment among banks and many papers conclude that the introduction of deposit insurance system can solve this systemic risk problem, i.e., the bank run issue, to a large extent. Some studies show that the introduction of deposit insurance system can effectively alleviate the fragility of the banking system (Anginer et al., 2014; Diamond and Dybvig, 1983), and the expectation of bank protection in deposit insurance system can reduce risk contagion (Dell'Ariccia and Ratnovski, 2019). Liu et al. (2016) also proposes that the "risk minimization" mode of deposit insurance system is conducive to reducing bank moral hazard.

However, things can be a little different in China. The stylized fact is that depositors may feel their bank deposits are less safer after the implementation of the deposit insurance system. Before 2015, China's banking industry generally practiced rigid redemption, i.e., depositors believed that their investment in the bank was fully guaranteed by the government's credit. Once a bank goes bankruptcy, depositors could at least get all their principal back. Whereas, with the advancement of interest rate liberalization reform in China, banks gradually abandon the implicit promise of rigid redemption, and the new deposit insurance only protects depositors' principal

up to half a million RMB. Compared with the process from "completely unprotected" to "partially protected" for foreign depositors, China's deposit insurance system transforms the situation of depositors' funds from "completely safe" to "relatively safe", increasing the depositors' expectation of insecurity.

Another important stylized fact upon media sentiment in China is also worth noting. In such government-oriented economies as China, media coverage on banks usually tends to take a relatively positive tone of narrative but fluctuates wildly in certain periods along with the reputational risk, which can be seen in Fig. 2 in Section 3. In fact, compared with the familiar positive reports, a large-scale increase in negative media coverage of banks will lead to a more sensitive psychological shock on depositors. Some studies, such as Iyer and Puri (2012) using account-level data or Anastasiou and Drakos (2021) and Anastasiou and Petralias (2021) using country-level data, have already shown that negative sentiment can still result in large-scale withdrawals even under the deposit insurance system. However, previous studies have not clearly analyzed this situation where media sentiment towards banks is not so negative as to trigger a bank run but it threatens the stability of bank deposits to such a degree that depositors lose confidence in problem banks and transfer their deposits to another bank, affecting the accumulation of bank systemic risks.

In this paper, we attempt to investigate on how media sentiment affects bank systemic risk in medium or long term. That is to say, we mainly focus on the behaviors of depositors affected by negative bank news, who transfer deposits out of the bank and not transfer deposits back even after the negative news ends, leaving an influence in medium or long term. Specifically, we mainly conduct the following three works. First, we construct our unique bank-level media sentiment index by using more than 900,000 news articles in INFOBANK economic news database in China with textual analysis techniques.

Second, we employ panel regressions with quarterly data to examine how the decline in deposit stability caused by negative media sentiment is transmitted to bank systemic risk through financial behaviors? Using frontier systemic risk decomposition methodology presented in Van Oordt and Zhou (2019), we confirm that negative media sentiment increases bank systemic risk in the year ahead. We also find the evidence that those systemic risks are oriented from the change of bank liability structure. The withdrawal due to the negative media sentiment forces banks to raise inter-bank lending, which increases the cost of their liability and encourages them to engage in risky and similar business, in hence enlarging both bank tail risk and systemic linkage.

Third, we additionally use monthly data of media sentiment and bank systemic risk indicator as well as its two sub-components to illustrate how bank tail risk and systemic linkage interact in dynamics. Interestingly, we find that in the initial period after the negative shock of media sentiment, the systemic linkage increases significantly while bank tail risk changes insignificantly. However, in the latter period, bank tail risk gradually becomes the dominant factor of systemic risk.

The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, we refine relevant studies on how media sentiment affects bank systemic risk. Previous literature has mainly focused on the impact of media coverage or sentiment on asset pricing (Anand et al., 2021; Calomiris and Mamaysky, 2019; Garcia, 2013; Huang and Zhang, 2022; Soo, 2018; Tetlock, 2007; Zhu et al., 2017). And the existing papers studying the impact on bank systemic risk mostly use systemic risk measures only based on bank stock or CDS market data (González-Fernández and González-Velasco, 2020; Ho et al., 2020; Irresberger et al., 2015; Smales, 2016). Besides, when it comes to the impact of sentiment, many papers pay attention to investors' perception and its outcome on financial performance. Different from all above studies, this paper attempts to demonstrate how perception caused by media sentiment changes the reality of bank balance sheet behaviors and in hence systemic risk. $ ^{1} $

Second, we add to the stream of literature on bank systemic risk. On one hand, we explore the mechanism on how the loss of liability stability under the external negative shock is transmitted into bank tail risk and systemic linkage. We empirically confirm that negative sentiment will not only affect the bank's own risk taking but also significantly increase the interconnectedness among banks, and the latter is often considered more important in systemic risk field than the former. On the other hand, we empirically test whether interconnectedness among banks rises in the risk accumulation period but collapses when individual risk exposure actually realizes.

The rest of paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 provides the theoretical analysis on how media sentiment affects bank systemic risk. Section 3 introduces the description of our data and main variables. Section 4 introduces our model and econometric methodology. Section 5 presents the estimates of our baseline model with a series of robustness checks. Section 6 extends our research by detecting the balance-sheet mechanism on how media sentiment influences bank systemic risk. Section 7 further discusses the dynamics of the results. Finally, Section 8 concludes.

Contagion or amplification mechanism, related to the interconnectedness among banks, mainly stands from the macro-prudential perspective. Banks with stronger linkage with the system are often more sensitive to extreme systemic shocks, and thus systemically riskier (Davydov et al., 2021).

In this paper, we use Van Oordt and Zhou (2019)'s methodology to decompose systemic risk into two sub-components: bank tail risk and systemic linkage. The former represents the volatility of bank stock returns under extreme shocks, while the latter represents the correlation between the individual bank return and the banking system return in tail-distribution. $ ^{2} $

wnat's more, we relate the market-data-based bank tail risk with bank balance sheet risk-taking behaviors and relate the market-data-based systemic linkage with two types of bank interconnectedness, namely direct linkage and indirect linkage. $ ^{3} $ The direct linkage is mainly reflected in the interbank lending network. In the direct-connectedness network, if a bank is at risk, its counterparties will face high credit risk and liquidity risk as well due to repayment difficulties (Acemoglu et al., 2015; Allen and Gale, 2000; Upper, 2011). Indirect linkage is mainly reflected in the network formed by banks holding common or similar assets. In indirect-connectedness network, even though there is no direct business relationship between any two banks, the risk of one bank may still be transmitted to another bank through the price fall in asset fire-sale (Duarte and Eisenbach, 2021; Greenwood et al., 2015; Shleifer and Vishny, 2010).

Then, the question becomes how negative media sentiment affects bank balance-sheet behaviors step by step and how it is finally reflected in systemic risk on the bank stock market. The starting point is on the liability side. As described in the Introduction section and supported by previous studies (Anastasiou and Drakos, 2021; Anastasiou and Petralias, 2021; Iyer and Puri, 2012), negative media sentiment will change the perception of depositors, resulting in current withdrawals or future bank deposit inflows' reduction, which can weaken the stability of bank liabilities. In this case, problem banks will have more difficulties in obtaining and rolling over new deposits in the future, so they have to increase the proportion of interbank debt financing and thus enhance direct interconnectedness as well as bank systemic linkage in the market. $ ^{4} $

Next, structural adjustment on the liability side will be transmitted to the asset side. On one hand, since the interest expense of interbank liabilities is generally higher than that of deposit liabilities, the financing cost of total debt has risen with the passive increase in the ratio of interbank business. This will force banks to actively adjust their risk structure on the asset part to obtain higher returns, so as to match the cost pressure on the liability side. As a result, banks engage in more risk-taking activities and hence induce instability (González-Fernández and González-Velasco, 2020; Smales, 2016). Therefore, we propose negative media sentiment will increase bank risk taking behaviors and thus enhance bank tail risk in the market.

On the other hand, negative media sentiment will increase bank indirect linkage and hence systemic linkage in threefold. Firstly, motivated by the pursuit of higher yields or maintaining higher liquidity on the asset side, banks will hold more bonds and inter-bank asset investments. Compared to loans, such assets are traded in a standardized way in financial markets. When banks sell assets in the face of external shocks, they magnify the extent of losses across the system through indirect linkages. Secondly, for the sake of maintaining reputation, bank managers tend to follow the investment decisions of other banks, so that they can shirk responsibility to systemic factors and avoid punishment. Therefore, the increase in negative media sentiment of banks can trigger bank herding behaviors (Acharya and Yorulmazer, 2008; Benoit et al., 2017; Fan et al., 2021), which will lead to more homogeneous business with more similarity on asset or liabilities (Smales, 2016; Nifoi and Pochea, 2022). Thirdly, in negative sentiment, banks have the incentive

to diversify risk. The more dispersed the asset allocation is behaved, the more similar the asset structure shows.

## 3. Data and variables

We use bank-level panel data of 36 listed banks in China with quarterly observations during the period of 2007Q1-2021Q2., $ ^{56} $ By the end of 2021Q2, the total assets (liabilities) of the sample banks accounted for 65.18 % (65.66 %) of the total assets (liabilities) of China's banking financial institutions $ ^{7} $ so the sample selected in this paper has a good representativeness.

The core explanatory variable in this paper, bank sentiment index, is constructed by ourselves, using Python crawler technology and textual analysis method based on INFOBANK economic news database. Other variables such as bank market data to measure systemic risk, bank balance sheet data and macroeconomic conditions are all collected from WIND database. We interpolate a few missing bank characteristics and winsorize variables that lie beyond the 99th percentile and below the 1st percentile of their distribution in order to prevent the interference of outliers.

### 3.1. Bank media sentiment

Media sentiment will affect investors' perception of bank risk. In order to reflect the uncertainty of news media on banking business operation and risk management, and test whether and how the impact of this media information on public perception affects the bank systemic risk, we construct a new and modified bank media sentiment index. The construction process mainly includes three parts: the selection and crawling of news corpus, textual sentiment analysis and finally the index compilation.

### Step1: Database selection and news crawling

We select INFOBANK economic news database as our corpus source of bank sentiment analysis. This data source includes news reports on websites of more than 1500 medias around the world, as well as textual information such as comprehensive newspapers and financial newspapers. On the one hand, the high coverage on China's economic and financial news of this database assures that our bank sentiment index well describes the real emotional environment of banking sector in China. On the other hand, the daily updated database provides researchers with high-frequency and massive news sources, which well accommodates our demand of constructing a comprehensive and real-time sentiment indicator from news, for monitoring and early warning systemic risk in the future.

We select news reports from 2007Q1 (January 1st, 2007) to 2021Q2 (June 30th, 2021) in the "China economic news database" section of INFOBANK database, filtering by the occurrence of sample banks. For each news text, we use Python web crawler technology from Selenium library to simulate the browser behavior, capture the key information such as news title, content and date, and construct the original news corpus of this paper.

### Step2: Textual sentiment analysis

This paper selects the Chinese financial textual sentiment dictionary constructed by Jiang et al. (2019) to apply to Chinese news data. This dictionary is mainly constructed by translation and transformation method, by combing and expanding well-known Chinese and English financial sentiment dictionaries such as LM Dictionary (Loughran and McDonald, 2011), Hownet Sentiment Dictionary, Li Jun's Compendatory etc. Through translation, word2VEC algorithm processing and manual screening, it finally obtains 5890 negative words and 3338 positive words.

Compared with previous textual sentiment analysis research, we conduct a more detailed recognition and processing of positive and negative words in news texts, which is mainly reflected in the following two aspects. (1) This paper manages to analyze the textual sentiment in sentence units. Regular expression method in Python language is used here to narrow the scope of sentiment analysis to the sentences where our sample banks are located. (2) This paper has fully considered the twisting effect of negators in text emotional expression. Take an English example for illustration, the sentence "I am not happy" has a negator "not", which completely reverses the positive emotion expressed by the word "happy". To solve this problem, we decompose texts into sentences and further decompose each sentence into word unit. Next, for each sentence, we extract positive words, negative words and negators (including negative prefix). Then, within each sentence, we combine the negators and the emotional word (positive or negative word) right after it into an "emotional unit" and reverse the emotional direction expressed by the original one immediately at the same time.

### Step3: Bank sentiment index construction

The Sentiment index in this paper is calculated based on the “Relative Sentiment Shifts” principle and modified as Eq. (1) listed below.

 $$ S e n t i_{k}=\frac{n(p o s i t i v e)_{k}-n(n e g a t i v e)_{k}}{n(p o s i t i v e)_{k}+n(n e g a t i v e)_{k}} $$ 

By measuring the relative sentiment of the crawled news text, the sentiment index  $ Sent_{k} $ of the kth news can be obtained, where  $ n(\text{negative}_k) $ is the number of negative units in the kth news and  $ n(\text{positive}_k) $ is the number of positive units in the kth news. Note that, since the overall sentiment of a news text may not necessarily represent the sentiment of the bank that appears in it, we further narrow the scope of textual sentiment analysis to the sentence where the bank keyword is located. By recognizing all emotional units with sample bank occurrence, in each sentence we calculate a sentiment index and finally take the average value as the sentiment index of the current news report.

 $$ S e n t i_{i,t}=\frac{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}S e n t i_{i,t,k}}{K} $$ 

Eq. (2) shows the construction of bank sentiment index, where K represents the total number of news reports that bank i appeared in month  $ t $. To calculate  $ Sent_{it} $, we first figure out all the news reports with bank i's occurrence in month t and then take average of those sentiment indexes. If a bank doesn't appear in any news that month, the bank sentiment index  $ Sent_{it} $ will be null. The smaller the index, the stronger negative sentiment are held towards the bank that month. $ ^{9} $

In order to clearly and intuitively show the performance of the bank sentiment index constructed in this paper, we depict the changes of bank sentiment over time in Fig. 2. We can find that media coverage on banks usually tends to take a relatively positive tone of narrative but with volatile in some certain periods along with the reputational risk.

### 3.2. Bank systemic risk and its sub-components

We gauge the bank systemic risk in line with Van Oordt and Zhou (2019), which captures a bank's sensitivity to severe shocks in the financial system, and further use their decomposition method to obtain two sub-components: systematic linkage and bank tail risk. Specifically, when the financial system encounters extreme negative shocks, consider the linear tail relationship between the individual institution and the financial system's stock returns as follows:

 $$ R_{i}=\beta_{i}^{T}R_{s}+\varepsilon_{i},for R_{s}<-VaR(\overline{p}) $$ 

where  $ R_i $ and  $ R_s $ denote the stock return of bank  $ i $ and the return on an equity investment in the financial system,  $ VaR(\bar{p}) $ is the “value-at-risk” of an equity investment in the financial system, which is defined as the loss on a unit investment that is exceeded with some small probability  $ \bar{p} $,  $ \varepsilon_i $ represents the shocks from other sources which are assumed to be independent of the shocks in the financial system.  $ \beta_i^T $ in Eq. (3) corresponds to systemic risk at the individual bank level applied in this paper and a higher  $ \beta_i^T $ indicates that bank  $ i $ would suffer larger capital losses during periods of extreme market turmoil. To circumvent the obvious small sample problems in the extreme events, we instead utilize Extreme Value Theory (EVT) to estimate  $ \beta_i^T $. Let  $ R_i $ and  $ R_s $ follow heavy-tailed distributions with tail indices  $ \zeta_i $ and  $ \zeta_s $, respectively. Formally,  $ \beta_i^T $ can be expressed as:

 $$ \beta_{i}^{T}=\lim_{p\rightarrow0}\tau_{i}(\rho)^{1/\zeta_{s}}\frac{VaR_{i}(\overline{p})}{VaR_{s}(\overline{p})} $$ 

where  $ VaR_i(\bar{p}) $ and  $ VaR_s(\bar{p}) $ are the value-at-risks of  $ R_i $ and  $ R_s $ with probability level  $ \bar{p} $.  $ \tau_i(\rho) $ is the level of tail dependence between  $ R_i $ and  $ R_s $.

Van Oordt and Zhou (2019) note that the parameters in Eq. (4) can be estimated by applying EVT in a heavy-tailed environment, where the estimator  $ \beta_i^T $ can be thus given by combining the estimators of its components, as follows. With  $ n $ observations on the pair  $ (R_i, R_s) $, we consider the tail region as the  $ k $ worst observations. The coefficient  $ \beta_i^T $ is then estimated by:

In the empirical analysis, the choice of a low  $ k $ will lead to large estimation uncertainty, while a high  $ k $ may cause estimation bias. $ ^{10} $ For the estimation window of the daily stock return in one year, we use the parameter level of  $ k = 30 $ or  $ k = 50 $ to measure the systemic risk level of the sample banks. $ ^{11} $

As can be seen in Eq. (5), systemic risk (SR) of individual banks  $ \beta_i^T $ consists of two sub-components. The first component  $ \beta_i^T $ measures the systemic linkage (SL) of individual banks to severe adverse shocks in the financial system. This component can be interpreted from the macro-prudential supervision perspective that for banks with the same level of stand-alone tailed risk, those banks that are more sensitive to extreme systemic shocks are systemically riskier. The second component  $ \frac{\widehat{VaR}_i(k/n)}{VaR_i(k/n)} $ measures the level of bank-specific tail risk (IR). This component can be interpreted from the micro-prudential perspective that for banks with the same sensitivity to severe shocks in the financial system, those banks that have a higher level of tail risk are systemically riskier.

By taking the logarithm of Eq. (5), we obtain the following linear additive relationship between systemic risk, systemic linkage, and bank tail risk.

 $$ \log\left(\beta_{i}^{T}\right)=\log(Systemic Linkage)+\log(Bank Tail Risk) $$ 

For convenience, we denote the  $ \log(\beta_i^T) $,  $ \log(\text{Systemic Linkage}) $ and  $ \log(\text{Bank Tail Risk}) $ as  $ \ln SR $,  $ \ln SL $,  $ \ln IR $, respectively. We estimate these three variables for each bank and each month (quarter) by using one year of daily stock return data with an annually backward rolling estimation window. $ ^{12} $ The rolling risk result reflects the risk status of the bank in the next year, which can be used as an indicator for future risk warning and prevention.

### 3.3. Control variables

We control two categories of factors that are considered to have potentially confounding effects on bank systemic risk, namely, bank characteristics and macroeconomic conditions. Among the bank characteristics, we employ the following set of control variables according to previous studies (De Jonghe et al., 2015; Van Oordt and Zhou, 2019; Zhu et al., 2019; Davydov et al., 2021): (1) Size is measured as the natural logarithm of total assets; (2) Leverage is the ratio of equity capital of total assets; (3) Profitability is measured as the ratio of total assets to total assets.

with the return on assets (ROA) which is calculated as the ratio of net income to total assets; (4) Liquidity is the ratio of liquid assets to total assets; (5) Inefficiency is measured as the ratio of operating cost to total operating revenue; (6) Income diversification is calculated as the ratio of non-interest income to total operating revenue. (7) Loan ratio is calculated as the ratio of total bank loans to total assets. (8) Book to market is calculated as the ratio of market value to total equity.

In terms of macroeconomic conditions, we first adopt two variables to control the risk effect of business cycle, namely, the growth rate of real GDP and the inflation rate. The GDP growth indicates the position of the economy in the business cycle, whereas inflation is an indicator of macroeconomic imbalances. We next include the potential impact of monetary policy on banks' systemic risk (Adrian and Shin, 2010; Shin and Shin, 2011). We use the growth rate of M2 as a measurement of changes in monetary policy. A larger (lower) value implies an expansionary (contractionary) policy innovation.

## 4. Model

Our baseline econometric model is specified as follows:

 $$ Risk_{i,[t,t+3]}=\alpha+\gamma Senti_{i,t}+\lambda Char_{i,t-1}+\theta Macro_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{i,t} $$ 

where the dependent variable,  $ Risk_{i,[t,t+3]} $ is the indicator of banks' systemic risk and its two sub-components, i.e., SR, SL and IR, respectively in the following one year.  $ Senti_{i,t} $ is our self-constructed time-series measurement of media sentiment on bank i in quarter t. The larger the  $ Senti_{i,t} $, the stronger negative sentiment is held.  $ Char_{i,t-1} $ and  $ Macro_{t-1} $ denote, respectively, the vector of bank characteristics and macroeconomic conditions.  $ \varepsilon_{i,t} $ is the idiosyncratic error.  $ \alpha $,  $ \gamma $,  $ \lambda $ and  $ \theta $ are the coefficients to be estimated. We use the one-quarter lagged observations for our macroeconomic conditions and bank characteristics variables to mitigate the problem of endogeneity.

We adopt heteroscedasticity-adjusted robust standard error which has been clustered to bank level in all regressions in order to reduce the problems caused by the irregularity of disturbance terms. It should be noted that referring to Van Oordt and Zhou (2019). We do not consider bank fixed effect. The reason is that, in particular, the systemic linkage is relatively persistent over time. Owing to this persistency, fixed effects will absorb most of variation in the systemic linkage in our data. To check the robustness of our main results, we also employ various alternative econometric methodologies later in the paper.

Third, we replace the systemic risk measures estimated by extreme value theory with the conventional risk measures such as correlation and standard deviations. Assume that the linear relationship  $ R_i = \beta_i R_s + \varepsilon_i $ holds independently of whether  $ R_s $ is extremely negative or not. In this case, estimating  $ \beta_i $ rather than  $ \beta_i^T $ would also provide a good description of the sensitivity of bank  $ i $ to large adverse shocks in the financial system. Applying the ordinary least square method on all observations rather than a few tail observations only gives  $ \widehat{\beta}_i = \widehat{\rho} \times (\widehat{\sigma}_i / \widehat{\sigma}_s) $, where  $ \widehat{\rho} $ is the correlation between  $ R_s $ and  $ R_i $, and  $ \widehat{\sigma}_i / \widehat{\sigma}_s $ is the ratio of their standard deviations. Using  $ \widehat{\rho}_i, \widehat{\rho} $ and  $ \widehat{\sigma}_i / \widehat{\sigma}_s $ as dependent variables, the estimation results are listed in Panel C of Table 4 and their coefficients show the same sign direction and significance with the baseline regression.

Finally, we utilize alternative market-data-based approach to estimate systemic risk at the individual bank level. Specifically, in order to ascertain the robustness of our empirical findings, we use MES indicator estimated in Acharya et al. (2017), which represents the marginal expected loss of bank when the banking system is under stress over a period of time.

 $$ MES_{i}=-E\big(R_{i}\big|R_{s}\leq-VaR_{q}\big) $$ 

We also calculate the SRISK indicator in Brownless and Engle (2017) that reflects the extent of bank capital shortfall of bank. The specific estimation of SRISK is based on the realization of long-term marginal expected loss LRMES, which is approximately equal to  $ 1-\exp(18*MES) $ proposed by Acharya et al. (2017). SRISK of reflecting capital shortfall can be expressed as:

 $$ SRISK_{it}=\max[0,kD_{it}-(1-k)(1-LRMES_{it})MV_{it}] $$ 

where k refers to the minimum capital adequacy ratio, which is set at 8 % according to Basel III. D is the book value of bank debt. MV is the market value of bank equity. We take its natural logarithm during regression.

Besides, we utilize  $ \triangle CoVaR $ referring to Adrian and Brunnermeier (2016), which represents the risk contribution of a single bank to the banking system. The results in Panel D of Table 4 exhibit significantly negative coefficients of bank media sentiment on MES, SRISK and  $ \triangle CoVaR $, reflecting that negative shock to bank media sentiment will increase systemic risk.

#### 5.2.2. Endogeneity analysis

To begin with, since bank fundamentals may be reflected in media sentiment, resulting in endogenous problem, we remove the fundamental factors of banks from the constructed media sentiment index and take the residual as the core explanatory variable in the original benchmark model to conduct the regression again. Fundamental factors include size, leverage, liquid assets to total assets, operating cost to total operating revenue, non-interest income to total operating revenue, loan to assets, book to market ratio as well as the dummy variable of insurance deposit system. $ ^{14} $ We report the estimation results in Panel A of Table 5. The coefficients are negative and statistically significant, which means that negative media sentiment shock to banks leads to higher bank tail risk and systemic linkage and further promote rising systemic risk.

Second, there are endogenous problems such as reverse causality in banking media sentiment and systemic risk, bank tail risk and systemic linkage. Based on Rizwan's (2021) approach, we attempt to use Heckman and Robb (1985)'s two-stage regression, which is as follows:

 $$ Senti_{it}=\alpha_{0}+\beta_{1}\Delta Senti_{it-1}+\beta_{2}Risk_{it-1}+v_{it} $$ 

 $$ Risk_{it}=\alpha_{1}+\varphi_{1}\Delta Senti_{it-1}+\varphi_{2}Controls+\varphi_{3}\widehat{v}_{it-1}+\varepsilon_{it} $$ 

where  $ \widehat{v}_{it-1} $ is the residual fitting value in the first stage of regression. After the addition of  $ \widehat{v}_{it-1} $, the coefficient sign and significance of bank sentiment remain consistent with the benchmark regression in Panel B, Table 5. This means that after controlling the reverse causality problem, the research findings in this paper are still robust and can reflect the impact of Chinese banking media sentiment on banking systemic risk currently.

In line with the calculation of systemic risk indicators above, all following balance-sheet behavior indicators in this section are still calculated by the average of four quarters within one ahead year.

(2021), we calculate the cosine similarity between the asset class of bank n and m in each year within each economy as the dot product of the pair's portfolio weight vectors normalized by the vectors' lengths.

 $$ Similarity_{mnt}=\frac{w_{mt}\cdot w_{nt}}{\left\|\left|w_{mt}\right|\right|\cdot\left\|\left|w_{nt}\right|\right\|} $$ 

where w is bank's vector of weights. Since all weight vectors have non-negative elements. Asset similarity (AS) is closer to 1 when financial structures between banks are more similar and closer to 0 when they are entirely different. Then we calculate the asset similarity at the individual bank level by size-weighted averaging on bank's asset similarity with all others in the economy each year. $ ^{22} $ The weight is calculated as the bank total asset as the share of aggregate banking sector asset in each economy.

On the other hand, followed by Cai et al. (2018), we construct asset interconnectedness (AI) indicator between the bank i and j in each quarter. Distance between two banks is measured by their Euclidean distance on their financial structure.

 $$ D i s t a n c e_{i j t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}*\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(w_{i,k,t}-w_{j,k,t}\right)^{2}} $$ 

where  $ w_{i,k,t} $ represents the ratio of kth asset or liability of bank i to its total assets or total liabilities. The distance measure is normalized to a scale of [0,1] with 0 reflecting no distance (completely matched financial structure) and 1 reflecting full distance (nearly orthogonal financial structure). Then we calculate the asset interconnectedness at the individual bank level by weighted average of all distances between banks. $ ^{23} $

 $$ Interconnectedness_{it}=\left(1-\sum_{i\neq j}\rho_{jt}*Distance_{ijt}\right)*100 $$ 

As expected, the results in Table 10 and Table 11 show that indirect linkage indicators are significantly and negatively correlated with bank media sentiment. Therefore, the negative shock to bank media sentiment will enhance the indirect linkages between banks and then increase systemic linkage. $ ^{24} $

## 7. Further analysis in dynamics

In order to better describe the generation process of bank systemic risk under the negative shocks to bank media sentiment and compare the relative dominance of the two risk subcomponents (bank tail risk and systemic linkage) at different stages, we adopt the prospective regression method here for dynamic analysis. Since higher-frequency data can obtain more sample points in the regression of a given forecast period, thus more conductive to mining and summarizing the dynamic impact laws, monthly data is used in this section instead. The specific form of the regression equation is as follows:

 $$ Risk_{i,t+h}=\alpha_{h}+\gamma_{h}Senti_{i,t}+\lambda_{h}Char_{i,t-1}+\theta_{h}Macro_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{i,t},h=0,1,\cdots24 $$ 

where the explained variable  $ Risk_{i,t+h} $, is the forward h period either of the two risk subcomponents, with monthly frequency and window period for the next year.  $ Senti_{i,t} $ denotes the media sentiment on bank i in month t. The other bank characteristics  $ Char_{i,t-1} $ and macro conditions  $ Macro_{t-1} $ remain the same every month in the same quarter. In addition, we set the forecast period h of the explained variable to two years (24 periods) for our prospective regression and analyze the influence by observing the change of the coefficient  $ \gamma_h $ before the core explanatory variable. It should be emphasized that, since this paper focuses on explaining the risk generation process from the perspective of negative shocks, the analysis in this section mainly focuses on the trend of  $ -\gamma_h $, which reflects the impact of one unit of negative sentiment shocks on bank risk shown in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5 shows the dynamic impact of negative shocks to bank sentiment on systemic risk for group k = 30 (under approximately 10 % quantile). We can see that the impact on systemic linkage (lnSL30) is significant in the beginning one year and gradually turns insignificant later on, while the impact on bank tail risk (lnIR30) remains insignificant until half a year later. Specifically, the 10 % confidence interval of the systemic linkage (lnSL30) in Fig. 5 is significantly above 0 in the early stage. In the later stage, the 10 % confidence interval of systemic linkage begins to include 0, and the 10 % confidence interval of bank tail risk begins to be significantly higher than 0. That is to say, in the early stage when bank sentiment is negatively impacted, the change in systemic risk is mainly dominated by systemic linkage, while in the later stage, it is mainly dominated by bank tail risk.

This is because the influence of media sentiment on bank systemic risk will go through a process from risk accumulation to risk realization. In the normal operation of banks, negative sentiment shocks may reduce the depositors' sense of security, and thus decrease the stability of the non-core liabilities. To compensate for the negative impact of the loss of deposits, on the one hand, banks tend to increase their inter-bank liabilities which will naturally enhance the direct systemic linkage. On the other hand, banks tend to hold more bonds and inter-bank investments to maintain liquidity, shirk responsibilities through herding behaviors to preserve reputation, and diversify risk in the similar overall field, which strengthen the indirect systemic linkage. Both systemic linkages can be observed in bank balance sheet and are generated from bank behaviors.

Initially, the systemic linkages are created for the purpose of sharing benefits among banks and hardly pose any risk to them.

However, with increasing systemic linkage, once the bank suffers a negative shock in the future, the losses will spread quickly and easily along the already established correlation channels, which may eventually induce systemic risks. Therefore, in the early stage of bank systemic risk generation, systemic linkage plays a significant leading role, which just corresponds to the risk accumulation process.

However, with time going by, bank tail risk has gradually replaced systemic linkage, becoming the dominant factor of systemic risk, which corresponds to the risk realization process. In that stage, the bank faced with negative sentiment shock becomes more vulnerable due to taking more risks on the asset side to deal with the higher interest expense of inter-bank liabilities compared with bank deposits. In this case, once the bank suffers a negative shock, the loss will spread immediately through the inter-bank connection channels established in the previous risk accumulation process, causing more losses to be realized for individual banks in the system. In summary, this dynamic analysis can help us identify and clarify the time lag phenomenon between systemic risk accumulation and realization process. ## 8. Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the impact of media sentiment on bank systemic risk, based on Chinese bank samples during the period of 2007–2021. Under systemic risk decomposition methodology, we find consistent and robust evidence that negative shock to media sentiment will increase both bank tail risk and systemic linkage, hence the total level of systemic risk in market performance.

Our research makes contributions to extant literature focusing on the impact of media sentiment on bank systemic risk by searching for the linkage between market-data-based performance and banks' balance-sheet behaviors. We provide the evidence of negative media sentiment shocks affecting banks deposit stability. In summary, we present consistent results that the “sentiment-bank tail risk” nexus is largely attributable to the more risk-taking willingness of the banks and “sentiment-systemic linkage” nexus is associated with more inter-bank business and homogeneous strategies among banks.

Our results bear important policy implications that regulatory authorities should strengthen the prudential supervision of banks. Supervisory authorities should incorporate sentiment management into their macro-prudential policy framework to monitor and prevent the occurrence of systemic financial risks. The results of this paper find that negative sentiment shocks have a profound impact on bank systemic risk. In addition to concerning about rapid contagion of negative shock to media sentiments under herding effect, we should also focus on the increase in the willingness of banks to take active risks due to the decline in the stability of their liabilities. While doing a good job in micro-prudential bank individual risk management, authorities should also enhance macro-prudential management in response to changes in bank interconnectedness.

We use methodology in Bertrand and Mullainathan (2001) to examine whether change in the liability side is a channel on the change of behaviors on the asset side.

Assume the channel factors donate as X, here referring to the inter-bank ratio, we set two-stage specification as follows. In the first stage, we run a similar regression like baseline model to test the impact of bank media sentiment on the inter-bank liabilities to total liabilities:

 $$ X_{it}=\alpha_{F}+\gamma_{F}\cdot Senti_{it}+\eta_{F}\cdot Others+\varepsilon_{ijt} $$ 

Here  $ \gamma_{F} $ captures the influence of bank media sentiment on the non-core liabilities. From this equation we predict a bank’s channel factors regressed by media sentiment. Call this predicted value  $ \widehat{X}_{it} $. We then ask how bank risk taking and bank indirect linkage responds to these predictable changes due to the change of bank media sentiment:

 $$ Risk_{it}=\alpha+\lambda\cdot\widehat{X}_{it}+\eta_{F}\cdot Others+\varepsilon_{it} $$ 

Then  $ \lambda\widehat{\gamma}_{F} $ is the key result that reflects how bank media sentiment affects bank risk indicators through the factor X of inter-bank liabilities, risk-taking and indirect business association. The results in the second regressions are displayed in Table B1, Table B2, which show significantly positive coefficients. It is suggested that negative shock to bank media sentiment enhances risk taking and increases indirect linkage among banks.
