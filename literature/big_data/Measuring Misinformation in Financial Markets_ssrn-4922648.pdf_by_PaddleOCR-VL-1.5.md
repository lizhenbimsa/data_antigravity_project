# Measuring Misinformation in Financial Markets

Jianqing Fan, Qingfu Liu, Yang Song, Zilu Wang

December 2025

## Abstract

We propose a framework for measuring firm-level misinformation. By leveraging advanced AI and LLM technologies, we categorize unstructured text into comparable information, assess the reliability of information sources, extract reliability-weighted benchmark from each set of comparable information, and quantify misinformation as the semantic divergence from this benchmark. Applying our framework to a large-scale corpus of 254.8 million textual materials, we find that firms with weaker balance sheets and poorer governance structures exhibit a higher degree of misinformation, and misinformation intensifies during major corporate events. We also demonstrate that misinformation significantly impacts investor behavior, stock return, and firm-level risk.

## 1 Introduction

Accurate and timely information is the cornerstone of financial markets (e.g., Fama, 1970; Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980). Yet, the rise of misleading or false information, often referred to as “misinformation,” is becoming increasingly prevalent. $ ^{1} $ According to a 2023 survey by Nationwide, 34% of investors aged 18-54 acted upon financial information seen on the Internet or social media that turned out to be misleading or factually incorrect. $ ^{2} $ Such misinformation can possibly skew market perceptions, lead to poor decision-making, and cause significant market inefficiencies, thereby increasing financial risks. Indeed, the World Economic Forum in January 2024 identified misinformation as one of the biggest emerging risks the world is facing. $ ^{3} $

Despite its widespread presence, academic research on misinformation in financial markets remains limited. One major challenge lies in the difficulty of measuring misinformation due to factors such as the asymmetric nature and lack of disclosure of genuine information. Overcoming this challenge is crucial for understanding the impact of misinformation on investor behavior and market dynamics.

Our study aims to address the urgent need to measure and analyze misinformation in financial markets. We adopt an operational focus on misinformation, restricting our attention to firm-related statements that are, in principle, objectively verifiable. $ ^{4} $ Yet in practice, the underlying factual state of a firm at a given point in time is typically latent and cannot be directly observed or verified manually at scale. To overcome this challenge, we leverage recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) to develop a systematic framework for quantifying firm-level misinformation. Conceptually, we treat the

factual state of a firm on a given dimension as a latent variable and view each text statement as a noisy signal about this latent state, with some sources being more tightly anchored to that state than others. Within this framework, we construct a public-information-based, reliability-weighted benchmark to approximate this latent factual state, and our firm-level misinformation index summarizes the average deviation of observed statements from this benchmark.

Our approach is grounded in two key principles: information consistency and the wisdom of select crowds. The principle of information consistency asserts that signals that are internally coherent and corroborated across diverse sources are more likely to be close to the latent factual state. $ ^{5} $ The principle of the wisdom of select crowds emphasizes that not all sources are equally reliable; signals from more reliable sources (for example, those with stronger accountability or better historical performance) should receive greater weight (Surowiecki, 2005; Mannes et al., 2014). $ ^{6} $

The input to our framework is a massive firm-level textual corpus comprising hundreds of millions of textual materials from diverse sources, including firm announcements, news reports, analyst reports, and social media posts. As a first step, we filter out forward-looking predictions, emotional expressions, and subjective opinions from the corpus. This enables us to focus on identifying misinformation, which concerns the factual accuracy of objectively verifiable information, while distinguishing it from disagreement, which primarily reflects differences in subjective interpretation and belief. For example, analysts may issue divergent forecasts of a company's future earnings; even if some of these forecasts ultimately prove incorrect, they are not considered misinformation, as they reflect differing views on the firm's prospective financial performance.

To quantify misinformation, we overcome two primary challenges: (i) transforming and

categorizing unstructured textual materials from diverse sources and formats into comparable information sets, and (ii) constructing, within each set, a “reliability-weighted information benchmark” that approximates the latent factual state. Using this framework, we assess the factual inconsistency of individual pieces of information by measuring their deviation from the inferred benchmark within their respective comparable information set.

To overcome the first challenge, we develop a two-tier topic classification (TC) and entity extraction (EE) procedure using LLMs. Specifically, the TC procedure identifies the topics of the text (e.g., “financial performance” or “business operations”), $ ^{7} $ while the EE procedure further extracts specific entities under a given topic (e.g., “operating income” under the topic of financial performance or “market share” under the topic of business operations). Different pieces of information about the same company can be meaningfully compared only if they have identical topic-entity labels. Subsequently, we also use LLMs to convert narrative texts into structured, quantitative vectors to quantify the underlying semantic insights.

To tackle the second challenge, we adopt a network-based approach to identify the reliability-weighted information benchmark for each set of comparable information—that is, information pertaining to the same company with identical topic-entity labels. Specifically, for a given comparable information set, each piece of information is represented as a node in the network, and connections between nodes are determined by measures of semantic consistency (Strogatz, 2001; Chandrasekaran and Mago, 2021).

Recognizing that not all information is equally reliable, we assign higher reliability weights to nodes from more credible sources and exhibiting greater logical coherence in sentence structure. In this way, we emphasize the wisdom of select crowds. Importantly, source credibility is allowed to evolve dynamically as new evidence accumulates—rewarding sources that have historically produced less misinformation with greater influence in estimating the factual state. Consistent with the principle of information consistency, we identify an op-

timal representation of reliability-weighted information benchmark within the information network, incorporating both the semantic relationships and reliability weights. $ ^{8} $

In the last step, we quantify the degree of misinformation for each set of comparable information by measuring the semantic deviations of individual statements from the inferred benchmark within the information set. Building on this, we calculate firm-level misinformation scores using a bottom-up aggregation across all the information sets associated with the firm. The overall framework for measuring misinformation is illustrated in Figure 1. In a nutshell, our approach is closely aligned with the literature of truth discovery in information science, which infers unobservable truth by aggregating information from multiple and possibly conflicting sources, as well as AI hallucination detection in natural language generation. $ ^{9} $

We apply our method to measure monthly firm-level misinformation in the Chinese financial market, the second-largest capital market in the world. Our study focuses on Chinese A-share companies from January 2015 to December 2022. After data processing, our textual corpus contains 254,826,060 articles, $ ^{10} $ pertaining to 3,994 public firms. While we study the Chinese markets due to the ready availability of this extensive corpus, our approach can be applied to other markets as well.

We start by evaluating the properties of our misinformation measure (MISI). First, we assess the behavior of MISI around publicly disclosed misinformation-related events, as disclosed either by regulators through penalty announcements or by firms through rumor clarification statements. $ ^{11} $ Using these events as an external benchmark, we show that statements that are closely aligned with claims officially identified as false or misleading exhibit systematically higher piece-level MISI than other statements. We also find that MISI rises significantly during these events, indicating responsiveness to recorded misinformation incidents. Second, we examine the relationship between MISI and the quality of corporate information disclosure, as rated by regulators. We hypothesize that firms with higher disclosure quality should exhibit lower MISI. Consistent with this expectation, we find a significant negative correlation between a firm's MISI and its disclosure quality. Third, we investigate whether MISI reacts to exogenous changes in the information environment. In particular, we study the enactment of China's Cybersecurity Law in June 2017, which mandates that all websites verify user identities through real-name registration and is thus expected to curb misinformation—especially among firms with a large retail investor base. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observe a significant decline in MISI for firms with high retail ownership following the law's implementation. $ ^{12} $

We further examine the relationship between misinformation and disagreement. While misinformation refers to misleading and false statements, disagreement primarily reflects differences in subjective interpretation—possibly arising from factors such as information asymmetry, heterogeneous preferences and beliefs, or even cognitive biases (Diether et al., 2002; Cookson and Niessner, 2020). To study this, we construct a novel measure of disagreement based on subjective opinions, emotional expressions, and forward-looking predictions in the corpus, which are explicitly filtered out when computing the misinformation measure. We then classify this subset of the corpus into comparable information sets. Following the approach of existing disagreement measures, we compute the standard deviation of semantic similarity scores within each comparable information set and aggregate these to obtain a firm-level measure of disagreement. $ ^{13} $ Our analysis reveals that increases in MISI significantly predict subsequent rises in disagreement, but not the reverse. This asymmetry suggests that the spread of misinformation can amplify disagreement in the market, whereas elevated disagreement does not, in itself, give rise to greater misinformation.

In the next part of the empirical analysis, we further explore the drivers of misinformation. We first examine whether and how firm characteristics are related to the degree of misinformation. We find that firms with weak balance sheets, high financial risks, and poor corporate governance structures have higher average levels of misinformation. Moreover, we examine whether the occurrence of major corporate events can drive misinformation. During significant corporate events, there is likely to be a surge in information speculation and dissemination. This creates an environment ripe for the spread of misinformation, as stakeholders may attempt to influence market perceptions and reactions. We find that major corporate actions, e.g., financing operations and mergers and acquisitions, indeed trigger the circulation of misinformation. This finding underscores the importance of managing misinformation during major corporate activities.

In the final part of the paper, we explore the implications of misinformation for finance.

cial markets, focusing on investor behavior, price dynamics, and downside risk. We begin by analyzing the relationship between misinformation and trading activity. Elevated levels of misinformation may lead investors to react more frequently and aggressively as they attempt to capitalize on perceived opportunities or mitigate potential risks. Consistent with this hypothesis, we find that higher levels of misinformation are associated with increased abnormal trading volume. Importantly, this relationship varies across investor types. Specifically, we observe net buying among small and medium-sized trades, but net selling among large trades. This divergence suggests that less-sophisticated retail investors are more likely to buy in response to misinformation, whereas large, sophisticated investors tend to sell during periods characterized by high levels of misinformation.

Second, we find that misinformation also contributes to market mispricing. Following the decomposition approach of Rhodes-Kropf et al. (2005) and Borochin and Yang (2017), we construct a firm-level measure of mispricing relative to fundamentals. Our results show that firms with higher levels of misinformation are significantly more likely to exhibit mispricing. Moreover, we observe that increases in misinformation are associated with a significant immediate rise in stock prices, followed by subsequent reversals.

Finally, we document strong predictive power of misinformation for stock downside risk. Intuitively, when genuine information about a company is obfuscated and misinformation circulates, the firm's risk profile is likely to deteriorate (e.g., Hutton et al., 2009). Following Kim et al. (2011) and Calomiris and Mamaysky (2019), we measure downside risk using two metrics: maximum drawdown and negative conditional return skewness (crash risk). Consistent with this hypothesis, we find that firms with higher levels of misinformation are more prone to experience deeper drawdowns and elevated crash risk in the future.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 discusses our contribution to several strands of extant literature. Section 3 presents the methodology for measuring firm-level misinformation. Section 4 describes the data. Section 5 analyzes the properties of the misinformation measure. Section 6 examines the drivers of firm-level misinformation.

Section 7 studies the implications of misinformation on investor behavior and stock price dynamics. Section 8 concludes. Technical details, additional analyses, and robustness checks are provided in the appendices.

## 2 Relation to Prior Literature

Our paper makes several contributions to the literature. Classic works have established the pivotal role of information in shaping investor behavior and market outcomes (e.g., Fama, 1970; Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980). While extensive research has analyzed the role of information in financial markets, little attention has been paid to the study of misinformation, despite its growing relevance. $ ^{14} $ Our study aims to bridge this gap by introducing a novel framework for systematically measuring misinformation at the firm level. Our approach enables the identification and quantification of misinformation across a large-scale textual corpus, allowing us to examine its effects in a rigorous and scalable way. We find that the circulation of misinformation significantly influences investor trading behavior and stock price dynamics. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically quantify and analyze misinformation at the level of individual firms.

Our study also contributes to the literature on financial textual analysis (e.g., Gentzkow et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023b; Gad et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024). Existing research shows that analyst reports, social media posts, and news articles

can significantly influence investor behavior and asset prices (e.g., Jiang et al., 2019; Cohen et al., 2020; Beckmann et al., 2024). However, these sources are not without biases, which can skew perceptions and decision-making in financial markets (Qin et al., 2018; Schmidt, 2020; Fan et al., 2025). By employing advanced LLMs and an information network approach, our paper provides an empirical framework to assess the quality of information contained in textual materials. Relatedly, while previous textual analyses typically focus on document-level characteristics like sentiment (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024), a few studies have shifted to extract and analyze specific topics from text materials (e.g., Bybee et al., 2024; Giglio et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023b). We provide a methodology for multi-label topic classification and entity extraction that can be used for future research of textual analysis.

Our research is also related to the growing literature on machine learning (ML) and big data in finance (e.g., Kleinberg et al., 2018; Erel et al., 2021; Easley et al., 2021; Lyonnet and Stern, 2022; Kaniel et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Van Binsbergen et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). For example, several papers demonstrate the power of ML, generative AI, and LLMs in information collection, processing, and extraction (e.g., Gentzkow et al., 2019; Giglio et al., 2022; Beckmann et al., 2024; Kim and Nikolaev, 2023). Our paper integrates the strengths of these technologies to tackle the task of measuring misinformation, leveraging the understanding capabilities of generative AI for information labeling, the efficiency and speed of LLMs for information embedding, and the cost-effectiveness of few-shot frameworks for text classification and extraction.

We note that there is a strand of literature in computer science dedicated to misinformation detection (e.g., Wu et al., 2019; Zhou and Zafarani, 2020). However, these methods significantly diverge from our approach. They often rely on algorithms trained on specific datasets, where texts are pre-labeled as true or false, then assign probabilities of falsehood to individual texts based on these training datasets. Such techniques are not well-suited for the finance context, characterized by voluminous and subtly false information and the lack of specialized labels for training.

## 3 Framework for Measuring Misinformation

In this section, we develop the framework for measuring misinformation. We overview the framework in Section 3.1, with details explained in Section 3.2.

### 3.1 Overview of the framework

Misinformation refers to false or misleading information that deviates from objective facts. Accordingly, measuring misinformation inherently requires evaluating such information against objective facts. In practice, however, such facts are often unobservable or infeasible to verify manually at scale.

To address this challenge, we develop a framework for quantifying misinformation based on two complementary principles: information consistency and the wisdom of select crowds. Conceptually, we treat the factual state of a firm on a given dimension in a given month as an unobserved latent variable, and view each objectively verifiable statement as a noisy signal about this latent state. The principle of information consistency holds that information is more likely to be close to the underlying factual state if it is internally coherent and can be corroborated across different sources. $ ^{15} $ The second principle recognizes that not all information sources are equally reliable. Accordingly, greater weight should be assigned to sources with higher credibility, such as firm disclosures and official media reports that are subject to legal and reputational constraints. Crucially, these credibility assessments should be updated dynamically as new evidence accumulates, rewarding those that have historically produced less misinformation.

Based on these two principles, we utilize advanced AI and LLMs technologies to quantify firm-level misinformation at a monthly frequency using hundreds of millions of pieces of textual materials. As a first step, we explicitly exclude subjective opinions, emotional

expressions, and forward-looking predictions from the corpus. This preprocessing enables us to distinguish misinformation from disagreement, which primarily reflects differences in subjective interpretation. $ ^{16} $

We then address two primary challenges. First, we convert and categorize textual materials from diverse sources and formats into comparable information. Second, for each set of comparable information, we construct a reliability-weighted information benchmark—the semantic representation supported by credible information across various sources—as an approximation of the latent factual state.

The first critical step involves systematically transforming noisy, unstructured textual materials into structured, comparable information. For instance, consider the following three segments regarding the same company:

- The company emphasizes employee training and development, aiming to enhance overall skills and productivity.

• Our main product line has achieved significant growth.

• The company’s marketing efforts have received widespread recognition.

Segment 1 clearly relates to human capital, whereas Segments 2 and 3 relate to business operations but focus on different specific aspects (product line vs. marketing), and therefore cannot be directly compared. We assign to each segment a topic label o (e.g., “human capital” or “business operations”) and an entity label e capturing the specific subject within that topic (e.g., “employee”, “product line”, or “marketing”). Each segment can thus be organized into a topic-entity tuple (o, e), and only information with identical topic-entity labels of the same company can be meaningfully compared.

Specifically, leveraging the recent development of LLMs, we develop a two-tier topic classification (TC) and entity extraction (EE) procedure to transform unstructured texts into comparable information sets. The TC procedure identifies the topics of the text, while

the EE procedure captures entities underlying the identified topics. It is worth emphasizing that one piece of textual material (e.g., a quarterly report) can contain multiple topics, and our algorithm can comprehensively identify all relevant topics and entities. After this step, unstructured textual information can be categorized by the topic-entity labels.

The second critical step involves extracting an estimate of the latent factual state from each set of comparable information—that is, information pertaining to the same company and sharing an identical topic-entity tuple  $ (o, e) $. To achieve this, we first convert unstructured data into fixed-length embedding vectors using LLMs. $ ^{17} $ Each piece of information is represented as a tuple  $ (o, e, \mathbf{x}) $, where o denotes the overarching topic, e identifies the specific entity, and  $ \mathbf{x} $ is the corresponding semantic feature vector.

Next, we conceptualize the extraction of this benchmark as a two-stage optimization problem implemented over an information network. In the first stage, we estimate a reliability weight for each piece of information. Higher weights are assigned to information that is logically coherent in its sentence construction and originates from sources with a strong historical track record. Importantly, source credibility is updated dynamically, allowing the framework to adapt as new evidence becomes available. In the second stage, we extract the optimal representation of reliability-weighted benchmark within each comparable information set (denoted as  $ \mathbf{x}^{*} $). This optimization procedure incorporates both the reliability and volume of available information, operationalizing the principles of information consistency and the wisdom of select crowds.

Using this framework, the falsity degree of a given piece of information is measured by the divergence between its semantic feature vector (x) and the corresponding information benchmark representation (x*) within its comparable information set. $ ^{18} $ We then quantify firm-level misinformation through a bottom-up aggregation across all information sets associated with the firm.

Further discussions of the framework Our framework relies on the assumption that, under typical conditions with at least some regulated sources that anchor the information environment and with errors that are not perfectly correlated across all sources, the reliability-weighted information benchmark x* is expected to be close to the underlying factual state. This assumption is standard in the literature on truth discovery in information science (Li et al., 2016), as well as in research on detecting AI hallucinations in natural language generation (Ji et al., 2023).

While this assumption is widely accepted in the computer science literature and is reasonable in most cases, it may break down in rare and extreme scenarios where historically credible sources simultaneously disseminate misinformation. In such environments, our measure may understate misinformation in high-weight mainstream sources while overstating it for dissenting statements that are closer to the true factual state. Within our framework, however, such risk is substantially mitigated by the presence of firm announcements and official media reports, which are strictly regulated and thus provide relatively reliable soft anchors for the latent factual state. $ ^{19} $ To further mitigate this risk, we also dynamically update source credibility and leverage semantic network relationships to downweight isolated or contradictory statements.

In addition, because our framework evaluates misinformation based on divergence from the benchmark, it does not distinguish between intentional and unintentional falsehoods. As such, it captures both deliberate misinformation (i.e., disinformation) and inaccuracies arising from errors or misjudgments.

### 3.2 The Framework for Measuring Misinformation

In this section, we detail the framework, which consists of five steps: (a) filtering the corpus to retain only objectively verifiable text; (b) organizing texts into comparable topic

entity clusters; (c) converting narrative texts into structured, quantitative vectors to capture semantic meaning; (d) constructing information networks to identify the underlying reliability-weighted information benchmark within each cluster; and (e) measuring misinformation as deviations from the identified benchmark and aggregating these deviations to the firm level. We dedicate certain technical details to the appendix.

#### 3.2.1 Stripping away subjectivity: identifying objectively verifiable texts

As discussed in Section 3.1, we intend to distinguish misinformation from disagreement. To this end, we exclude subjective opinions, emotional expressions, and forward-looking statements from our analysis. Given the semantic complexity of this task and the scale of the corpus, we implement a classification approach that leverages recent advances in LLMs. $ ^{20} $

In the first step, we use a generative LLM (ChatGPT-4o) to annotate a representative sample of texts, drawn evenly from four source types: social media, news articles, company disclosures, and analyst reports. $ ^{21} $ Prompts are designed to instruct the LLM to classify each text as objectively verifiable or subjective. To enhance annotation quality, we adopt a chain-of-thought (CoT) structure that encourages step-by-step reasoning. $ ^{22} $ All generated annotations are manually reviewed for accuracy, resulting in a high-quality training dataset.

In the second step, we fine-tune a pre-trained BERT model by adding a binary classification layer and training it on this labeled dataset. For each text, the classifier outputs the probability that it is objectively verifiable, based on the (contextualized) embedding generated by the BERT model, and assigns the text to the class with the higher probability.

Further technical details, including model architecture and performance, are provided in Appendix A. $ ^{23} $

#### 3.2.2 From clutter to clusters: categorizing text into comparable information sets

As explained in Section 3.1, one primary challenge is making information across diverse sources and formats comparable. To address this issue, we adopt a two-tier strategy that categorizes each piece of information by assigning it a specific topic-entity tuple  $ (o, e) $. That is, each piece of information is labeled with a coarse-grained topic label  $ (o) $ and a fine-grained entity label  $ (e) $.

This process includes two steps: topic classification and entity extraction. In the first step, we perform topic classification. $ ^{24} $ Drawing on recent studies of firm value (e.g., Belo et al., 2022) and asset pricing (e.g., Feng et al., 2020; Leippold et al., 2022), we focus on eight key topics that are closely tied to both the short-term and long-term firm values: business operations (BO), financial performance (FP), risk management (RM), corporate governance (CG), environmental responsibility (ER), social responsibility (SR), human capital (HC), and R&D innovation (RD).

We use LLMs for topic classification following the approach outlined by Li et al. (2023b). Specifically, we utilize the ERNIE model, a BERT variant developed by Baidu for the Chinese language, to encode texts into quantifiable vectors. Given that financial texts are typically complex in content and cover multiple topics simultaneously, instead of directly fine-tuning the embeddings, we use them as inputs for the Unified Semantic Matching (USM) model to

enhance the accuracy of topic classification. The USM model is an open-source and highly effective algorithm designed for diverse text-based semantic classification and information extraction. $ ^{25} $ In a nutshell, this model constructs a shared semantic space for user-defined labels and texts through Directed-Token-Linking (DTL) and then performs semantic matching. Although our implementation of multi-label topic classification is also a contribution to financial textual analysis, we leave the details in Appendix A.

After the initial step of topic classification, the second step aims to extract entities from texts within each topic cluster. Traditional entity extraction methods, typically dependent on manual fixed rules or lexicons, struggle to cope with the diverse and context-rich nature of financial texts (Li et al., 2020). Thus, we utilize LLMs to achieve our goal. In particular, we further fine-tune the USM model using the embeddings from the LLM (the ERNIE model) to extract entities. This refined algorithm allows us to identify entities in an unsupervised way by analyzing the language sequences, rather than relying on predefined entities. $ ^{26} $ The details of the entity extraction procedure are also in Appendix A.

On average, there are 26.34 entities identified per month for a given company under a given topic. Examples of extracted entities are shown in Figure 2, which presents the high-frequency entity word cloud for the eight pre-determined topics based on the textual materials in 2018. The size of each word in these clouds corresponds to its frequency. The figure clearly demonstrates the effectiveness of our entity extraction algorithm. For example, under the topic of “financial performance,” entities like “operating income,” “net profit,” and “earnings per share” emerge prominently. Under the topic of “human capital,” entities like “talents” and “employee” are highly frequent. To further validate our results, we follow the approach of Sautner et al. (2023) and invite ten doctoral students to collaboratively assess the reasonableness of the extracted entities.

#### 3.2.3 Dissecting the narrative: quantifying semantic insights

Transforming complex narratives into quantifiable features is a significant challenge in textual analysis (Zhou et al., 2024). Traditional models like TF-IDF and lexicons, while useful for providing keyword insights, often miss the intricate relationships of words and overlook deeper semantic connections (Kim et al., 2024). In contrast, LLMs that rely on the Transformer architecture have been proven to effectively encode textual data into vectors (embeddings), capturing rich semantic features (Chen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023b).

To ensure a comprehensive understanding of textual information, we extract semantic features based on ERNIE, the BERT variant optimized for processing Chinese texts. Similar to BERT, ERNIE translates the composite meaning of the entire input sequence into a 768-dimensional numeric vector associated with the classification (CLS) token. Following Kim and Nikolaev (2023), we use the embeddings associated with the CLS tokens as our semantic feature vector. $ ^{27} $

#### 3.2.4 Finding the information benchmark: building information networks

After structuring the textual materials into topic-entity clusters and generating their corresponding vector representations, our next objective is to construct the reliability-weighted information benchmark that approximates the latent factual state. We adopt an unsupervised approach with three steps: (i) constructing a network to quantify semantic relationships among information within a given comparable information set; (ii) dynamically assessing the reliability of each piece of information by evaluating both source credibility and content coherence; (iii) extracting the reliability-weighted benchmark from each network by jointly accounting for the reliability and volume of available information.

Constructing information networks For each topic-entity pair $(o,e)$ of a given company, we construct an undirected information network, $\mathcal{G} = \langle \mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E} \rangle$, for the corresponding information set $I$ associated with $(o,e)$. Here, $\mathcal{V}$ represents the nodes, each of which corresponds to an individual piece of information in information set $I$. $\mathcal{E}$ denotes the set of edges. For example, the edge between information $i$ and $j$ is established based on the cosine similarity between the vector semantic representation $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}_j$ of information $i$ and $j$:

 $$ s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\times\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}}. $$ 

Cosine similarity is extensively used in textual analysis to measure the closeness of textual content in high-dimensional spaces (Chandrasekaran and Mago, 2021; Fan et al., 2025). A higher cosine similarity indicates strong semantic alignment, while a lower value reflects semantic contradiction. We also define the edge weight matrix  $ \mathbf{B} = (b_{ij})_{|I|\times|I|} $ by

 $$ b_{i j}=1+s(\mathbf{x_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{j})=1+\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\|_{2}\times\left\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|_{2}}. $$ 

Consequently, pairs of information that are more semantically consistent receive higher edge weights, while those that are contradictory or less related have lower weights.

Measuring reliability of information We measure the reliability of each piece of information based on the credibility of the information source and the coherence of the information content. In doing so, we can emphasize more reliable information.

Information sources vary significantly in credibility. For example, retail investors and analysts possess different levels of knowledge and insights, while official media sources are typically more reliable than unofficial ones. We quantify this aspect by assigning a credibility weight to each information source (e.g., a particular news agency). $ ^{28} $

Recognizing the dynamic nature of credibility, we introduce a penalty mechanism to

adjust the weight of each information source based on its propensity to disseminate misinformation. Specifically, a source's credibility weight is recalibrated at the end of each month, contingent on its average misinformation measure in the preceding month. This is done using an exponential decay formula for adjustment:

 $$ c_{s,t}=c_{s,t-1}\times e^{-\alpha M I S I_{s,t-1}}. $$ 

Here,  $ c_{s,t} $ represents the credibility weight of information source s in month t.  $ MISI_{s,t-1} $ denotes the average misinformation measure calculated from all articles disseminated by source s in month t-1; a higher value indicates greater degree of misinformation. $ ^{29} $ The parameter  $ \alpha $ is a positive constant determining the rate of decay due to misinformation, set here at 0.1. To initialize source credibility, we use ChatGPT-4 to obtain initial source weights, following the approach of Yang and Menczer (2025). $ ^{30} $ Time series plots of the credibility weights for information sources, based on a random sample of 500 companies, are presented in Appendix A.4. One can see that social media has the lowest credibility weight, and company announcements have the highest credibility weight. It is worth noting that our results are robust without taking this dynamic adjustment approach.

Additionally, we assess the content reliability by evaluating the logical coherence of sentence constructions, inferred through their formation probability. This step helps mitigate the impact of texts that are grammatically incorrect or do not conform to common sense. Specifically, for each information i, we use the generative pre-trained GPT model of Radford et al. (2019) to compute a content coherence score  $ l_{i} $:

 $$ l_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log P(word_{j}\mid word_{<j}), $$ 

where  $ (word_1, word_2, ..., word_n) $ are the words, and  $ P(word_j \mid word_{<j}) $ is the probability assigned by GPT to the j-th word given its context. $ ^{31} $ The use of this model is motivated by its proven ability to capture the statistical and logical regularities of natural language, making it highly effective for quantifying content coherence and identifying potentially unreliable or anomalous information.

We then integrate the content coherence and the source credibility to assign an initial reliability weight for each piece of information, assuming equal importance for both factors:

 $$ w_{i}=c_{s,t}+l_{i}. $$ 

Here,  $ w_{i} $ represents the reliability weight of information i at time t, which is from source s. For notational simplicity, we omit subscripts t and s in the weight  $ w_{i} $.  $ c_{s,t} $ represents the credibility of source s at time t, calculated based on equation (3), and  $ l_{i} $ is the content coherence score for information i. Both  $ c_{s,t} $ and  $ l_{i} $ are numerical values, rescaled to have a range from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater reliability.

To assign a final reliability weight to each piece of information, we perform one extra step of refinement using the constructed information network. This refinement step balances two goals: first, the refined weights align closely with the initial reliability weights defined in equation (5); second, assigning similar weights to information that exhibits similar semantic proximity within the network, so that information conveying similar meanings shares similar reliability assessments. The objective of this refinement is to mitigate the influence of source-specific biases, a problem highlighted in Qin et al. (2018) and Fan et al. (2025).

Specifically, following Garza and Schaeffer (2019), this refinement step is formalized via

the following optimization problem:

 $$ \min_{\tilde{\mathbf{w}}}\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\mathbf{w}})=\min_{\tilde{\mathbf{w}}}\frac{\eta}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}b_{i j}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{i}}}\tilde{w}_{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{j}}}\tilde{w}_{j}\right|^{2}\right). $$ 

Here, $\mathbf{w}$ denotes the vector of initial reliability weights (defined in equation (5)) for all information in set $I$ associated with topic-entity $(o,e)$, and $\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}$ represents the refined reliability weights. The Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|$ measures the distance between the refined and initial weights, and $f_i = \sum_{k=1}^{|I|} b_{ik}$ with the edge weight, $b_{ik}$, defined in equation (2). The regularization parameter $\eta$ balances the trade-off between maintaining the proximity of initial and updated weights and the semantic similarity among the texts. To get the idea, suppose that information $i$ and information $j$ have identical semantic representation $(\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_j)$. Then, $f_i = f_j$ and $b_{ij} = 1$ (the maximum possible value), so that the optimization process tends to assign more similar values to $\widetilde{w}_i$ and $\widetilde{w}_j$.

While we take the steps to dynamically adjust the credibility scores of information sources and refine reliability weights to mitigate the influence of inherent source-specific biases, we also verify that our results in the following sections are quantitatively robust, albeit slightly weaker, under a simplified procedure without these steps.

Extracting the benchmark Next, we turn to extract the information benchmark within the information set associated with a given topic-entity pair of a company. Our method transforms this task into an optimization problem designed to account for the quantity and reliability of the information, reflecting the principles of information consistency and the wisdom of select crowds.

Consider, for example, the scenario involving evaluating a company's financial performance. If all information sources uniformly suggest high performance, this information benchmark strongly indicates its veracity. However, contrasting scenarios, such as an authoritative source claiming "high performance" while numerous social media discussions suggest

“low performance,” present a more complex picture. In such a case, despite the generally lower credibility of social media, the overwhelming volume of these discussions could suggest the information benchmark is more aligned with “low performance.”

Our goal is to balance information reliability and volume. In particular, we calculate the information benchmark  $ \mathbf{x}^{*} $ by

 $$ \mathbf{x}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\sum_{i\in I}\widetilde{w}_{i}\cdot d(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x})=\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\sum_{i\in I}\widetilde{w}_{i}\cdot(1-s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x})), $$ 

where  $ \widetilde{w}_{i} $ represents the refined reliability weight for information i in equation (6), and  $ d(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathbf{x}) = 1 - s(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathbf{x}) $ measures the semantic difference between information  $ \mathbf{x}_{i} $ and  $ \mathbf{x} $. The vector  $ \mathbf{x}^{*} $ symbolizes our approximation of the latent factual state, which is weighted by information reliability. As one can see, more reliable information carries higher weights in the optimization process. This results in a reliability-weighted information benchmark for a given comparable information set.

#### 3.2.5 Measuring misinformation: aggregating deviations from the benchmark

In the last step, we calculate firm-level misinformation using a bottom-up approach. To this end, we first calculate the misinformation measure for each piece of information i as the deviation from the identified information benchmark within its comparable information set:

 $$ M I S I_{i}^{p i e c e}=d(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{*})=1-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{*}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\times\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{2}}. $$ 

Here,  $ x_{i} $ and  $ x^{*} $ represent the vector of semantic features for information i and the corresponding information benchmark, respectively.

With this, the misinformation measure at the topic level is calculated as

 $$ M I S I^{t o p i c}=\sum_{e\in E^{o}}M I S I_{e}^{e n t i t y}=\sum_{e\in E^{o}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}^{e}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{e}}M I S I_{i}^{p i e c e}, $$ 

where  $ E^{o} $ is the set of entities associated with topic o, and  $ |\mathcal{I}^{e}| $ is the number of pieces of information associated with entity e. It is an aggregated deviation for a topic. Then, the misinformation measure at the firm level is calculated as

 $$ M I S I^{f i r m}=\frac{1}{\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\left|\mathcal{I}^{o}\right|}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\left|\mathcal{I}^{o}\right|\cdot M I S I_{o}^{t o p i c}. $$ 

Here,  $ \mathcal{O} $ is the set of the eight pre-determined topics relevant to the firm, and  $ |\mathcal{I}^{o}| $ is the total number of pieces of information for topic  $ o $, which is used to weight the contribution of each topic to the firm-level MISI. $ ^{32} $

## 4 Data and the Misinformation Measure

In this section, we detail the massive dataset that we use to measure firm-level misinformation. We also provide descriptive statistics of the misinformation measure.

### 4.1 Dataset

We construct a comprehensive firm-level textual corpus (FT corpus) spanning the period from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2022. This corpus includes multiple datasets, encompassing firm disclosures, analyst reports, news reports, and social media posts, further categorized by their sources:

• Firm Disclosures: This dataset consists of firm announcements, annual and quarterly reports, and investor Q&A sessions, collected from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database and the WIND database. It covers 5,000 firms with a total of 1,071,464 records.

• News Reports: We gather over 3,327,580 news reports related to more than 4,500 firms from the Huike database and CSMAR database. These news reports are from 13

major media sources, which we classify as official, semi-official, and unofficial, following An et al. (2022) and Hong et al. (2023). $ ^{33} $

• Analyst Reports: This collection comprises 568,468 analyst reports covering roughly 4,500 firms. The data are sourced from the WIND database, the CSMAR database, and manually collected from the Luobo Investment Research website.

• Social Media: The dataset includes 285,080,647 posts from GUBA, which is China's largest retail investor stock market forum.

For notational simplicity, we refer to all firm disclosures, news reports, analyst reports, and social media posts as articles. In preparing the FT corpus, we exclude articles without a verifiable publication date and articles that do not reference a specific company. Consistent with Sautner et al. (2023), we also exclude firm-month observations with articles totaling fewer than 100 in that month to ensure data reliability. Our final sample consists of 254,826,060 articles, pertaining to 3,994 publicly-traded companies. As we highlighted in Section 3, our algorithm can comprehensively identify all relevant topics within a single article if the article (e.g., a quarterly report) contains multiple topics.

## 5 Properties of the Misinformation Measure

In this section, we evaluate the properties of our misinformation measure. In addition, we explore the relationship between misinformation and disagreement, and we find that misinformation can significantly predict disagreement, but not the other way around.

### 5.1 Misinformation during publicly disclosed misinformation events

In this subsection, we use recorded misinformation-related events that are disclosed by either regulators or firms as an external benchmark to validate our misinformation measure. We conduct validation at two levels. At the text (piece) level, we show that MISI is significantly higher for statements that are later officially clarified as rumors or sanctioned as

fraud than for other statements. At the firm level, we find that MISI registers significantly elevated values around these events, consistent with a build-up and subsequent resolution of misinformation.

To this end, we construct a dataset of disclosed company events associated with misinformation. The event data is sourced from two primary channels: (i) company announcements of rumor clarifications, which are obtained from the Chinese Research Data Services (CN-RDS) database, and (ii) penalty announcements on firm fraud, which are issued by the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC). For both sources, we apply a keyword-based filter targeting terms indicative of misinformation, such as “falsified records,” “major discrepancies,” “misleading statements,” and “inconsistencies with facts.” We retain only events with clearly defined occurrence months and manually verify each event to ensure accuracy. Using this procedure, we identify 1,079 misinformation-related events involving 537 firms that are covered in our corpus. $ ^{35} $

Text-level validation We first validate MISI at the text level using these misinformation-related events. Based on these events, we construct a candidate-misinformation set consisting of pieces that are closely aligned with claims officially identified as false or misleading, and a baseline set of other pieces. $ ^{36} $ We then compare average piece-level MISI between the candidate-misinformation and baseline sets.

Specifically, for each event we (i) extract the allegedly false claim(s) from the official document and normalize them into one or more declarative query sentences, (ii) encode the query sentences and all same-firm, same-day pieces in the embedding space, $ ^{37} $ and (iii)

compute semantic similarity. Strategy 1 defines the candidate-misinformation set as the top five pieces per event by similarity among those whose similarity exceeds a pre-specified threshold of 0.9; if fewer than five pieces exceed the threshold, all qualifying pieces are included. The baseline set consists of pieces released by the same firm in the same calendar month of the previous year. Strategy 2 keeps the same candidate definition as strategy 1 but defines the baseline set as pieces released by the same firm in the same calendar month over the previous three years. Strategy 3 defines the candidate-misinformation set as the single piece with the highest similarity to the extracted false claim for each event, and uses pieces released by the same firm in the same calendar month of the previous year as the baseline. Strategy 4 maintains the top-1 candidate definition but extends the baseline window to pieces released by the same firm in the same calendar month over the previous three years. $ ^{38} $ We then compare average piece-level MISI between the candidate-misinformation and baseline sets.

Firm-level dynamics We begin our analysis by examining the frequency of disclosed misinformation events in relation to firms' MISI levels. We find that 89% of the events occur when a firm's MISI is above its historical average level, and over 70% occur when the firm's MISI exceeds the 90th percentile of its historical distribution. Moreover, when firms are grouped into monthly terciles based on their MISI scores, those in the highest

tercile consistently record more misinformation events than those in the lower two groups. To account for potential confounding factors, we estimate panel regressions in which event dummies—representing different types of misinformation events—are regressed on the firm's MISI. The results, reported in Appendix Table B.3, confirm that MISI is highly responsive to the occurrence of disclosed misinformation events.

To further sharpen our test, we explore the differences in the two types of publicly disclosed events, rumors and frauds. A key distinction between rumor events and fraud events lies in their reporting lag. Rumor events are typically clarified quickly by firm—often within one month on average—whereas fraud events require longer regulatory investigation and are disclosed with greater delay. Therefore, we expect the dynamics of MISI to differ between these two event types. In particular, while MISI should respond to both types of events, fraud events should be associated with a prolonged period of misinformation until eventual disclosure.

To see this, we conduct an event study analysis that measures the dynamic response of MISI to the occurrence of misinformation events. Specifically, we estimate the following regression model:

 $$ \begin{aligned}MISI_{i,t}&=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=2}^{5}\gamma_{j}\mathbb{I}_{i,t-j}+\sum_{k=0}^{5}\alpha_{k}\mathbb{I}_{i,t+k}\\&+\eta\times Firm Characteristics+FEs+\epsilon_{i,t}\end{aligned} $$ 

Here,  $  \text{MISI}_{i,t}  $ represents the misinformation measure for firm  $  i  $ at time  $  t  $, and  $  \mathbb{I}_{i,t-j}  $ and  $  \mathbb{I}_{i,t+k}  $ are indicator variables for  $  j  $ months before and  $  k  $ months after the event, respectively. We include four pre-event months  $  (j = 2, \ldots, 5)  $, the event month  $  (k = 0)  $, and five post-event months  $  (k = 1 \text{ to } k = 5)  $, omitting the first lag  $  (j = 1)  $ as the reference period.  $  \text{Firm}  $ Characteristics includes a comprehensive set of control variables related to corporate governance, balance sheet, and financial risk attributes that may influence a firm's susceptibility to misinformation.  $  \gamma_j  $ and  $  \alpha_k  $ capture the average changes in MISI before and after the event,

relative to the omitted baseline period. All regressions include event and calendar month fixed effects.

### 5.2 Misinformation and disclosure quality

Timely and accurate disclosure of corporate information is crucial in mitigating information asymmetry and enhancing the overall informational environment (Cookson and Niessner, 2020; Huang et al., 2021). Companies with high-quality disclosure practices are better positioned to limit the spread and influence of inaccurate or misleading content. Therefore,

we would expect firms with higher disclosure quality to exhibit lower levels of MISI.

To measure firm disclosure quality, we use the annual disclosure quality grades issued by the regulators, the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges. $ ^{39} $ These grades are categorized as A, B, C, and D, corresponding to excellent, good, fair, and poor, respectively. We construct an indicator variable, IDQ, which equals one if a firm receives an “excellent” (A) or “good” (B), and zero otherwise. We run panel regressions to investigate the relationship between IDQ and MISI. Because disclosure grades are updated annually, we aggregate the monthly MISI data into annual measures. Specifically, for each firm-year, we compute both the average and median MISI, using each as a dependent variable in separate regression specifications.

### 5.3 Misinformation and real-name registration policy

In this section, we study how the misinformation measure responds to an exogenous shift in the information environment. Specifically, we focus on the enactment of China’s Cybersecurity Law (CSL) in June 2017, which mandates real-name registration for social media users. By raising the legal risk associated with spreading false information, the policy is expected to curb misinformation—particularly on platforms popular among retail investors. $ ^{40} $

Thus, we expect firms with a higher proportion of retail investors to exhibit a pronounced decline in measured misinformation following the introduction of real-name registration.

To test this hypothesis, we classify firms into two groups based on whether their pre-CSL share of retail investors is above or below the median (mean). Our empirical specification is as follows:

 $$ \begin{aligned}MISI_{i,t}&=\alpha+\beta_{1}HighRetail_{i,t}\times Post_{t}+\beta_{2}HighRetail_{i,t}\\&\quad+\beta_{3}Post_{t}+\eta\times Firm Characteristics_{i,t}+FEs+\varepsilon_{i,t}.\end{aligned} $$ 

Here, HighRetail $ _{i,t} $ is an indicator for whether a firm belongs to the high-retail-investor group, and Post $ _{t} $ is an indicator for the periods following the enactment of CSL, and the coefficient  $ \beta_{1} $ captures the differential change in MISI for high-retail-investor firms after the policy, relative to low-retail-investor firms. ### 5.4 The relationship between misinformation and disagreement

Misinformation and disagreement are related but different phenomena. While misinformation refers to misleading and false statements, disagreement primarily reflects differences in subjective interpretation or belief. In this section, we investigate their empirical relationship. In the process, we also introduce a novel measure of disagreement, constructed based on subjective opinions, emotional expressions, and forward-looking predictions in the corpus.

The literature offers several proxies for measuring disagreement, such as analyst forecast dispersion (e.g., Diether et al., 2002), trading volume around earnings announcements (e.g., Kandel and Pearson, 1995), and abnormal short interest (e.g., Karpoff and Lou, 2010). More recently, Cookson and Niessner (2020) and Cookson and Niessner (2023) propose text-based disagreement measures using the dispersion of sentiment (bullish vs. bearish tone) in social media posts. Building on this approach, we construct a more comprehensive firm-level disagreement measure (DISA) based on subjective opinions, emotional expressions, and forward-looking predictions in the corpus, which are excluded when computing our misinformation measure. Specifically, we first group these texts into comparable information sets and calculate pairwise semantic similarity using embeddings as in Section 3.2.3. We then compute the standard deviation of these similarity scores within each set and aggregate these to obtain a firm-level measure of disagreement. Full construction details are provided in Appendix A.5. $ ^{41} $

Table 6 presents our main findings. Panel A reports the effect of current misinforma-

The results show that higher MISI significantly predicts greater disagreement in the future, and this effect is robust across multiple forecast horizons. In contrast, Panel B examines the reverse relationship and finds that current disagreement does not predict future levels of MISI.

## 6 What Drives Misinformation?

In this section, we explore firm characteristics that may drive misinformation. We find that firms with weak balance sheets and poor governance structures have higher average levels of misinformation. We also show that misinformation tends to increase around major corporate events.

Firm characteristics and misinformation We first examine whether and how firm characteristics are related to the degree of misinformation. Following Fan et al. (2025) and Li et al. (2023b), we focus on firm characteristics related to balance sheet, financial risk, and corporate governance structure.

Specifically, we estimate the following regression specification at a quarterly frequency:

 $$ MISI_{i,q}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}Firm~Characteristics_{i,q}+FEs+\epsilon_{i,q}. $$ 

Here, Firm Characteristics $ _{i,q} $ includes a set of firm-specific characteristics in quarter q, while MISI $ _{i,q} $ represents the average monthly misinformation in quarter q. The regression is estimated based on quarterly data due to the availability of firm characteristics. Table 7 reports the results.

Major corporate events and misinformation Having established the relationship between firm characteristics and misinformation, we now examine whether the occurrence of major corporate events can drive misinformation. Indeed, during significant corporate events, there is likely to be a surge in information dissemination and speculation. This creates an environment ripe for the spread of misinformation, as stakeholders may attempt to influence market perceptions and reactions. Additionally, the complexity and uncertainty surrounding these events can lead to misunderstandings and the unintentional spread of inaccurate information.

## 7 Misinformation, Investor Behavior, and Price Dynamics

The flow of information is a key driver of investor behavior and market dynamics. As such, misinformation is also expected to meaningfully influence investor perceptions and reactions. In this section, we demonstrate that a higher degree of misinformation is strongly associated with elevated trading volume. Importantly, this relationship varies by investor type:

small and medium-sized trades are characterized by net buying, while large trades exhibit net selling, suggesting divergent responses to misinformation across market participants. We further show that misinformation contributes to mispricing and has strong predictive power for future downside risk, as measured by maximum drawdown and crash risk.

Misinformation and trading activity We begin by examining whether higher levels of misinformation lead to increased trading volume. Investors monitor information that may impact stock prices, making them sensitive to news that signal potential opportunities or risks, even when such information is misleading. As a result, elevated misinformation may prompt more frequent and aggressive trading activity, as investors seek to capitalize on perceived opportunities or mitigate potential risks.

To capture trading activity, we follow Booker et al. (2023) and construct an adjusted turnover variable defined as  $ AdjTrade_{i,t} = Turn_{i,t} - mean(Turn_{i,t-15:t-4}) $, where  $ Turn_{i,t} $ is the turnover for firm i in month t, and the benchmark mean is computed over the preceding 12 months, excluding the most recent three months to avoid mechanical correlation. We regress this variable on our misinformation measure to assess the impact of misinformation on trading behavior.

Panel A of Table 9 shows that higher levels of misinformation are significantly associated with increased trading activity. This relationship remains robust after controlling for various fixed effects, as well as lagged adjusted turnover, disagreement, and a comprehensive set of firm characteristics. The table further demonstrates that misinformation also predicts higher trading activity in the subsequent month.

both the search volume on Baidu.com (the Chinese equivalent of Google) and the search volume on 13 major stock trading and stock broker apps.

Next, we examine whether the impact of misinformation on trading activity varies across investor types. We hypothesize that retail and small investors are generally more susceptible to misinformation, whereas large and institutional investors are better equipped to identify misinformation (Tan et al., 2024; Bogouslavsky et al., 2024). This heterogeneity in information processing may lead to divergent trading behaviors in response to elevated levels of misinformation.

To test this hypothesis, we classify trades by order size, under the assumption that larger trades are more likely to originate from sophisticated investors (Hvidkjaer, 2008; Jiang et al., 2022). Specifically, trades are categorized as small (less than 40,000 CNY), medium (40,000–1 million CNY), and large (above 1 million CNY). $ ^{42} $ For each group, we compute buy-sell imbalance, denoted as OIB, where higher values indicate stronger net buying. $ ^{43} $ We then regress OIB on our misinformation measure, controlling for firm and lagged market characteristics and various fixed effects.

Panel B of Table 9 presents the results. We find that small and medium trades exhibit net buying in response to elevated misinformation, both contemporaneously and in the subsequent month. In contrast, large trades exhibit significant net selling in response to misinformation. This divergence supports the notion that less-sophisticated small investors are more likely to buy into misinformation, whereas sophisticated investors tend to sell when misinformation is high, potentially capitalizing on the misinformed behavior of smaller market participants.

Misinformation and price dynamics We next examine whether misinformation contributes to mispricing. To do so, we follow Borochin and Yang (2017) and apply the decomposition methodology of Rhodes-Kropf et al. (2005) to construct a mispricing measure (MISVAL). This measure isolates the firm-specific component of the market-to-book ratio after adjusting for industry effects and long-term valuation benchmarks, and it captures the extent to which a firm's market valuation deviates from fundamentals. A positive value of MISVAL indicates that a firm is overvalued relative to its fundamentals, while a negative value reflects undervaluation. We also use an indicator variable which equals one if MISVAL > 0 and zero otherwise.

Panel A of Table 10 shows that firms with higher levels of misinformation are significantly more likely to be overvalued relative to their fundamentals, suggesting that misinformation contributes to mispricings in financial markets. This effect is expected in the context of the Chinese stock market, where short-selling restrictions constrain arbitrage activity. In such an environment, certain market participants may have incentives to disseminate positive misinformation to inflate stock prices—ultimately resulting in subsequent price reversals.

The negative coefficients reveal a clear return reversal pattern—stocks with high levels of misinformation tend to underperform in the subsequent months.

In short, our evidence shows that firms with higher levels of misinformation are more likely to be overvalued relative to their fundamentals, and that such pricing distortions are followed by significant return reversals.

## 8 Conclusion

Measuring and understanding misinformation, an increasingly critical issue in financial markets, poses a significant challenge for researchers. We address this challenge by introducing a systematic framework to quantify firm-level misinformation, grounded in the principles of information consistency and collective wisdom. Our approach leverages recent advances in AI and LLMs and is applied to a large-scale corpus of 254.8 million textual materials from diverse information sources.

Our exploration of the drivers of misinformation reveals that firms with weaker balance sheets and poorer governance structures tend to exhibit higher levels of misinformation. Moreover, we find that major corporate events, such as financing operations, corporate splits, and mergers and acquisitions, are associated with elevated levels of misinformation. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring and managing misinformation particularly during periods of heightened corporate activity.

We also show that misinformation has significant implications for financial markets. Specifically, the circulation of misinformation strongly influences investor behavior and stock return dynamics. Moreover, the degree of misinformation exhibits strong predictive power for future stock drawdowns and crash risk. Overall, our framework offers a valuable tool for measuring misinformation, enabling researchers, practitioners, and regulators to better understand the source and impact of misinformation in financial markets.

## APPENDIX

### A Additional Technical Details

In this section, we supplement additional technical details of the framework for measuring misinformation, detailing how we preprocess text, identify objectively verifiable texts, and categorize text into comparable subsets.

### A.1 Data preprocessing

We begin with a diverse array of raw data sources, including firm announcements, social media posts, news articles, and analyst reports. Data from social media and news articles are typically in plain text format, while firm announcements and analyst reports are in PDF format. Our preprocessing steps include the following:

Converting all PDF files to text. We utilize PyMuPDF, $ ^{45} $ an open-source PDF toolkit in Python, to extract structured information from PDF documents and transform it into text. PyMuPDF is known for its efficiency and comprehensive functionality in handling PDF files, including text extraction and text property extraction. The converted text documents are formatted into a sentence segmentation module, inclusive of newline characters and other symbols. During this phase, we perform various normalization and cleaning processes, including removing headers and footers, discarding tables, and eliminating inherent text formatting. For the content of analyst reports, we only extract the summary sections. Ultimately, we form 290,048,159 raw articles. After preprocessing, as described in Section 4, we have 254,826,060 articles related to the sample firms.

Chunking segment. Following the methodology outlined in Li et al. (2023b), we segment all text files using their inherent paragraph structure and headings to maintain the seman-

tic integrity necessary for subsequent model processing. $ ^{46} $ Specifically, we initially identify headings based on textual conventions and then divide the text into sections accordingly. Each section is subsequently split into paragraphs based on newline characters. For paragraphs exceeding the character limit, we further segment them into semantic units based on punctuation marks, setting a character limit of 200. For these segments, we perform a series of denoising operations. Considering the low signal-to-noise ratio in social media, which contains a high volume of irrelevant or minimal information sentences, we eliminate segments shorter than ten characters. Additionally, for news articles, we retain segments with high relevance to companies by checking if company names, abbreviations, or codes appear in the surrounding text. We discard segments that are excessively templated. This segmentation process results in a total of 141,270,419 distinct segments.

### A.2 Identifying objectively verifiable texts

This section provides additional details regarding Section 3.2.1. We describe the generative LLM-based annotation process, BERT model fine-tuning, evaluation results, and illustrative examples of objectively verifiable versus subjective texts.

In the first step, we use the generative LLM (ChatGPT-4o) to annotate a representative stratified random sample of 40,000 texts. ChatGPT-4o is programmatically prompted via API with a carefully designed chain-of-thought instruction, encouraging detailed semantic reasoning to classify each text as objectively verifiable or not.

You are a financial text analysis expert with strong skills in capturing subtle semantic details. You will be given paragraphs extracted from texts related to listed companies. Your goal is to determine whether the text is objectively verifiable. Please strictly follow the steps and requirements below.

Definition: A statement is objectively verifiable if it makes a concrete, specific assertion about an entity, event, or attribute that can be confirmed or refuted using external evidence or reliable sources. Subjective opinions,

emotional expressions, and speculative predictions are not considered objectively verifiable.

Step-by-step annotation instructions:

1. Summary: Provide a neutral, factual summary of the paragraph's main content (no more than 20 words).

2. Verifiability analysis (with reasoning): Analyze whether the statement is objectively verifiable, referring to the above definition. Clearly explain your reasoning—identify which parts (if any) are factual and verifiable, and which are subjective or unverifiable.

3. Final label: Output your conclusion as "Verifiable": "Yes" or "No", along with a brief justification.

Input: {content}

Output format (JSON):

{
    "Summary": "A factual summary of the paragraph.",
    "Reasoning": "Briefly describe your analysis and reasoning process.",
    "Verifiable": {
        "Result": "Yes" or "No",
        "Justification": "Justify your label with reference to the above definition, including which parts (if any) are factual and verifiable, and which are subjective or unverifiable."
    }
}

In the second step, we fine-tune a pre-trained BERT model (Devlin et al., 2018) on this annotated dataset. BERT's core strength lies in its use of bidirectional self-attention, which allows the model to capture contextual information from both directions in a sentence. For an input sequence of tokens  $ w_{1}, w_{2}, ..., w_{n} $, each token is embedded as a vector, and the model computes attention weights as:

 $$  Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V $$ 

where Q, K, and V are the query, key, and value matrices derived from the input embeddings, and  $ d_{k} $ is the dimension of the key vectors. This mechanism allows the model to aggregate information from all positions in the sequence, focusing on semantically relevant content.

During pre-training, BERT is optimized with masked-language-modeling and next-sentence-prediction objectives. For classification, BERT prepends a special [CLS] token to each input. The final hidden state of this [CLS] token serves as a holistic embedding for the entire text, summarizing its overall semantics.

We add a binary classification layer on top of the BERT model. The classification head takes the final hidden state of the [CLS] token as input and passes it through a single fully connected layer that produces two logits. A softmax function transforms these scores into class probabilities, assigning each text to the class with the higher probability. Using a random 30% of the data set as a test sample, the classifier achieves a F1 score of 91.48%, calculated as:

 $$  Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, $$ 

 $$  Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}, $$ 

 $$  F1score=2\times\frac{Precision\times Recall}{Precision+Recall} $$ 

where TP, FP, TN, and FN denote true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, respectively.

In the third step, to ensure the robustness of our classifier, we randomly select and re-annotate 500 texts using ChatGPT-4o with the original prompt. We observe an extremely high agreement rate of 98.2% (491 out of 500), confirming that the fine-tuned model closely replicates the semantic reasoning of the generative LLM. To achieve these steps, we employ a machine learning model specifically designed and trained for this task.

We utilize the same foundational pre-trained large language model (LLM) and text processing model for both information classification and extraction, ensuring consistency across our model's logic and processing. Pre-trained LLMs are now the foundation of almost all natural language processing tasks, converting text into fixed-dimension vectors. We use the ERNIE model, which builds on an architecture similar to BERT but is particularly optimized for Chinese text (Sun et al., 2020). ERNIE is trained on a vast corpus of Chinese knowledge graphs and text, including encyclopedic professional knowledge, news information, and forum dialogues in various language styles. It has shown superiority over BERT in several Chinese text classification tasks, proving more effective in providing contextually relevant embeddings (Sun et al., 2020). We capture representations of each sentence in a 768-dimensional vector space, regardless of varying lengths.

A standard practice is to fine-tune the pre-trained model on a specific task using labeled datasets, adjusting the model parameters (Chen et al., 2022). However, given the context of our research, we adopt a few-shot learning framework to mitigate the need for extensive training datasets. This approach utilizes a smaller number of high-quality training samples, effectively leveraging the pre-trained model's general language understanding capabilities while adapting to task-specific nuances.

Specifically, we use the Unified Semantic Matching (USM) model proposed by Lou et al. (2023), a widely employed and highly effective algorithm designed for diverse text-based semantic classification and information extraction tasks. As of February 2023, this model ranks first in ZeroCLUE and FewCLUE competition tasks. This model vectorizes both user-defined labels and text, then constructs a shared semantic space for labels and

text through Directed-Token-Linking (DTL) and performs semantic matching. This process involves linking tokens within the text (Token-Token Linking, TTL), linking labels to text tokens (Label-Token Linking, LTL), and linking tokens to labels (Token-Label Linking, TLL). Each linking score is computed as follows, ultimately determining the semantic matching relationship:

 $$ s_{\mathrm{T T L}}(t_{i},t_{j})=\mathrm{F F N N}_{\mathrm{T T L}}^{\mathrm{l e f t}}(h_{t_{i}})^{T}\cdot R_{j-i}\cdot\mathrm{F F N N}_{\mathrm{T T L}}^{\mathrm{r i g h t}}(h_{t_{j}}), $$ 

 $$ s_{\mathrm{L T L}}(l_{i},t_{j})=\mathrm{F F N N}_{\mathrm{L T L}}^{\mathrm{l a b e l}}(h_{l_{i}})^{T}\cdot R_{j-i}\cdot\mathrm{F F N N}_{\mathrm{L T L}}^{\mathrm{t e x t}}(h_{t_{j}}), $$ 

 $$ s_{\mathrm{T L L}}(t_{i},l_{j})=\mathrm{F F N N}_{\mathrm{T L L}}^{\mathrm{t e x t}}(h_{t_{i}})^{T}\cdot R_{j-i}\cdot\mathrm{F F N N}_{\mathrm{T L L}}^{\mathrm{l a b e l}}(h_{l_{j}}). $$ 

Here,  $ h_{t_{i}} $,  $ h_{t_{j}} $,  $ h_{l_{i}} $, and  $ h_{l_{j}} $ represent the embeddings of tokens and labels, while  $ R_{j-i} $ is a relative position encoding matrix. FFNNs (Feed-forward neural networks) process these embeddings to calculate the linking scores. For this combined pre-trained large language model and unified semantic matching model, we construct training sets for both classification and extraction tasks separately.

For the information classification task, we handle a multi-label assignment involving eight core topics plus an "others" category. The goal of the extraction task is to identify and describe relevant entities within the text. We randomly extract 600 samples from each data source and use the same batch for annotations. Initially, we label the training sets for both tasks using ChatGPT-4. We employ a chain-of-thought prompting strategy, as detailed in Li et al. (2023b). The prompt used is as follows:

a. The chosen topics must reflect the company's behavior or status and not be generic. If the text is too general, classify it as ``other."

c. The text should be classified by its core topic.

3) Extract the core specific described entities (such as products, etc.) from the text, as well as descriptive content and stance (positive, negative, or neutral). Please respond in the following format:

``Topic: [Your Answer]''

``Entity: [Your Answer]''

Subsequently, we ensure the accuracy and comprehensiveness of labels through manual annotation. We then divide the dataset into training, validation, and testing sets in an 80/10/10 ratio and optimize the model based on the Micro-F1 metric.

The trained classification model achieves good performance, with a Micro-F1 score of 92.04 on the test set. For the information extraction task, we similarly use the F1 score as the optimization goal, ultimately achieving a score of 78.36.

We perform clustering on the extracted entities to facilitate canonicalization. For this purpose, we employ the hierarchical clustering technique, a widely used method well-documented in Ward Jr (1963); Hastie et al. (2009). For monthly text data slices from each firm, we use the scikit-learn library (https://github.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn) in Python to assign the same cluster ID to synonymous entities. This approach ensures that entities with similar meanings are grouped together, enhancing the analysis' consistency and reliability.

### A.5 Measuring firm-level disagreement

We now explain how we construct the firm-level disagreement measure (DISA) based on subjective statements across various information sources. Specifically, subjective statements refer to the residual corpus after filtering out all objectively verifiable statements (see Section 3.2.1), so that DISA is constructed from subjective opinions, emotional expressions,

and forward-looking predictions.

We first group all subjective texts into comparable information sets according to their topic and entity labels, using the same topic classification and entity extraction procedures described in Section 3. We also get the embedding semantic vector for each piece of information as in Section 3.

We then calculate the pairwise cosine similarity between all texts within the same comparable information set:

 $$ s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\times\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}}, $$ 

where  $ \|\cdot\|_{2} $ denotes the Euclidean norm. The standard deviation of these similarity scores is computed to quantify the degree of disagreement within each set:

 $$ \mathrm{DISA}_{g}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{g}(N_{g}-1)/2}\sum_{i<j}\left(s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})-\bar{s}_{g}\right)^{2}}. $$ 

Here,  $ N_{g} $ is the number of texts in group g and  $ \bar{s}_{g} $ is the mean pairwise similarity. Finally, we aggregate the disagreement scores pertaining to the same firm to obtain a firm-level measure.

### B.1 Misinformation and public response

Drawing on the insights of Dyck et al. (2010) and Fan et al. (2025), $ ^{47} $ we posit that an increase in MISI should correspond with heightened public skepticism about a firm's authenticity. In other words, a significant amount of public discussions questioning a firm's veracity strongly indicate the presence of misinformation.

To test this hypothesis, we construct a lexicon focused on terms related to the concept of falsehood. $ ^{48} $ We use keyword matching against this lexicon across the FT corpus (excluding firm announcements and firm quarterly and annual reports) to measure public skepticism:

 $$ P S_{i,t}=\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{I}_{i,t}\right|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{i,t}}\delta(p,\mathcal{B}). $$ 

Here,  $ \mathcal{I}_{i,t} $ is the information set for firm  $ i $ at time  $ t $, and  $ |\mathcal{I}_{i,t}| $ represents the number of pieces of information.  $ \mathcal{B} $ represents the “falsehood” lexicon set, and  $ \delta(p, \mathcal{B}) $ is a binary function defined as:

 $$ \delta(p,\mathcal{B})=\begin{cases}{1}&{\mathrm{i f~a n y~w o r d~i n~}p\;\mathrm{m a t c h e s~a n y~k e y w o r d~i n~}\mathcal{B},}\\ {}&{}\\ {0}&{\mathrm{i f~o t h e r w i s e}.}\\ \end{cases} $$ 

In addition to the ratio, we also use  $ \log(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{i,t}}\delta(p,\mathcal{B})) $ as an alternative measure of public skepticism.

Panel A of Table B.1 presents these results. We find that higher levels of misinformation about a firm significantly correlate with increased public skepticism about the firm, evidenced by more discussions questioning the firm's authenticity. Additionally, we consider public attention measures, which include the volume of searches on search engines and the search volume of investors on 13 major stock trading and stock broker apps, as a complementary indicator of the public's concern about the firm's credibility. Panel B of Table B.1 reports these results. An increase in skepticism regarding a firm's authenticity is expected to drive more searches related to the firm.

### C Validating the FT corpus

This section investigates the validity of the FT corpus. Theoretically, the diversity and independence of data sources are critical. As discussed in Surowiecki (2005) and Allen et al. (2021), the quality of an information set hinges not merely on volume but on the variety and autonomy of the sources. For instance, while each analyst might represent an individual data source, they are not entirely independent, owing to possible herding behaviors within the analyst community that could undermine collective wisdom (Kremer et al., 2014; Prelec et al., 2017). Additionally, reducing information redundancy is essential. Excessive or biased redundancy can obscure meaningful signals, leading to noise amplification rather than enhanced understanding (Ash, 1990; Schmidt, 2020; Xu et al., 2021).

Thus, we quantitatively assess the effectiveness of our data sources. Fedyk and Hodson (2023) define old news as news that has some overlap with historical news and discuss the informational value of "old news." We apply a similar principle to evaluate the contributions of each data source, known as Information Gain Analysis (IGA). This analysis helps to quantify the incremental information each new data brings to our corpus. Information gain diminishes as the dataset approaches an "information saturation point," beyond which additional data fails to significantly enhance content value.

Specifically, IGA is a quantitative approach derived from information theory, widely used in machine learning for feature selection (Li et al., 2017), and it involves several key steps:

Preprocessing. Prior to calculating information gain, textual data must undergo preprocessing, including the removal of stop words and segmentation of the text into words, to facilitate effective analysis of words.

Entropy calculation. Entropy, denoted as  $ H(D) $, quantifies the uncertainty within the dataset D and is calculated using the equation:

 $$ H(D)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(x_{i})\log_{2}p(x_{i}), $$ 

where  $ p(x_i) $ is the frequency of occurrence of the  $ i $-th word in dataset  $ D $. We then introduce an additional dataset  $ D_{\text{new}} $ and compute the entropy of the combined datasets  $ H(D \cup D_{\text{new}}) $.

Information gain calculation. Given the textual nature of our corpus, we adopt a simplified version of standard calculation. Information gain here is calculated as the difference between the original entropy and the combined entropy:

 $$ \Delta H=H\big(D\cup D_{\mathrm{n e w}}\big)-H\big(D\big). $$ 

This metric assesses the reduction in uncertainty and thus the value added by the new data to D. A decrease in information gain below a specified threshold (here, 0.001) suggests a stable contribution level from the data source.

We apply IGA based on principles of source diversity, independence, and minimal redundancy to ensure the validity of our corpus. First, we select data groups based on the interactions of three primary financial market participants—firms, investors, and financial intermediaries to ensure diversity. Our selection includes nine distinct groups: firm announcements, investor Q&A sessions, analyst reports, social media posts, governmental newspapers, mainstream economic and financial newspapers, online financial platforms, and financial information websites. To validate stability, we randomly initialize the data groups and iteratively add new groups in a randomized order. Across 50 experiments, we calculate the average information gain and entropy at each iteration as shown in Panels (a) and (b) of Figure C.1. The results show that the corpus's stability is achieved with the inclusion of the nine data groups.
