## ABSTRACT

We use textual analysis of mandatory accounting filings to develop firm-level, time-varying measures of exposure to individual government agencies including the Securities Exchange Commission (SEC) and Internal Revenue Service (IRS). The measures vary predictably across industries and with agency-specific events such as the Sarbanes-Oxley Act at the SEC and budget cuts at the IRS. The measures positively relate to undisclosed agency investigations and financial statement downloads. Firms' total exposure across government agencies negatively relates to their profitability, consistent with exposure to government agencies imposing net costs. Consistent with a causal interpretation of these results, the positive stock market reaction to the surprise election of Donald Trump, who promised to reduce the power of government agencies, positively varies with firms' exposure to government agencies. As initial applications of our measures, we demonstrate that expanded SEC oversight increases firms' stock liquidity and reduced IRS oversight decreases firms' effective tax rates.

## 1. Introduction

we develop and validate measures of firm exposure to government agencies and examine how they relate to firm outcomes. A large literature in economics, business, and political science examines how government actions, including specific laws and regulations, affect firms. This literature largely studies singular regulations and laws, and occasionally regulators, or differences in regulation. $ ^{1} $ While this literature provides important insights, it does not directly explore the potential effects of government agencies. This omission is surprising given the considerable scope and powers of government agencies. For example, as noted in National Latino Media Coalition v. Federal Communications Commission, 816 F.2d 785, 788 (D.C. Cir. 1987), “When Congress delegates rulemaking

authority to an agency, and the agency adopts legislative rules, the agency stands in the place of Congress and makes law."

Given the potential importance of government agencies, we develop and validate firm- and agency-specific measures of exposure to government agencies based on firms' mandatory accounting disclosures. We then examine how the measures relate to corporate outcomes such as profitability. We broadly define government agencies as departments, agencies, or bureaus that derive supervisory or rulemaking powers from the federal government. Examples of government agencies include the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Department of Justice (DOJ), and the IRS.

Prior work examining government agencies focuses on specific actions by individual regulators, such as SEC investigations or IRS audits (Blackburne et al., 2021; Hoopes et al., 2012). However, these studies do not explore differences in the effects of many government agencies on firms. Because our measures of exposure to government agencies vary across firms and agencies over time, they can overcome these challenges.

We construct our measures of exposure to government agencies using textual analysis of public firms' annual financial statements. By SEC mandate, public firm financial statements include discussions of exposure to risk factors and descriptions of management strategy, compliance with government mandates, and factors affecting financial performance. These discussions frequently explain how government agencies change, or may change, the firm's operations and how the firm's management intends to navigate exposure to government agencies. We search firms' annual financial statements for references to specific agencies in conjunction with agency action words (e.g., audit, compliance, etc.). Similar to the approach of Hoberg and Phillips (2016) and based on the guiding principle for text-based work propagated by Bae et al. (2023), a strength of our approach is its simplicity: government agencies have standardized titles and almost universally accepted acronyms (e.g., the IRS, SEC, FDA, etc.). These standardized titles and acronyms allow us to identify references to specific federal agencies with little potential measurement error or bias and in turn construct agency- and firm-specific measures of exposure to government agencies.

We validate the accuracy of the measures in several ways. Consistent with the increasing length of the Code of Federal Regulations, the sum of the measures of exposure to government agencies ("total" exposure to government agencies) increases over time. The measures of exposure to certain agencies are greater in certain industries, exactly as one would predict based on that agency's purview. For example, exposure to the FDA is greater in the pharmaceutical industry. Further, exposure to government agencies changes as anticipated around meaningful changes in regulation that increased the purview and authority of agencies. Following SOX, which increased the purview and authority of the SEC, our measure of firms' exposure to the SEC increases. Following a significant budget cut at the IRS our measure of firms' exposure to the IRS decreases. In addition to validating the accuracy of our measures, our SOX and IRS budget tests demonstrate how our measures reflect both changes in regulation and changes in agency funding.

To further validate our measures, we demonstrate that they capture undisclosed government agency attention after conditioning on industry-year and firm fixed effects. First, we show that our measure of SEC exposure relates to otherwise undisclosed SEC investigations identified ex post using Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests (Blackburne et al., 2021). $ ^{2} $ Second, we show that our measures of exposure to different government agencies relates to downloads of firm financial statements by the different agencies (e.g., our measure of EPA exposure relates to financial statement downloads by the EPA).

Having validated our measures of exposure to different agencies, we use them to explore the fundamental question of whether government agencies impose private net costs on firms. $ ^{3} $ We find a significant negative relation between total exposure to government agencies and pretax return on assets using specifications with and without year, industry-year, and firm fixed effects. In our most stringent specification including firm and industry-year fixed effects, we find that a one standard deviation increase in government agency exposure within an industry-year associates with a 1.8 percentage point reduction in pre-tax return on assets.

While the association between government agency exposure and firm profitability is suggestive, we recognize that other explanations may exist for this association. For example, agencies may target firms based on their profitability. In light of this and other potential alternative explanations, we use the surprise election of Donald Trump to President as a source of unexpected variation in market expectations about the future of government agencies. Donald Trump campaigned in part on decreasing the power and scope of government agencies (Belton and Graham, 2020; Crews, 2021; Popovich et al., 2019). Trump was not expected to win the election (Wagner et al., 2018b; Wagner et al., 2018a; Gaertner et al., 2020; Gallemore et al., 2021) and as a result his election win represents a discrete, unforeseen decrease in expected future government agency oversight. $ ^{4} $ Consistent with government agencies imposing costs on firms, we find that firms highly exposed to government agencies earned higher equity market returns around the Trump election relative to their less exposed counterparts.

Having documented that overall exposure to government agencies negatively relates to firm profitability, we next highlight the value of our agency-specific measures by investigating agency-specific events and outcomes. We examine how SOX and IRS budget cuts distinctly impacted firms' exposures to the SEC and IRS and, in turn, agency-specific outcomes at affected firms. We find that SOX relatively increased the SEC exposure of firms previously less regulated by the SEC, consistent with SOX expanding the purview of the SEC to less-regulated firms. Moreover, the stock liquidity of these firms subsequently increased, consistent with the SEC's goal of

maintaining orderly and efficient markets. We also find that the IRS budget cuts relatively decreased the IRS exposure of firms previously more regulated by the IRS, consistent with the budget cuts decreasing IRS scrutiny of already regulated firms. Moreover, the effective tax rates (ETRs) of these firms subsequently decreased, consistent with decreased IRS oversight allowing for more tax planning. In addition to highlighting the value of our agency-specific measures, these results highlight how our measure can capture agency-specific outcomes, including agency-specific benefits such as stock liquidity.

As another application of our measure, we explore potential interactions between government agencies and in how firms navigate exposure to different agencies (Charoenwong et al., 2023). We find that overall exposure to government agencies more negatively relates to profitability when government agencies are more fragmented (i.e., when overlapping regulators do not act in a complementary manner; Kalmenovitz et al., 2024). These results suggest that future work can use our measures to examine interactions between different regulators.

Finally, in light of recent developments in generative AI and large-language models, we compare the effectiveness of our dictionary-based method to a complex AI and large-language approach (e.g., Armstrong, 2023; Choi and Kim, 2023; de Kok, 2024; Kim et al., 2023). We use OpenAI's GPT 3.5 Turbo model to create an alternative measure of exposure to the SEC and assess how the new measure compares to our existing measure. We find no significant difference between the measures' abilities to predict undisclosed SEC investigations or 10-K downloads by the SEC. The similar performance of our measure and the generative AI-based measure suggests that, despite the substantial benefit of AI-based measures in many settings, the incremental benefit in our particular setting is not large (e.g., because of the highly structured nature of regulatory agency discussions).

We contribute to the literature by examining the relation between exposure to government agencies and corporate outcomes. One stream of related research studies the effects of individual regulations and cooperation among government agencies on capital market outcomes (Christensen et al., 2016; Duarte et al., 2008; Grout and Zalewska, 2006; Hail et al., 2018; Hutton et al., 2021; Illiev, 2010; Lang et al., 2020; Silvers, 2021; Silvers, 2021). A second stream examines how transparency regulations reallocate resources among firms (Bird et al., 2020; Breuer, 2021; Breuer and Breuer, 2020; Breuer et al., 2019; Glaeser and Omartian, 2022; Kim and Olbert, 2022; Kim and Valentine, 2021). A third stream examines the effects of political uncertainty or connections on corporate outcomes and decisions (Baker et al., 2016; Christensen et al., 2023; Christensen et al., 2023; Ferracuti et al., 2022; Godsell, 2021; Hassan et al., 2019; Hassan et al., 2019; Nagar et al., 2019; Wellman, 2017). We build on these literatures by developing and validating a firm-level measure of exposure to individual agencies and documenting how firm-level exposure to agencies relates to firm-level outcomes.

Our measures may also be useful to other researchers. Most directly, researchers interested in the effects of different government agencies, or wanting to control for the effects of government agencies, can benefit from our measures (Leuz, 2007). $ ^{5} $ Moreover, our measures can allow researchers to draw distinctions between aggregate, sector-level, and firm-level exposure to government agencies, as well as between different agencies and the interactions among them. Finally, the measures can allow researchers to draw deeper inferences in settings where firms' exposure to government agencies can moderate the effect of other economic forces.

Our paper also relates to a burgeoning literature on firm-level compliance and the operational costs of regulation (Calomiris et al., 2023; Kalmenovitz, 2023; Singla, 2023; Trebbi et al., 2023). This literature develops firm-level measures of the direct compliance costs of overall regulation (e.g., the costs of hiring compliance officers to comply with Congressional mandates). Our main contribution to this literature is developing firm-level measures of exposure to individual government agencies. We discuss this difference and other differences between our measures and firm-level measures of regulatory compliance costs in detail in Section 2 (e.g., we discuss how our measures can directly reflect benefits of regulation and changes in government agency budgets). Throughout our tests, we highlight distinctions between our measures and those of prior work, for example by demonstrating that our measures capture agency-specific actions, such as investigations, while prior measures generally do not. We conclude we capture a distinct dimension of the overall regulatory environment: exposure to government agencies.

## 2. Background

We broadly define government agencies as departments, agencies, or bureaus that derive supervisory or rulemaking powers from the federal government. $ ^{6} $ Similar to Hart (2009), we define exposure to government agencies to include potential or realized agency actions that directly or indirectly affect corporate behavior and outcomes. This definition is quite broad and encompasses government agencies' enforcement actions, rulemaking, and general oversight (e.g., SEC disclosure rules, FDA investigations, Federal Communications Commission [FCC] rulemaking, etc.). A firm can be more exposed to government agencies than other firms by being subject to a higher quantity of agency laws that each individually have an equal effect, or an equal quantity of agency laws that have a larger effect on the firm. However, our definition excludes government actions unrelated to government agencies, such as tariffs, elections, tax rates, etc. $ ^{7} $ Our definition is also broader than the focus of Armstrong (2023), which seeks to capture a very specific type of regulatory intervention (a firm currently undergoing IRS audit). We measure overall exposure to regulatory agencies, which includes the threat of an audit or investigation.

Government agencies try to overcome problems that result from monopolies, market failures, asymmetric information, and other undesirable outcomes and economic forces (Stiglitz, 1993). Government agencies allegedly correct these problems and protect

individual people in their various capacities (as investors, consumers, healthcare patients, breathers of air, etc.). While government agencies are meant to benefit society, they may do so by imposing private costs on the firms they oversee. Whether government agencies impose net costs on a particular firm depends on the agency's mandate and the relationship between the agency and the firm. For example, if firms would produce goods in lower-cost ways that pollute more in the absence of EPA oversight, the existence and involvement of the EPA may impose costs at firms, thereby reducing profits (even if potentially benefiting society).

However, the net cost of exposure to government agencies certainly varies by firm and in some cases may be negative (indicating a net benefit to a particular firm). For example, government agencies can benefit firms that capture them. Firms, through a variety of means including lobbying (e.g., Blau et al., 2013; Lambert, 2018) and hiring former agency employees (Hendricks et al., 2021), may seek to change oversight and rules in favor of their firm. Government agencies can also benefit firms and individuals by constraining managers' actions, preventing opportunistic behavior, or even by providing direct assistance (Armstrong and Glaeser, 2023; Haw et al., 2004; Silvers, 2021; Warfield et al., 1995). As such, government agencies can monitor managers and partially replace the monitoring role of managerial ownership or stakeholder oversight and thereby increase firm value (Warfield et al., 1995). Government agencies can also provide information to managers that helps them invest more efficiently and thereby increase firm value (Roychowdhury et al., 2019; Shroff, 2017). In total, exposure to government agencies could both benefit and harm firms, suggesting an ambiguous relation between exposure and firm outcomes.

Closely related to government agencies is government regulation, which includes broad government rules and laws. The literature on government regulation focuses on specific regulatory interventions, such as SOX (e.g., Zhang, 2007), Dodd-Frank (e.g., Dimitrov et al., 2015), or Regulation Fair Disclosure (Jorion et al., 2005), or that target certain firm aspects, such as corporate governance (Larcker et al., 2011), monopolies (Joskow, 2007), and entry (Klapper et al., 2006). However, examining individual regulatory interventions paints an incomplete picture of the overall effects of government regulation because regulations are frequently reactions to distinct economic events, are often promulgated directly by the federal government, and because government oversight may affect outcomes without the need for intervention (e.g., Hail et al., 2018). In light of these issues, Hassan et al. (2019) and Kalmenovitz (2023) develop firm-level, time-varying measures of exposure to political risk or government regulation.

Hassan et al. (2019) define political risks as “risks of a political nature” and use textual analysis of political science textbooks and newspaper articles to identify political discussions in conference calls. We measure a distinct construct from political risk. Our measures capture exposure to individual government agencies, while political risk is the overall risk of political instability or change due to many different political factors, very few of which directly relate to government agencies. For example, of the top 120 political topics Hassan et al. (2019) use to construct their measure of political risk, three are government agencies (the Federal Aviation Administration [FAA], EPA, and Federal Reserve). The other 117 include topics such as “the states,” “first amendment,” “public opinion,” and “the Taliban” which, while important, do not relate to government agencies.

More closely related to our measures of exposure to government agencies is the measure of Kalmenovitz (2023), which is based on the estimated paperwork burden imposed by regulations and hence directly captures the compliance costs of regulation. This innovative measure is useful for answering many questions that deal with the compliance costs of regulation in general, controlling for regulatory compliance costs, etc. The primary innovation of our measure is that it is regulatory agency-specific, which is important given the different purviews and powers of different government agencies (e.g., the FAA versus the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency).

We also note three other significant distinctions between our measure and the direct compliance costs measure of Kalmenovitz (2023). First, exposure to government agencies captures government agency actions, such as investigations, that are not reflected in direct compliance costs. $ ^{8} $ This distinction highlights how government agencies can take firm-specific actions, whereas regulation does not directly target individual firms. Second, compliance costs reflect costs, whereas exposure to agencies reflects costs and benefits. Third, exposure to government agencies reflects changes in government agency budgets, which can affect agency activity independently of any changes in regulation. Capturing budget-driven effects is increasingly important in America's partisan political climate. Without presidential assistance, Congress can affect agency budgets with a simple majority but can only pass regulations with a filibuster-proof super majority. Consequently, Congress increasingly affects policy change by funding or defunding agencies (Burns, 2024). $ ^{9} $

In total, exposure to government agencies and government regulation, and in particular the direct compliance costs of regulation, are related, but distinct concepts. Throughout our tests we highlight these distinctions, as well as the key innovation of our measure: regulatory agency-specificity.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1. Measures of exposure to government agencies

Exposure to government agencies is complex and potentially difficult to measure. Many prior studies measure firm regulation as an indicator variable based on industry membership (Baginski et al., 2002, 2004; Guan et al., 2020; Hutton et al., 2012; Kasznik and Lev, 1995). Governments may enact laws that target certain industries, but government agencies monitor and enforce compliance at the firm level based on firm-specific behavior and attributes. Consequently, industry-based measures of regulation ignore potentially important variation in exposure to government agencies within industries and firms. Additionally, some firms operate in multiple industries, further complicating the classification. Using a measure that varies by firm-year can address these challenges and allow researchers to draw deeper inferences.

To create a firm-specific, time-varying measure of exposure to different government agencies, we use 10-K disclosures of U.S. incorporated firms. Section 17(a)(2) of the Securities Act of 1933 prevents firms from disclosing misleading facts and omitting material facts from their financial statements. Thus, it is likely costly for firms to misrepresent 10-K discussions of the material effect agencies have on their outcomes and operations. Nonetheless, firms and managers may behave strategically with respect to disclosure, which is a limitation of using 10-Ks to measure exposure to government agencies. $ ^{10} $

An alternative approach to using 10-K disclosures is using conference call discussions, which are not audited, unavailable for many firms, and not mandatory (Calomiris et al., 2023). However, a benefit of using conference call discussions is that call participants can “force the issue” by asking questions about government agencies even if managers are otherwise not forthcoming. While we acknowledge that neither approach strictly dominates the other and that using conference call discussions may be more appropriate in other settings, we choose to use 10-K disclosures in our setting because of their greater availability and mandatory nature. An additional relative strength of 10-Ks in our setting is that in our experience conference call participants tend to only discuss new government agency oversight, while 10-Ks tend to discuss both ongoing and new oversight. Consequently, our 10-K based measure reflects the “stock” of exposure to government agencies, while conference call discussions only capture the “flow” of exposure to government agencies.

discussions of government agencies occur in several places in firms' 10-K filings. Item 1 Business and Item 1A Risk Factors are the most common sections for these discussions, but there are many others, including Item 3 Legal Proceedings and Note 13 Income Taxes. For this reason, we examine all disclosures made throughout the 10-K instead of focusing on a specific section. Appendix B provides several illustrative examples of 10-K discussions of government agencies, of which we discuss the first four. The first two examples from 22nd Century Group and Biomerica Inc. are standard references to government agencies in the business items and risk factors sections of firms' annual filings. The third example discloses an investigation by the Department of Labor into BPP Liquidating Trust's employee 401(k) plan. In the fourth example, Microsoft discusses the company evaluating the effect of IRS proposed regulations.

We compute text-based measures of exposure to government agencies by counting the number of sentences in firms' 10-Ks that reference federal agencies and any form of the following agency action words: regulation, jurisdiction, authority, examination, audit, enforce, and investigation, among others (see Appendix B for the complete list of words). We obtain the comprehensive list of federal agency names from the U.S. government. $ ^{11} $ We require sentences to include an action word so that our measure excludes references to agencies that do not indicate oversight by the agency. For example, firms often reference the Department of Labor-Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index (CPI), but references to the CPI do not indicate oversight by the Department of Labor.

If a sentence includes an action word but not an agency name, or vice versa, we do not count it as indicating exposure to a government agency. Our text-based measure of exposure to individual government agencies, [Agency] Exposure, is the number sentences indicating exposure to a given government agency r scaled by the total number of sentences in the 10-K filing following Li et al. (2013):

 $$ [Agency]Exposure_{i,t,r}=\frac{\#Agency sentences_{i,t,r}}{\#10K sentences_{i,t}} $$ 

where i indexes firms and t indexes years. Our text-based measure of overall exposure to government agencies, Total Government Agency Exposure, is the number sentences indicating exposure to any government agency scaled by the total number of sentences in the 10-K:

 $$ Total Government Agency Exposure_{i,t}=\sum_{r\in R}[Agency]Exposure_{i,t,r} $$ 

Following De la Parra Hurtado (2021), we construct our measures at the sentence level because “the close connection between thoughts and sentences makes sentences a natural place to see meaning flowing from thought to language” (Perry, 1994). We focus on federal government agencies because we expect the large U.S. public firms that we study to be subject to intense scrutiny from federal agencies. However, we acknowledge that state government agencies can also affect corporate outcomes (Bagchi and Sivadasan, 2017). We expect individual state agencies are significantly less salient and important for public firms than are federal agencies because public

firms typically operate in many, or all, U.S. states, diluting the significance of any given state agency. Future work may use our same measurement technique to study regulation by state (or foreign) regulators.

The strengths of our approach are its simplicity and its ability to identify individual federal government agencies. $ ^{12} $ Federal government agency names are standardized with easily identified titles and widely accepted acronyms (e.g., the Internal Revenue Service [IRS] or the Department of Justice [DOJ]). These highly standardized names allow us to cleanly measure exposure to government agencies.

In addition to the strengths of our measure, we note several important caveats. Our measure is based on the annual reports of public firms. Public firms must discuss financially material matters in their annual reports. Those levels of selection need to be considered when interpreting the results of using the measure, especially the relative importance and costs by agency. Because the measure is generated from textual disclosures, the measure focuses on publicly traded, larger firms, (i.e., those required to file 10-Ks), and on firms that are materially affected by regulatory agencies (which often means negatively affected). These caveats reduce the extent to which our measure can allow researchers to generalize the effect of more exposed firms and the differential effects of agencies to the broader economy.

### 3.2. Data

The sample includes U.S. incorporated firms from 1998 through 2019. The sample period begins in 1998 because the Census started using the NAICS definition of industry during 1997, and we define industries using the NAICS definition to be consistent with how many government agencies view industries. We focus on firms incorporated in the United States because Total Government Agency Exposure measures exposure to U.S. government agencies. We remove observations with missing data or negative revenue. We require firms to have at least 2 years of data to avoid singleton observations. All continuous variables are winsorized at the 1 and 99 percent levels. The final sample is comprised of 4541 firms and 46,197 firm-year observations. Appendix A defines all variables and data sources.

Despite the industry commonalities in exposure to government agencies, we find that a significant portion of the exposures are time-varying and firm-specific. Specifically, we use Shapley values to examine the percentage of variation explained by several sets of fixed effects (Grömping, 2007; Huettner and Sunder, 2012). $ ^{15} $ Shapley values are calculated by considering each combination of the full set of regressors after removing regressors one by one. When a regressor is removed, the change in R-squared represents that regressor's marginal contribution in that permutation. All permutations are considered equally probable, so the Shapley value is equal to a regressor's average marginal contribution across all permutations.

Fig. 4 presents the Shapley values. Panel A shows that industry fixed effects explain 11% of the total variation in Total Government Agency Exposure, firm fixed effects explain 67%, and year fixed effects explain 5%. 17% of the variation is unexplained by the fixed effects and controls. This variation is time-varying and firm-specific, suggesting that firm-year differences are significant determinants of firm exposure to government agencies and highlighting the need for a time-varying, firm-level measure that captures this variation. In Panel B we repeat the process, but use industry-year fixed effects instead of industry and year fixed effects separately. We find that industry-year fixed effects explain 18% of the total variation in Total Government Agency Exposure, and firm fixed effects explain 64%. As in Panel A, 17% of the variation is unexplained by the fixed effects and controls.

### 4.2. Changes in Total Government Agency Exposure in response to significant changes in regulation

To further validate our measures, we examine how they vary with interventions that increased the scope and power of government agencies. ### 4.3. Government agency exposure and undisclosed oversight

We next examine whether our measures of exposure to individual government agencies relate to oversight that the agencies did not disclose. Doing so addresses the possibility that the time-varying, firm-specific differences in government agency exposures reflect random differences in managers' propensities to discuss agencies, or other idiosyncratic factors. Further, doing so allows us to document whether our measures of exposure to government agencies relate to otherwise nonpublic oversight.

First, we examine whether exposure to the SEC relates to SEC investigations that were never publicly disclosed by the SEC (Undisclosed SEC Investigation; Blackburne et al., 2021). The SEC investigations process is very secretive, and as such only senior managers of the firm, the SEC staff, and outside counsel are aware of an ongoing investigation. Additionally, even if the investigation is material, firms are not required to directly disclose the investigation unless it reaches the final stage: an enforcement action (Blackburne et al., 2021). However, because managers are aware of the investigation, they may be more likely to discuss the SEC in their 10-Ks when under investigation. We obtain raw data on all closed SEC investigations between January 1, 2000 and August 2, 2017 from Blackburne et al. #### 5.1.1. Donald Trump election event study natural experiment

The evidence in Table 4 that exposure to government agencies negatively relates to profitability suggests that government agencies impose costs on firms. A potential concern with this evidence is that the results in Table 4 are potentially endogenous. For example, government agencies may target firms that they expect to become more profitable, which would endogenously attenuate our estimates. We attempt to address this concern by including firm fixed effects and examining concurrent profitability. Consequently, for endogenous selection to explain our results government agencies must target firms based on concurrent changes in profitability. While we believe this is unlikely, in this section, we examine a source of plausibly exogenous variation in expected exposure to government agencies created by Donald Trump's 2016 election to President.

Donald Trump campaigned heavily against government regulation and government agencies, and to some extent carried out those promises (Belton and Graham, 2020; Crews, 2021; Popovich et al., 2019). ### 5.2. Changes in exposure to the SEC and liquidity after SOX

We next highlight the benefits of our agency-specific measure by investigating how SOX affected SEC Exposure and stock liquidity. In this test, we are especially interested in whether SOX relatively increased oversight of previously regulated firms or expanded SEC oversight to firms previously not significantly regulated by the SEC. We identify firms that were not significantly regulated by the SEC prior to SOX with an indicator equal to one if SEC Exposure = 1 for firm i in the year prior to SOX (No Significant SEC Exposure Pre-SOX). We then interact the indicator with an indicator for the regulatory change (No Significant SEC Exposure Pre-SOX * Post SOX). We then regress SEC Exposure on these additional terms along with the fixed effects and controls from Eq. (3).

Table 6 Panel A reports the results of estimating the modified Eq. (3). We find that SOX expanded SEC oversight to previously less regulated firms. In particular, the coefficient on No Significant SEC Exposure Pre-SOX * Post SOX in Table 6 Panel A column (2) suggests that SEC oversight at previously less regulated firms increased about one third of the population standard deviation after SOX. This evidence highlights that SOX significantly expanded the purview of the SEC, providing insights into the distributional effects of SOX.

In Table 6 Panel B we examine how the expanded purview of the SEC after SOX affected stock liquidity, measured inversely with the natural logarithm of the Amihud (2002) illiquidity measure ( $ \ln(\text{Amihud}) $). We find that firms that received relatively increased SEC attention after SOX experienced significant relative increases in stock liquidity of about 5%. ### 5.5. Comparing the dictionary-based approach to measuring exposure with generative AI

In light of recent developments in generative AI and large-language models, we compare the effectiveness of our dictionary-based method against a more complex approach. As noted in de Kok (2024), Generative Large Language Models can complete tasks that were previously only solvable with human reasoning. Along this vein, Kim et al. (2023) use a GPT model to generate risk assessments from earnings calls and find that these risk assessments outperform existing measures. To compare our approach against more complex approaches, we use a GPT model from OpenAI to create an alternative measure of exposure to the SEC, SEC Exposure AI. $ ^{25} $

To create SEC Exposure AI, we start by collecting every sentence in a 10-K that mentions the SEC. For each sentence, we prompt OpenAI's GPT 3.5 Turbo model with the following: “A company is exposed to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) if there are potential or realized SEC actions that can directly or indirectly affect a company's behavior or outcomes. Does the company explicitly mention its exposure to the SEC in the sentence provided? Answer with one word, Yes or No: {SEC sentence}”. We count the number of sentences that return “Yes” and scale the sum by the total number of sentences in the 10-K to generate SEC Exposure AI.

We find that SEC Exposure and SEC Exposure AI are 44% correlated (statistically significant at all conventional levels; untabulated), suggesting they capture very similar constructs. Table 1 includes descriptive statistics for SEC Exposure AI. The mean of SEC Exposure AI (0.0033) is higher than the mean of SEC Exposure (0.0026), and the standard deviations are similar (0.0028 for SEC Exposure AI and 0.0026 for SEC Exposure). The higher mean of SEC Exposure AI suggests that the model identifies other regulation words aside from the ones we identify, highlighting the need to compare the validity of the measures.

We present the results of our comparative validation tests in Table 9. Panel A compares the power of SEC Exposure and SEC Exposure AI at predicting undisclosed SEC investigations. Columns 1 and 3 match Table 2 Panel A columns 3 and 4. We find that SEC Exposure is slightly better at predicting undisclosed SEC investigations than is SEC Exposure AI, but the difference between the two is not statistically significant. Panel B compares the ability of the two measures to predict SEC 10-K downloads. Columns 1 and 3 match column 1 of Table 3 Panels A and B. We find that SEC Exposure is marginally worse at predicting SEC downloads than SEC Exposure AI, but again the difference is not statistically significant. ## 6. Conclusion

We develop and validate firm-level, agency specific measures of exposure to government agencies. Using the measures, we find that overall exposure to government agencies negatively relates to firms' profitability, suggesting government agencies impose costs on firms. Consistent with a causal interpretation of these results, firms more exposed to government agencies earned higher returns than their less exposed counterparts following the surprise presidential election of Donald Trump, who promised to reduce the scope and power of government agencies. To highlight benefits of our measure, we demonstrate that SOX expanded oversight of the SEC to previously less regulated firms and that affected firms experience increases in stock liquidity. Further, we demonstrate that IRS budget cuts reduced IRS oversight of previously more regulated firms and that affected firms experience decreases in effective tax rates. These tests highlight how our measure can capture both costs and benefits of exposure to government agencies, agency-specific actions, agency-specific outcomes, and budgetary effects.

Our tests are by no means comprehensive and for the most part document associations. However, we believe our measures will be of use to future researchers interested in further exploring the effects of government agencies or drawing deeper inferences in situations where exposure to government agencies should moderate outcomes. For example, researchers can use our measures to explore how exposure to government agencies affects innovation, market power, competition, taxation, how and whether different types of agencies interact to affect firms, whether exposures to agencies harms or benefits peer firms, and many other important questions.

## Appendix B. Measuring Government Agency Exposure

After obtaining and cleaning 10-K filings from EDGAR, we use the Python NLTK sentence tokenizer to split each filing into sentences. A government agency exposure sentence is one that includes both a federal agency name and an agency action word. Following Li et al. (2013), we remove instances where the word "not" precedes the agency action word by three words or fewer in the same sentence. Total Government Agency Exposure is the number of government agency exposure sentences in a 10-K filing scaled by the total number of sentences. We obtain the comprehensive list of federal agency names from: https://www.usa.gov/federal-agencies.

To obtain a list of agency action words, we obtain a random list of 200 sentences from 10-Ks that include a federal agency name. We read each sentence and manually determine whether it references being subject to a federal government agency's rules, regulations, or monitoring. Some examples are.

1. “In the event the U.S. **Department of Justice** begins strict enforcement of the CSA in states that have laws legalizing medical and/or adult recreational cannabis/- marijuana, there may be a direct and adverse impact to any future potential business or prospects that we may have in the cannabis/marijuana business.” [22nd Century Group Inc, 2017, Item 1. Business]

2. “Biomerica’s immunodiagnostic products are regulated in the United States as medical devices primarily by the **FDA** and as such, require regulatory clearance or approval prior to commercialization in the United States.” [Biomerica Inc., 2016, Item 1A. Risk Factors]

3. “The Trust has been advised that the U.S. **Department of Labor** has investigated the Company’s decision to suspend trading of shares of Company common stock held by employees in the Company’s 401(k) Plan … and that the Department of Labor believes the Company’s decision was inappropriate.” [BPP Liquidating Trust, 2004, Item 3. Legal Proceedings]

4. “On August 1, 2018, the **Internal Revenue Service** published on its website proposed regulations relating to the transition tax imposed by the TCJA … We are currently evaluating the impact of the proposed regulations.” [Microsoft Corp, 2018, Note 13. Income Taxes]

5. “Sales of these commodities are, however, subject to laws and to regulations issued by the **Federal Trade Commission** (FTC) prohibiting manipulative or fraudulent conduct in the wholesale petroleum market.” [Sandridge Mississippian Trust I, 2019, Item 1. Business]

6. “Following the approval of applications that the Company filed with the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System and the Bank filed with the **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency** (“OCC”), the Company became a bank holding company and the Bank became a national bank on November 30, 2016.” [BankFinancial Corp, 2016, Item 1. Business]

7. “The U.S. **Department of Labor** and its agencies ... regulate our employment practices and standards for workers.” [US Foods Holding Corp, 2018, Item 1. Business]

8. “In January 1999, the Company received notice of a proposed adjustment pursuant to an examination by the **Internal Revenue Service** of the Company’s fiscal 1995 and 1996 tax returns, disallowing the utilization of $4.6 million capital loss carryforward to partially offset the gain recognized by the Company in connection with the sale of its health care operations in July 1996.” [Heico Corp, 2000, Item 3. Legal Proceedings]

9. “Federal regulations require that a hospice program satisfy certain Conditions of Participation (“COP”) to be certified and receive **Medicare** payment for the services it provides.” [Cemed Corp, 2011, Item 1. Business]

For each sentence that we deem to be about exposure to a government agency, we identify the key word or words that make it a government agency exposure sentence. This process yields the following agency action words:

Words that start with.

regulat, jurisdiction, investigat, inspec, enforce, authorit, comply, complie, compliance, violat, examin, approv, ruling $ ^{27} $

Or any of the following words.

rule, rules, law, laws, audit, audits, audited, auditor, auditors, auditing.

We require sentences to include an agency action word because firms interact with agencies in ways that are not in the spirit of oversight. For example, firms often reference the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which is a Department of Labor—Bureau of Labor Statistics report but references to the CPI do not indicate oversight by the Department of Labor. Requiring agency actions words excludes these references.
