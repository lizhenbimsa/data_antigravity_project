# Can investors learn from patent documents? Evidence from textual analysis

Yuxiang Zheng

Rutgers School of Business–Camden, Rutgers University, Camden, New Jersey, USA

## Abstract

This paper examines the role of patent texts in the stock market valuation of patents. Utilizing the large language model BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) to summarize contextual information within patent texts, I find that patent texts explain 31.5% of the variation in the stock market valuation of patents and provide large incremental explanatory power beyond other structured patent characteristics, firm characteristics, and technological trends. Additionally, patent texts significantly predict the level, volatility, and cumulation speed of future earnings, suggesting they contain genuine information about firms' performance. However, investors do not fully incorporate such information within patent texts into stock prices, as evidenced by the predictive power of patent texts for future stock returns. This underreaction is diminished after the pre-grant publication of patent applications is mandated. My findings underscore the value of patent texts as a source of information on internally developed intangibles and have implications for academics, practitioners, and regulators.

##### KEYWORDS

BERT, big data, patent, textual analysis, voluntary disclosure

# Les investisseurs peuvent-ils tirer de l'information de documents de brevets? Données probantes tirées d'une analyse textuelle

#### Résumé

La présente étude se penche sur le rôle des textes de brevets dans l'évaluation de la valeur boursière des brevets. En utilisant le grand modèle de langage BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) pour obtenir un aperçu de l'information contextuelle qui se trouve dans les textes de brevets, j'établis que ces textes expliquent 31,5 % de la variation de la capitalisation boursière des brevets et fournissent un vaste pouvoir explicatif outre les autres caractéristiques structurées des brevets, les caractéristiques de l'entreprise et les tendances technologiques. De plus, les textes de brevets prédisent de façon significative le niveau, la volatilité et la vitesse d'accumulation des revenus à venir, ce qui donne à penser qu'ils contiennent de l'information authentique sur le rendement des entreprises. Toutefois, les investisseurs n'intègrent pas pleinement ce type de renseignements au cours des actions, comme le montre le pouvoir prédictif des textes de brevets sur les futurs rendements boursiers. Cette sous-réaction s'atténue après l'imposition de l'obligation de publier les demandes de brevet avant leur délivrance. Mes observations mettent en lumière la valeur des textes de brevets en tant que source d'information sur les actifs incorporels développés à l'interne et ont des conséquences pour les chercheurs, les praticiens et les organismes de réglementation.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

In today’s knowledge-based economy, innovation has emerged as a crucial driver of firms’ growth (Bloom et al., 2020). Yet, valuating firms’ internal innovation progress has been a complex task, and the financial reporting system is insufficient to address this challenge (Hegde et al., 2018; Kankanhalli et al., 2022; Lev, 2018; Verrecchia, 2001). This paper explores the role of patent texts in bridging this information gap. As milestones of firms’ internal innovation processes, patents periodically summarize firms’ innovation outcomes. The US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) promotes the public disclosure of detailed findings by bestowing exclusive property rights to patent assignees. As a unique and rich information source often not disclosed elsewhere, patents may serve as a potential information channel for investors to learn about and valuate firms’ internal innovations. $ ^{1} $

The value relevance of patents, in general, is supported by the observed positive market reaction to patent grants documented by Kogan et al. (2017) and Martens (2023). While receiving a patent, on average, signals relatively successful innovation outcomes, patents vary significantly in their values (Allison et al., 2004; Lemley & Shapiro, 2005). Previous research has explored several value-relevant patent characteristics, such as the completeness of patent citations (Allison et al., 2004, 2009; Allison & Tiller, 2003), patent claims' scope (Allison et al., 2004; Lanjouw & Schankerman, 2004; Marco et al., 2019), and patented inventions' originality (Hirshleifer et al., 2018). However, these studies focus primarily on the structural aspects of patent documents, leaving the rich but unstructured information from patent texts underexplored. Acknowledging this gap, Glaeser and Lang (2024) emphasize in their recent review work the need for research on the descriptive content of patent disclosures. Answering their call, I examine two primary research questions: (1) Do patent texts convey information that drives the stock market valuation of patents? and (2) If so, does the stock market promptly and fully incorporate this information into stock prices?

Valuation information within patent texts is inherently multidimensional. While the intrinsic value of an invention is rooted in its technical features and functionalities, its private economic value also hinges on the specific context in which it is deployed. To distill and comprehend the value-relevant information embedded in patent texts, I use the large language model BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers). BERT is a deep learning model that leverages bidirectional transformers to comprehend texts as sequentially connected and interacting elements.

I apply Google’s pretrained BERT model to patent texts that are labeled with the stock market valuation of each patent, as measured by Kogan et al. (2017). To directly predict the continuous patent stock market valuation, I add a linear layer to the original architecture of the Google BERT model. Each year, I use patents granted in the prior year to fine-tune the model and then apply the fine-tuned model to the current year’s patent texts to generate a predicted value for each patent’s stock market valuation. This predicted value, solely based on patent texts, serves as an integrated measure of the contextual information in patent texts that is relevant to patent stock market valuation. Finally, I regress patent stock market valuation on this BERT-based integrated measure of patent texts. I find that patent texts have significant predictive power for the stock market valuation of patents; they explain 31.5% of the variation in patent stock market valuation. This explanatory power is incremental to other structured patent characteristics such as patent citations, patent scope, and patent originality, as well as firm characteristics and the evolution over time of specific technological fields.

After documenting the association between patent texts and stock market valuation of patents measured by short-term stock market reaction, I investigate whether investors promptly respond to patent text information. The complexity of the technical and scientific language in patent texts likely forms a knowledge barrier, imposing processing costs on investors. This impedes investors' timely and full incorporation of patent text information into their valuation models (Blankespoor et al., 2020). My findings suggest that the stock market reaction to patent texts is incomplete around patent grants: patent texts have significant predictive power for 1-year-ahead market-adjusted stock returns. Additional analysis shows that such predictive power diminishes after the American Inventors Protection Act (AIPA) of 1999 mandates the disclosure of initial applications before patent grants. This finding is consistent with the expectation that the pre-grant publications have a preemptive effect on the valuation information in granted patent texts, thereby accelerating the stock price discovery for patents (Hegde et al., 2018).

Lastly, to address the concern that the documented association between patent texts and stock market reaction is driven by managers strategically employing patent texts to inflate investors' expectations about patents' future commercialization and private economic value, I examine the association between patent texts and firms' future earnings. Innovation activities create value by increasing future earnings but also likely introduce substantial uncertainty.

Using BERT to summarize patent text information, I find that patent texts can predict future 5-year cumulative earnings; they can also predict the standard deviation and accumulation rate of future 5-year earnings. These findings suggest that patent texts contain genuine information and map into stock market valuation by revising investors' expectations about future earnings.

This paper makes several contributions. First, it adds to the intangible asset literature by identifying patent texts as an alternative information channel for the stock market to learn about firms' innovation. Prior studies mainly focus on information sources within the financial reporting system, such as R&D expenses (see Lev, 2018, for a review) and firms' narrative disclosure (Jones, 2007; Merkley, 2014). These studies suggest that information about intangibles is value-relevant, yet disclosures of innovation from financial reports are, in general, insufficient. Recent research has begun to investigate the function of the patent disclosure system in the capital market and has documented an average positive market reaction to patent grants (Kogan et al., 2017; Martens, 2023). I expand these recent studies by showing that contextual information in patent texts is value-relevant and that the stock market reacts, but underreacts, to such information. My paper also extends Kogan et al. (2017) by showing that investors process patent texts when forming their valuation of patents.

Second, this study contributes to the literature on textual analysis in accounting and finance (for a recent review, see Bochkay et al., 2023) by utilizing the BERT model in the patent domain to extract value-relevant information. A few studies have employed the BERT model to analyze curated financial disclosures, such as earnings announcements, annual reports, and analysts' reports, and documented its superior efficacy (A. H. Huang et al., 2023; Siano, 2022). In this era of information overload, investors obtain company information through various channels above and beyond the financial disclosure system. In their review work, Leuz and Wysocki (2016) call for research to examine non-traditional disclosures. My study aligns with the growing literature on textual analysis and big data by showing the power of the BERT model in summarizing value-relevant information from uncurated patent texts.

Finally, the findings are relevant to both regulators and practitioners. First, recognizing the importance of patents to firm value, the SEC has considered increasing the mandatory disclosure of patents in annual reports (SEC, 2016). By linking the contextual information in patent texts to patent stock market valuation, I provide direct evidence of patent texts' value relevance. Still, the positive association between patent texts and future stock returns suggests that the patent disclosure system is insufficient for the capital market and that additional disclosure from the financial reporting system could be potentially beneficial. Second, the market underreaction to patent texts is likely due to knowledge barriers and processing costs. My study, therefore, has implications for how deep learning algorithms, such as BERT, combined with historical data, could help investors overcome knowledge barriers and process scientific disclosures.

## 2 | INSTITUTIONAL BACKGROUND AND RELATED LITERATURE

### 2.1 | Institutional background

#### 2.1.1 | Patent document structure and information content

A patent is a form of legal protection granted by the USPTO that affords the patent holder exclusive rights to an invention. To qualify for this protection, an invention must meet two principal requirements. First, it must be “new,” meaning that it has not been “patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the effective filing date of the claimed invention” (USPTO, n.d.). Second, it must be “useful,” requiring the invention to have practical, real-world applications (USPTO, n.d.). Because of these requirements, each patent document contains information that is, by its very nature,

pertinent to the business activities of the patenting firm and is not likely to be found elsewhere in the public domain (World Intellectual Property Organization, 2004). In addition, firms choose to patent an invention and disclose all the details of this invention when the economic benefits of legal protection outweigh the cost of revealing it to the public, particularly to rivals (Glaeser et al., 2020). Without such benefits, managers would lack the incentives to voluntarily disclose their inventions in detail to the public.

The USPTO publicly releases all patent documents through its online USPTO Patent Full-Text and Image Database (PatFT). This public disclosure system aims to foster innovation and prevent redundant research, thus maximizing social welfare. To achieve this goal, the USPTO mandates that patent applicants describe their inventions in detailed documents. Each patent document must articulate the invention “in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms as to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use the same [invention]” (35 United States Code § 112). To ensure adherence to these standards, the USPTO assigns each patent application to a specialized patent examiner within the relevant technology field. The examiner conducts a comprehensive review of the claimed invention and contrasts it with prior art to verify its accuracy and truthful representation. These detailed disclosure requirements make the USPTO patent system a robust and credible source of information regarding firms’ internally developed intangible assets.

A typical patent filed with the USPTO consists of several standard sections: Title, Abstract, Claims, Brief Description of the Drawings, Brief Description of the Invention, and Detailed Description of the Invention. $ ^{2} $ Although these sections together contribute to a complete picture of an invention, each, with its own emphasis, caters to different aspects of the invention’s disclosure. The Abstract section is a high-level summary of the invention. The Claims section outlines the precise legal boundaries of patent protection. The Brief Description of the Drawings section presents a concise written explanation of the drawings or figures included in the patent.

The Brief Description section typically contains two subsections: (1) Background of Invention, and (2) Summary of Invention. This section conveys information about the prior art in the technological field, scientific importance, existing problems that the invention seeks to address, potential commercialization, and more. Such discussions are significantly based on inventors' best knowledge and judgment. For example, in Apple Inc.'s auto-driving technology Patent 10970518 (Appendix S1), $ ^{3} $ the applicants describe the invention as "an important component in a variety of real-world applications....Compared to image-based detection, point clouds may provide more reliable depth information, useable to more accurately localize objects and characterize their shapes." In addition to human judgment, this section also presents a concise overview of the essential technical aspects of the invention.

Lastly, the Detailed Description section parallels the Summary of Invention subsection in the Brief Description section but provides a profound elaboration of the invention's technical details. These technical details are essential for scientists to comprehend the invention and facilitate its replication. Conversely, its extensive level of detail might be overly intricate for the capital market to grasp the private economic value of the invention.

Among the various sections, the Brief Description section is optimal for examining the value relevance of patent texts using a machine learning approach for two primary reasons. First, it concisely captures the core elements of the invention while incorporating human expertise and interpretation, striking a balance between technical details and human judgment. Second, the Summary subsection provides adequate technical details without being overly resource-demanding, making it computationally efficient for machine learning algorithms, compared to the Detailed Description section. This brevity and focus on key elements enable the machine learning algorithm to efficiently process and analyze patent data on a large scale. As a result,

the Brief Description section serves as an efficient basis for this study to examine the value relevance of patent texts.

#### 2.1.2 | Patent publication process

The patent publication process typically involves three important dates: the filing date, the publication date, and the grant date. The filing date is when the application is officially submitted to the patent office. However, after the filing date, the patent application remains confidential until the patent is granted by the USPTO after a thorough review and revision process. The grant date is usually several years after the filing date and is generally beyond the control of the firm that has submitted the application. Before the enactment of AIPA, the grant date was the first time the public became aware of the patent application and had access to its information content. After AIPA, all US patent applications with foreign parallel applications (filed in the European Union or Japan, for example) are required to be published 18 months after the first application, whereas inventors filing patents only in the United States can opt out of the 18-month disclosure requirement by submitting a non-publication request to the USPTO.

Despite AIPA hastening the disclosure of patent applications at the 18-month publication mark, the information contained in the pre-grant publication is mostly preliminary and unverified. The content of a patent document is still subject to thorough examination by patent officers and potential significant revision. Therefore, even after AIPA, the grant date continues to be the crucial point at which the patent's official recognition and the precise details of the invention's protection by the USPTO become clear to the public. Kogan et al. (2017) compare the market reactions around the patent filing date, the publication date post-AIPA, and the grant date. They find that there is no significant price movement around the filing date, that the stock price movement is weak around the publication date post-AIPA, and that there is significant stock price movement around the grant date. To remain consistent across the sample period, I follow Kogan et al. (2017) and focus on the patent grant date in examining the value relevance of patent texts both before and after AIPA.

### 2.2 | Literature review

In the current knowledge-based economy, innovation acts as a crucial driving force for firm growth (Bloom et al., 2020). Despite its economic importance, assessing firms' internal innovation activities is rather challenging. Part of the reason is the vague connection between R&D inputs and cash flow returns. Claiming that "at the time most research and development costs are incurred, the future benefits are at best uncertain" (Accounting Standards Codification 730-10), the FASB has been avoiding recognizing internal innovation investment on balance sheets. In addition, proprietary costs deter firms from voluntarily disclosing their innovation activities (Verrecchia, 2001). Analyzing the narrative R&D disclosure included in annual reports, Jones (2007) finds that proprietary costs decrease R&D disclosures, and Merkley (2014) finds that firms communicate more about their R&D activities when earnings performance is not satisfactory. Overall, the R&D-related disclosures from the financial reporting system have been criticized for being insufficient and detrimental to capital market efficiency (Lev, 2018).

Recent research has shed light on the patent disclosure system as an alternative disclosure system beyond the financial reports, with evidence that stock market participants react positively to patent grants (e.g., Beyhaghi et al., 2019; Kogan et al., 2017; Martens, 2023) and that accelerated patent disclosures could increase stock price discovery efficiency and stock liquidity (Hegde et al., 2018). While a patent, compared to the initial research stage, signals relatively successful innovation outcomes and partially resolves the uncertainty (Glaeser et al., 2020), the

realization of a patent’s private economic value remains more probabilistic than guaranteed, and significant variation in patent values exists (Lemley & Shapiro, 2005). Therefore, an accurate assessment of the economic significance, as well as the associated uncertainty, of a patented invention is vital for the capital market to properly price the invention.

Prior studies have documented several structured characteristics of patents that can help investors form their valuations of patents. Using ex post patent litigation as an indicator of patent private economic value, Allison et al. (2004) find that patent value increases with the number of prior art citations and the number of claims. This is because comprehensive citations could mitigate the risk that a patent is invalidated in the future due to overlooked or uncited previous works (e.g., Allison & Tiller, 2003). Patent claims also define the scope of property rights protected by the patent (Kuhn & Thompson, 2019; Lanjouw & Schankerman, 2004; Marco et al., 2019). A broader scope of a patent suggests greater application potential. Both characteristics, therefore, contribute to patent private economic value. Furthermore, using citations to prior patents as a proxy for drawing and combining knowledge from different fields, Hirshleifer et al. (2018) find that patents drawing on more technological fields, which they interpret as being more original, have higher market value, and the stock market does not promptly price these original inventions.

While these prior studies have touched on important structural aspects of patent documents that are value-relevant, an overlooked aspect that should be of equal importance, if not more so, is the rich but unstructured patent texts. Glaeser and Lang's (2024) recent review work on accounting for innovation also underscores the necessity of examining the descriptive content of patent documents. Responding to their call and building upon existing literature on patent structured characteristics, this paper examines whether the patent texts convey value-relevant information and to what extent investors process such information.

Part of the reason why descriptive content in patent texts remains understudied is perhaps its unstructured nature. The highly specialized technical and scientific language used in patent texts presents difficulties in applying traditional textual analysis methods from accounting literature, such as bag-of-words (BOW) or dictionary-based methods, to patent texts. Dyer et al. (2024), in a recent study related to my paper but with a distinct focus, examine the disclosure quality of patent documents. They find that patents’ disclosure quality is important for external inventors in developing follow-on innovations. My paper differs from Dyer et al. (2024) in that (1) I focus on information content instead of disclosure quality, and (2) the audience of interest is stock market participants instead of inventors within the patent system. $ ^{4} $ In addition, Hsu et al. (2020), in an unpublished working paper, study the accuracy of deep learning methods in predicting patents’ future citations and stock market valuation, with both patent structural characteristics and patent texts as inputs. They conclude that the deep learning method outperforms the linear model and traditional machine learning methods. My paper complements Hsu et al. (2020) by quantifying the predictive power of the inputs, particularly patent texts, for patent stock market valuation. $ ^{5} $

A recent advancement of textual analysis in accounting literature is the application of deep learning algorithms to financial disclosures. For example, A. H. Huang et al. (2023) find that the deep learning model BERT outperforms traditional methods in categorization tasks, such as labeling the linguistic tone and discussions related to environment, social, and governance in financial disclosures. Siano (2022) highlights BERT's ability to summarize contextual information in earnings announcements, which has strong predictive power for short-term market reactions and future earnings. My study extends the growing literature in textual analysis using deep learning

algorithms by applying the BERT model to nonfinancial disclosures. It also answers Leuz and Wysocki's (2016) call for research on nontraditional disclosure.

## 3 | HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

### 3.1 | Patent texts and the stock market valuation of patents

When a firm’s innovation progress reaches the level of qualifying for a patent, it signals a milestone in its innovation efforts and increases the potential for future profit growth. Moreover, a granted patent provides the firm with a legally protected monopoly over the use of the patented invention. This right prevents competitors from copying or utilizing the technology for a certain period, which can result in competitive advantages and significant economic benefits for the patent holder. Given the economic importance of patents and the thorough details embedded within the patent texts (see Section 2.1.1 for details), it is natural to expect that patent texts could be a rich information source for investors in forming their valuations of patents.

However, the private economic value of a patented invention might not always be apparent to investors solely based on analyzing the patent text. The transition from patenting an invention to its commercialization is typically complex and filled with uncertainty. According to a survey study on a random sample of 9,060 triadic patents (i.e., patents that seek protection in multiple jurisdictions, including Europe, Japan, and the United States), only 55% of these patents have ever been commercialized (Walsh et al., 2016). In addition, patent documents primarily focus on describing the technological aspects of the invention, aiming to enable replication and inspire further innovations. As a result, discussions regarding potential commercial applications tend to be limited within these documents. Investors in the stock market, who are not the primary audience for patent disclosures, might struggle to correlate the content of patent texts with the private economic value of the underlying technology. This value is influenced by both the company's application of the technology and the product market's reception of the resultant product. $ ^{6} $ Lastly, despite the examiners' thorough review, the final document may still reflect managers' strategic disclosures, such as an inflated linguistic tone or reduced readability, which can dampen the value relevance of patent texts.

Considering the above arguments, while patent texts provide comprehensive details about the patented invention, other factors may interplay in determining the value relevance of patent texts. Therefore, whether patent texts provide material information for investors to form their valuation of the patent remains an empirical question. This leads to my first hypothesis (in null):

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Ceteris paribus, patent texts are not associated with the stock market valuation of patents.

## 4 | EMPIRICAL DESIGN AND FINDINGS

### 4.1 | Data and sample selection

Table 1 describes the sample selection procedure. I start with the patent database compiled and updated by Kogan et al. (2017), which links 3,053,011 patents granted by the USPTO between 1926 and 2020 to 8,561 US public firms. $ ^{7} $ Following prior literature (e.g., Curtis et al., 2020; Hirshleifer et al., 2018), my sample period spans from 1981 to 2020, during which 2,311,039 patents are granted to 7,105 firms. Next, I obtain firms' financial information from the Compustat database and exclude financial firms (four-digit SIC codes between 6000 and 6999) and utility firms (SIC codes beginning with 49) from the sample following Hirshleifer et al. (2018). This step results in 2,192,282 patents from 6,358 firms. Finally, I obtain detailed information on patent documents from PatentsView, which organizes and maintains comprehensive information on US-granted patents by sections. After merging my

sample with PatentsView and dropping observations missing key patent sections or key variables, the final sample contains 2,120,281 patents from 5,863 firms.

Table 2 provides descriptive statistics on the regression sample. Panel A of Table 2 presents the industry distribution of the sample firms and patents. Compared to the Compustat universe (subject to similar sample selection procedures), the sample firms under analysis are primarily concentrated in the manufacturing industry, which constitutes 75.46% of the firms in the sample. This pattern is mirrored in the distribution of patents with 79.49% of patents from the manufacturing industry. Panel B of Table 2 shows the summary statistics for key variables at both the firm level and patent level. The firms in the sample are, on average, relatively large and mature ones. They are also less intensive on tangible assets and have more R&D expenditures. In terms of their patenting activities, the median number of patents a firm receives annually is four, yet the most prolific firm has been granted over 2,000 patents. The median stock market valuation of the sample patents is $7 million as measured by Kogan et al. (2017).

### 4.2 | Measuring information content of patent texts

The extraction of information content from patent texts via textual analysis tools presents a complex challenge. Traditional methods, such as the BOW method, may fall short in effectively analyzing patent documents, primarily because the language used in patents is highly specialized, often incorporating industry-specific jargon, technical descriptions, and complex mathematical formulas. For example, dictionary-based sentiment analysis has been widely used and accepted in accounting research, benefiting from the development of tailored dictionaries for financial documents (e.g., Loughran & McDonald, 2011). Such tailored dictionaries, however, are not available for the scientific documents' domain to the best of my knowledge. Moreover, the valuation information contained within patents is inherently multidimensional, and the private economic value of patented technologies can vary significantly depending on the context or scenario in which they are applied.

Given these challenges, I apply the BERT model to extract a single variable from each patent, based solely on the contextual information in its text. BERT processes language inputs bidirectionally, meaning it can understand the full context of a word based on the surrounding words. This algorithm allows BERT to understand nuances in language, such as the different meanings a word can have based on its context. This is important in learning the value of the same technology within different contexts. Moreover, BERT is an attention-based model that emphasizes more relevant and important context clues and minimizes less important context (Bochkay et al., 2023). Several recent studies have applied BERT to analyze curated financial texts, documenting its superior performance compared to traditional methods in the domain of financial disclosures (A. H. Huang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021; Siano, 2022).

Specifically, I utilize the pretrained BERT model released by Google, which is trained on a massive corpus of texts, including Wikipedia and Google BookCorpus. Both sources contain a significant amount of scientific content. For example, related to Patent 10970518 ("Voxel-based feature learning network"), Wikipedia's entry on "Voxel" discusses the technology in scientific language comparable to that used in patent texts ("Voxel," 2024). This makes the Google BERT model well-suited for analyzing patent texts. The pretrained model encodes textual information by generating contextualized vector representations for words in the text (i.e., word embeddings), dynamically capturing each word's meaning based on its surrounding words. With the vector presentations, the fine-tuning process then associates these vectors with task-specific labels (i.e., patent stock market valuation in this study) by optimizing the parameters of the embeddings and additional task-specific layers.

I apply this BERT model to patent texts by patent grant years on a rolling basis: for each year t from 1981 to 2020, I use patents granted in the previous year t−1 for fine-tuning and patents granted in year t for “out-of-sample” modeling (i.e., predicting the stock market valuation of patents granted in year t based solely on their patent texts). This predicted value of the stock market valuation based on patent texts serves as an integrated measure of the information within the patent texts that is associated with patent stock market valuation, to the extent captured by the BERT model. This approach allows me to train the model purely on historical

data, which minimizes overfitting biases. It also avoids losing any observation due to splitting the sample into “training” and “testing” subsamples. Furthermore, technologies, as well as the language describing such technologies, in patent documents evolve over time. Training the model with the most recent patents on a rolling basis potentially provides higher accuracy.

When inputting patent documents into the BERT model, a limitation is BERT's 512-token maximum for text processing. Prior studies (e.g., Siano & Wysocki, 2021) often address this by analyzing only the first 512 tokens, which results in information loss from the full text available. To overcome this limitation, I adopt Siano's (2022) approach of slicing each patent document—specifically, the Brief Description section—into 512-token windows of text, allowing each window to overlap with the prior window for approximately 20% or 100 tokens. All text windows of each patent, labeled with the corresponding patent stock market valuation, are then used for fine-tuning. For “out-of-sample” predictions, I average the outputs from all text windows of each patent to generate the patent-level prediction.

One key distinction of my paper, compared to prior studies using BERT, is the integration of a linear layer into the BERT model. Prior research mostly focuses on classification tasks, where the output is a categorical label for two or more classes. In contrast, to examine the value-relevant content of patent texts, I directly fine-tune the BERT model with the continuous measure of patent stock market valuation developed by Kogan et al. (2017). To achieve this, I integrate an additional linear layer into the original architecture of the Google BERT model, which was initially designed for classification tasks. This modified BERT model, when fed with patent texts, generates predictions for the continuous measure of patent stock market valuation from the final linear layer. This predicted value serves as a summary measure of contextual information in patent texts that pertains to the stock market valuation of patents. $ ^{9} $

I start by examining the association between patent texts and patent stock market valuation from Kogan et al. (2017). Kogan et al. (2017) measure patent stock market valuation as the product of the estimated short-term stock return due to the private economic value of a patent and the firm's market capitalization, under a Bayesian framework. The patent-related stock return is derived from a firm's 3-day idiosyncratic return following the grant of a patent. Kogan et al.'s (2017) measure reflects investors' assessment of the patent's private economic value upon patent grant. As described in Section 4.2, for each firm $i$'s patent $p$ granted in month $m$, I obtain a predicted value of the stock market valuation of this patent solely based on its text, which I label as PatentText$_{i,p,m}$.$^{10}$ I then use OLS regressions and regress PatentStockMarketValuation$_{i,p,m}$, which is the actual value of patent stock market valuation for firm $i$'s patent $p$ granted in month $m$, on PatentText$_{i,p,m}$.

To ensure that the information content of patent texts that drives the stock market valuation of patents is incremental to other structural characteristics that prior studies have documented as

value-relevant, I control for several patent-level characteristics. First, No. PatCite (No. NonPatCite) is the natural logarithm of the number of patents (non-patent literature) cited by the focal patent. A higher number of citations to prior patents or non-patent literature implies a more thorough prior art search, which could reduce the risk of future invalidation due to uncited prior art, therefore implying a higher patent value (e.g., Allison et al., 2004, 2009; Allison & Tiller, 2003).

Second, patent claims, particularly independent claims, define the legal boundaries and protection scope of the patented invention. Prior studies use (1) the number of claims, either all claims (Lanjouw & Schankerman, 2004) or independent claims only (Marco et al., 2019), and (2) the length of the first claim, which Kuhn and Thompson (2019) argue is the broadest and most crucial one, to proxy for the scope of a patent. A patent has a larger scope when it has more (independent) claims and when the first claim is shorter (shorter claims imply fewer constraints and therefore a larger scope). I include the number of independent claims (No. Indep. Claim), the number of all claims (No. Claims), and the length of the first claim (1st Claim Length) to control for patent scope.

Lastly, Hirshleifer et al. (2018) find that the originality of a patent, measured by the number of technological fields from which it draws knowledge, is positively associated with firm value. I follow Hirshleifer et al. (2018) and include the number of technological fields of prior patents that the focal patent cites (InnOrig) as a control variable. $ ^{11} $ I also include technological class-by-month fixed effects to control for any time trend in the technological evolution of a specific field and firm fixed effects to control for firm-specific innovation patterns.

### 4.4 | Test of H2

The findings from Section 4.3 imply that investors strongly react to patent text information following the grant of a patent. Next, I proceed to investigate whether the stock market reaction is complete within the short window around the patent grant. Prior studies document that investors tend to underreact to firms' investment in intangibles, especially when the information is complex and difficult to process (Eberhart et al., 2004; Hirshleifer et al., 2018). I therefore examine the predictive power of patent texts for a firm's future stock returns:

 $$ \begin{aligned}CAR_{i,p,\{m+1,m+12\}}=&\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}Patent\ Text\left(CAR\right)_{i,p,m}+Controls+Tech\ Class\times Month\ FE\\&+Firm\ FE,\end{aligned} $$ 

where  $ CAR_{i,p,\{m+1,m+12\}} $ denotes the 12-month value-weighted market-adjusted buy-and-hold returns of firm i, cumulated from 1 month after the grant month m of patent p to 12 months after the grant month. To extract the information content from patent texts related to future stock returns, I repeat the fine-tuning process described in Section 4.2, replacing the stock market valuation of patents with the 12-month market-adjusted returns as the prediction target. To mitigate any forward-looking bias, firm-level control variables are measured using data from the most recent fiscal year. I cluster the standard errors by technological field and firm. If investors do not fully respond to patent text information in the short term, I expect that patent texts will predict future long-term stock returns following the patent grant.

The choice of the time window over which to measure the correction of market under-reaction is not trivial. A short-window return might not be sufficient to capture the incorporation of the patent valuation information into the stock price, while a long-window return could potentially be influenced by factors unrelated to the patent itself. Balancing these considerations, I choose a 12-month window to assess the market's correction of underreaction to patent valuation information. This specific choice is motivated, in part, by the fact that the 12-month window encompasses the release of the following year's annual report, which may provide insights into the patent's commercialization progress. $ ^{14} $

### 5.2 | Patent pre-grant disclosure

AIPA brought a significant change in the patent publication process. Prior to AIPA’s implementation, patent documents were not disclosed to the public until the patent grant day. After AIPA, mandatory disclosure of preliminary patent applications became effective, occurring 18 months after the application. While some prior studies have pointed out potential biases in pre-grant publications due to the lack of examination by patent examiners (Marco et al., 2019) and have noted weak market reactions around such publications (Kogan et al., 2017), pre-grant publications still retain the potential to preempt, to some extent, the information content of the final publication on the grant day. Therefore, I examine the changing nature of patent texts’ informativeness to the stock market following AIPA.

Table 6 presents the results of subsample analyses based on whether a patent was filed before or after AIPA's implementation. Two noteworthy observations emerge from the results. First, the association between patent texts and stock market valuation (Columns 1 and 2) and the association between patent texts and future stock returns (Columns 3 and 4) are significant before AIPA but become insignificant afterward. Second, the predictive power of patent texts for future earnings remains consistently significant both before and after AIPA (Columns 5 and 6). These results suggest that while the informativeness of patent texts in predicting future earnings remains unchanged, market reactions (underreactions) to patent texts around the grant date have diminished post-AIPA. Collectively, the results support the expectation that the pre-grant publications mandated by AIPA have a preemptive effect on the valuation information in patent texts for the stock market. These findings align with Hegde et al.'s (2018) conclusion that AIPA improves the efficiency of stock price discovery.

### 5.4 | Falsification test

To further mitigate the concern that the observed association between patent texts and the stock market valuation of patents is driven by unobservable firm-specific, technological field-specific, or time-specific characteristics, I run falsification tests and present the results in Table 8. Specifically, in Column 1 of Table 8, I randomly assign the prediction of each patent's stock market valuation based on patent texts within a firm. If underlying characteristics of a firm drive the results, I would expect to find similar results using randomized patent texts. Similarly, in Columns 2 and 3, I repeat the random assignments within each technological field and each year, respectively. Across the three columns, the coefficients on Patent Text become insignificant. These results provide additional evidence ruling out the alternative explanation that firm, technological field, and time-specific characteristics drive the results and show that the inferences are not spuriously driven by the sheer number of observations.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

This paper examines the value relevance of patent texts to the capital market. Governed by the USPTO, patent documents present a truthful and comprehensive description of firms' R&D outputs, including both the technical features and inventors' insights into the invention. Using the BERT model to capture contextual information within patent texts, I find that patent texts explain up to 31.5% of the variation in patent stock market valuation. The predictive power is incremental to patent structured characteristics, firm characteristics, and time trends of technological evolution. Perhaps because of knowledge barriers and processing costs, stock market participants do not fully incorporate information conveyed by patent texts into the stock price; patent texts have strong predictive power for future 1-year market-adjusted stock returns. Investigating the nature of the value-relevant information in patent texts, I find that patent texts strongly predict the level, uncertainty, and accumulation speed of future 5-year earnings. One caveat of my paper is that the findings rely on the superior capabilities of the BERT model, which may have measurement errors or may not fully capture value-relevant information in patent texts, thereby resulting in underestimating the value relevance of patent texts.

My paper contributes to the ongoing literature on the disclosure of firm innovation by documenting patent texts as a significant information channel regarding firms' innovation. Second, it extends the literature on textual analysis in accounting and finance by demonstrating the applicability of BERT in the patent domain for extracting valuation insights. It also has implications for practitioners about how deep learning algorithms like BERT could help mitigate the processing costs of specialized information content and guide investment decisions.
