## Abstract

This research investigates the impact of climate challenges on financial markets by introducing an innovative approach to measure climate risk, specifically the aggregate climate change concern index (ACCC). The study aims to assess and quantify the potential influence of climate change and risk-related factors on the performance and dynamics of financial markets. In this paper, concern is defined as the attention paid to the risk of climate change and the associated negative consequences. The findings demonstrate that the aggregate index exhibits robust predictability of market risk premiums, both within the sample and out-of-sample. By comparison, the index contains additional information beyond 14 economic predictors and 12 risk/uncertainty indexes in forecasting stock market return. Additionally, the index proves valuable for mean-variance investors in asset allocation, leading to significant economic gains. The study identifies the index's ability to capture the reversal of temporary price crashes caused by overreactions to climate change risk. Furthermore, it exhibits stronger return forecasting capability for green stocks, non-SOE stocks, and stocks in regions with low air pollution. Particularly during periods of low air pollution and relaxed regulation, the index displays an enhanced ability to forecast returns. The study's findings provide valuable insights for policymakers and financial institutions as they address 21st-century environmental challenges. Moreover, these findings can inform the design of adaptive measures and interventions aimed at mitigating ecological risks and promoting sustainable economic growth.

Keywords: Climate risk modelling; Climate challenges; Investor concerns; Return predictability

## 1. Introduction

In the 21st century, climate change has emerged as one of the most pressing environmental challenges worldwide. The increasing concentrations of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, primarily driven by human activities, have led to rising global temperatures, sea-level rise, altered weather patterns, and numerous ecological disruptions. These environmental changes pose significant risks to ecosystems, biodiversity, and human well-being. To address the complex and far-reaching impacts of climate change, there is a critical need to develop robust ecological risk modeling and effective climate risk management strategies.

While climate change pertains to long-term changes in temperature and typical weather in a place, ecological risks refer to potential adverse effects on ecological receptors caused by exposure to stressors, which can be chemical, physical, or biological in nature. The relationship between climate change and ecological risk management is multifaceted. While distinct, it's important to note that climate change can act as a stressor exacerbating ecological risks. Climate change, at its core, results in alterations to the Earth's climatic patterns, leading to both immediate and long-term impacts on ecosystems, species distributions, and habitat integrity. Each of these ecological risks, among others, arising due to climate change, necessitates comprehensive risk management efforts. Firstly, climate-induced ecological risks have tangible repercussions for sectors that are intrinsically linked to natural resources and ecological services, such as agriculture, fisheries, and tourism. As these sectors face disruptions due to ecological imbalances, financial markets, heavily invested in these sectors, become susceptible to volatility. The fallout from unmanaged ecological risks extends beyond the directly affected sectors. For example, large-scale migration due to ecological disruptions (like sea-level rise) can place strain on urban infrastructures and lead to increased borrowing and financial strains on governments, influencing sovereign bond markets. By implementing robust ecological risk management strategies, we can buffer some of these financial shocks. Secondly, financial markets, recognizing the symbiosis between ecological health and economic stability, can play a proactive role. By incorporating ecological risk evaluations into investment decisions or lending criteria, financial institutions can incentivize industries to adopt sustainable practices, thus bolstering both ecological and financial resilience. In the end, just as financial markets can be affected by ecological risks, they also play a role in exacerbating or mitigating them. Investment in unsustainable practices can heighten ecological risks, while green financing or impact investing can promote practices that mitigate these risks. This study, evidently bridges the domains of climate change, financial market dynamics, and ecological risk management.

Ecological risk modeling plays a crucial role in quantifying and understanding the potential consequences of climate change on ecological systems. It involves the analysis and assessment of the interdependencies between climate variables, ecological processes, and their cascading effects across different spatial and temporal scales. By employing mathematical models, simulations, and data-driven approaches, ecological risk modeling enables the evaluation of the vulnerabilities, sen

sitivities, and adaptive capacities of ecosystems in the face of climate change & risk. Furthermore, effective risk management strategies are essential to mitigate and manage the potential impacts of climate change on the environment and society. These strategies encompass a range of measures, including policy interventions, adaptive management approaches, and informed decision-making processes. Robust climate risk management frameworks enable policymakers, stakeholders, and communities to identify and prioritize actions that reduce exposure to climate-related hazards, enhance resilience, and foster sustainable development.

While the nexus between climate change and financial markets has garnered considerable attention, the nuances of their relationship vary significantly among scholars. Hong et al. [2019] initiated a novel interdisciplinary approach that connects climatic observations, particularly those concerning global temperatures and drought risks, to financial market dynamics. However, while they present empirical climate data as a determinant of financial implications, it remains ambiguous whether the direct correlation can be solely attributed to these climatic factors or if other externalities play a role. Choi et al. [2020] postulated a direct correlation between investors' attention to climate change and a heightened perception of climate risk. This perspective, though compelling, leans heavily on the assumption that attention directly translates to action within financial markets, which may be an oversimplification. They observed underperformance of carbon-intensive stocks in warmer periods, but external market factors during these periods are not adequately controlled for. The work by Engle et al. [2020] is pioneering, introducing dynamic hedging strategies catered to climate change. However, as these strategies are still nascent, their robustness and viability over the long term are yet to be tested comprehensively. Giglio et al. [2021]'s overview, while broad, arguably lacks in-depth critical analysis on certain key issues, acting more as a primer than a conclusive treatise. The unpredictable nature of future climate regulations, as highlighted by Ilhan et al. [2021], is an essential consideration. However, it warrants a deeper exploration of how different sectors may be affected disproportionately. The methodological contribution by Faccini et al. [2021] in devising proxies for climate risks is commendable, yet its reliance on textual analysis could introduce biases or overlook nuances that other methods might capture. Bolton and Kacperczyk [2021] provides a pivotal examination of the carbon emissions-stock returns paradigm, yet there's scope for further exploring underlying factors affecting investor behavior. Both Pástor et al. [2021] and Pástor et al. [2022] advocate for the potential superiority of green stocks, but the volatile nature of markets means that this superiority might not always hold true across different economic cycles or geopolitical scenarios. Lastly, Alekseev et al. [2022]'s methodology for hedging climate risks is innovative. Yet, its real-world application and scalability across different investment scenarios and market conditions remain to be critically assessed.

However, previous research mostly focused on the impact of climate change concern on the return spread between green and brown stocks, paying little attention to the effect of climate change concern on aggregate stock market returns. Given the exogenous and widespread shock

of climate change risk, investors may decrease their investment confidence in the aggregate stock market due to uncertainty about the future. Against this backdrop, we introduce a novel metric for quantifying both climate change and associated risks. We then analyze the relationship between this metric and the instability of financial markets.

Our research not only sheds light on the societal attitudes towards climate change & risk but also provides insights into the financial risks associated with environmental challenges. Through the integration of ecological risk modeling and risk management perspectives, our study aims to enhance our understanding of the complex interactions between climate change & risk, societal concerns, and financial market stability. By doing so, we hope to contribute to the development of effective strategies for mitigating ecological risks and addressing the environmental challenges posed by climate change & risk.

In this paper, we quantify climate change & risk by using climate change concerns. Climate change attention and climate change concerns are related yet distinct concepts in understanding investor behaviors and perceptions. Climate change attention refers to the acknowledgment and recognition of climate change as an issue. It's the preliminary stage wherein investors are becoming aware of the existence of climate change and its potential implications. At this stage, investors might seek information or track climate change-related news without necessarily evaluating its impact or consequences on their investments. It represents the volume or frequency of references to climate change in the information sources. Going beyond mere attention, concerns indicate a deeper understanding and evaluation of the potential risks and negative consequences associated with climate change [Ardia et al., 2022]. When we speak of "concern," we refer to the degree of perceived severity or urgency that investors assign to the risks of climate change. It's the next stage after attention, where the focus is on comprehending the implications of climate change, particularly in relation to financial markets and investments. This includes apprehensions about how climate change can impact future profitability, valuations, and overall market dynamics. In our study, when we quantify climate change and risk using climate change concerns, we are capturing the depth of investors' evaluations about the potential negative consequences of climate change on their investments. This encompasses not just the frequency or volume of mentions (as in attention) but also the intensity of sentiments associated with those mentions. By adopting the textual analysis approach as proposed by Ardia et al. [2022], we delve into understanding how these concerns, derived from various information sources in the Chinese stock market, influence investor behaviors and market trends.

Moreover, we investigate its capacity to forecast the aggregate stock market excess return directly. To capture investors' climate change concern, we follow Ardia et al. [2022] and use textual analysis to obtain climate change concerns from several influential information sources in the Chinese stock market. We then extract common components from the climate change concerns from different information sources using information aggregating methods, partial least squares (PLS)

(Huang et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2022). We also employ an alternative aggregating method, scaled principal component analysis (sPCA) (Huang et al., 2022), for robustness.

To construct the aggregate climate change concern index (ACCC) at the market level, we select five information sources covering three categories: (1) Influential media (media concern), including CCTV (China Central Television) News, YiMagazine, and National Business Daily $ ^{1} $; (2) Analyst reports; and (3) Comments in stock forums. First, we identify articles pertaining to climate change & risk using a climate change lexicon. Subsequently, we calculate an article-level concern score by analyzing the frequency of various lexicons, including a risk lexicon and a sentiment lexicon. Moving forward, we compute daily and monthly climate change concerns for each individual source. Lastly, we employ a dimensionality reduction method, namely Partial Least Squares (PLS), to aggregate the climate change concerns from each individual source into a unified climate change concern index.

In the empirical tests, we observe that the ACCC index constructed using the PLS method, denoted as  $ C^{PLS} $, exhibits superior in-sample performance in predicting stock market returns. In the in-sample regression analysis, the coefficients are consistently positive and statistically significant across all horizons (monthly, 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month), indicating that the predictability persists for up to one year. When comparing  $ C^{PLS} $ with climate change concern indexes constructed from individual information sources, our aggregate index demonstrates better predictive performance, suggesting that the aggregation method effectively extracts more valuable information for return prediction. To ensure robustness, we conduct three additional tests using alternative ACCC indexes: one constructed using the sPCA method ( $ C^{sPCA} $), one utilizing an alternative climate change lexicon ( $ C^{PLS_C} $), and one utilizing an alternative risk lexicon ( $ C^{PLS_R} $). The results from these robustness checks confirm the findings obtained with the original PLS-based ACCC index. Furthermore, we compare the forecasting power of our ACCC index ( $ C^{PLS} $) with 14 commonly used economic predictors, as outlined by [Welch and Goyal, 2008], and 12 policy risk/uncertainty indexes, as outlined by [Baker et al., 2016]. We discover that  $ C^{PLS} $ exhibits stronger predictability compared to the individual economic predictors and policy risk/uncertainty indexes commonly employed. Remarkably, our ACCC index ( $ C^{PLS} $) incorporates additional information that surpasses the predictive ability of all economic predictors, as well as risk and uncertainty indexes, in forecasting stock market returns.

Subsequently, we conduct an out-of-sample test using the  $ R_{OOS}^{2} $ metric proposed by [Campbell and Thompson, 2008] and the mean squared forecasting errors (MSFE)-adjusted statistic introduced by [Clark and West, 2007]. The findings reveal that the ACCC index, denoted as  $ C^{PLS} $, demonstrates superior predictability across all horizons. Moreover, the out-of-sample forecasting

power of the climate change concern index,  $ C^{PLS} $, surpasses that of all individual concern indexes, indicating its stronger ability to predict stock market returns.

Furthermore, we delve into the examination of the economic gains derived from the out-of-sample forecasts, considering an asset allocation perspective. The findings reveal that the forecasts indeed generate significant economic value for a mean-variance investor. Specifically, the ACCC index,  $ C^{PLS} $, exhibits positive annualized certainty equivalent return (CER) gains across all horizons. Importantly, our asset allocation results remain robust even when incorporating a transaction cost of 50 basis points (bp).

To understand why climate change concern can positively predict future stock market returns, we explore the underlying economic mechanism. Following Barber and Odean [2008] and Da et al. [2011], investor attention can lead to temporary price pressure in the short run and a reversal in the long run. Individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing stocks. The buying flow temporarily pushes up the price, and this attention-driven price pressure reverts to fundamentals subsequently. Due to climate change risk being a negative and exogenous shock for prices, we assume that climate change concern can cause a temporary price decrease in the short run and a reversal (increase) in the long run. As we all know, the Chinese stock market is dominated by individual (retail) investors (Liu et al., 2019; Carpenter et al., 2021). The effects may be more pronounced because there is likely to exist an overreaction in the short run when individual investors face complicated and unknown bad news (Howe, 1986; Da et al., 2011). Moreover, as highlighted in prior literature, the substantial overreaction to news in financial markets can be attributed to limited rationality (De Long et al., 1990; Bordalo et al., 2020). Limited rationality acknowledges that real-world decision-making is often less than perfectly rational and is influenced by various psychological and external factors. Specifically, climate change news, often perceived as negative news in the financial market, tends to trigger a short-term price slump (overreaction), followed by a long-term price reversal where prices gradually revert to their intrinsic value. In empirical tests, we find that our results support our hypotheses. We find that high climate change concern increases net selling in the current month. However, high climate change concern significantly increases net buying in the next month, and the net buying weakens over time. Therefore, climate change concern has positive predictability for future stock market returns.

To gain further insights into the economic mechanism at play, we extend our investigation to examine the cross-sectional impacts of climate change concern on stock returns. Our findings reveal that the positive predictability observed is particularly pronounced for green stocks, non-state-owned enterprises (non-SOEs), and stocks located in regions with lower levels of air pollution. These results indicate that stocks that are inherently challenging to value and subject to limited arbitrage opportunities within the context of climate change exhibit a higher degree of sensitivity to mispricing driven by investor attention. These findings align with the behavioral theories proposed by Baker and Wurgler [2006] and Da et al. [2011].

Furthermore, we explore the economic mechanism by examining the time-series variation in return predictability associated with climate change concern, akin to the consideration of business cycles in return predictability analysis by [Neely et al., 2014]. Our findings suggest that the out-of-sample return predictability is more pronounced during periods of low air pollution compared to periods of high air pollution. This indicates that investors display heightened sensitivity to climate change risks during low air pollution periods, as the impact of climate change is already reflected in stock prices during high air pollution periods. Additionally, we find that predictability is stronger during periods of loose regulation, as the climate change risk is already factored into stock prices during periods of stricter regulation.

Our paper makes three significant contributions to the existing literature. Firstly, we effectively quantified climate change and its associated risks through the application of text-mining techniques, inspired by the economic policy uncertainty index by Baker and Wurgler [2006], which effectively captures pertinent information regarding climate change concerns from various sources within the financial markets. By doing so, we contribute to the understanding of ecological risk modeling and provide a framework for assessing environmental challenges in the context of climate change & risk. Secondly, our research robustly demonstrates the significant predictive power of climate change concern for stock market returns, both within the sample and in out-of-sample scenarios, shedding light on the financial risks associated with environmental challenges and also resulting in significant economic gains for mean-variance investors. By investigating the potential impact of climate change concerns on stock market performance, we contribute to the field of risk management within the context of environmental challenges. Our findings can aid in the development of proactive strategies for managing and mitigating these risks. Thirdly, in addition to its predictive power for stock returns, we establish that climate change concern extends its influence to the broader financial market level [Choi et al., 2020] and [Pástor et al., 2022]. Drawing from the framework proposed by [Cochrane, 2008], our research advances the understanding of the role of climate change concern in finance by highlighting its influence on the aggregate financial markets. By elucidating the correlation between climate change concern and the instability of financial markets, our study offers invaluable insights to policymakers and financial institutions in their endeavors to confront the environmental challenges of the 21st century. Our findings have the potential to guide the development of adaptive strategies and interventions aimed at mitigating ecological risks and fostering sustainable economic growth.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: section 2 describes the data and the methodology for aggregating climate change concern. section 3 provides the empirical results. section 5 investigates the economic mechanism of the forecasting power. section 6 presents the robustness checks, and section 7 concludes.

## 2. Data and climate change concern

### 2.1. Climate change concern data collection

Following Huynh and Xia [2021] and Pástor et al. [2021], we use five sources of information to construct the climate change concern index for individual information sources. These five sources of information fall into three categories: (1) Influential media (media concern), including CCTV (China Central Television) News, YiMagazine, and National Business Daily. Following Engle et al. [2020] and Ardia et al. [2022], we first take the news in influential media into consideration. We select CCTV News due to its significance as one of China's primary media sources and the most-watched television program in the country. YiMagazine and National Business Daily are chosen because they are prominent media sources among financial market participants, listed in Hurun's top 10 Chinese financial media. Additionally, the availability of complete text archives for these three media sources since July 2012 offers us flexibility in constructing the climate change concern index using raw news content. (2) Analyst reports. We consider analyst reports, which play a crucial role in information acquisition and dissemination within financial markets. Analyst reports serve as important carriers of analyst information collection and dissemination, as highlighted by Asquith et al. [2005], Huang et al. [2014], and Huang et al. [2018]. Moreover, the CSMAR database provides access to abstracts (summaries) of the analyst reports. (3) Comments in stock forums. We incorporate comments from stock forums, which reflect individual investors' views and evaluations of individual stocks or the overall market. Comments related to climate change also indicate the individual investors' concerns regarding this topic, as noted by Antweiler and Frank [2004], Das and Chen [2007], and Chen et al. [2014]. We utilize web scraping technology to retrieve comments from stock forums on eastmoney.com. Next, we define an information universe of s=1, ..., S sources. Each day, from t=1 to T, these sources provide n=1, ..., N,s articles related to climate change. For each of these articles, we compute a measure of climate change concern based on textual information. We then aggregate the article-level climate change concern at the daily horizon to construct our climate change concern index for each individual information source.

#### 2.1.1. The articles related to climate change

The initial step in constructing the climate change concern indexes involves identifying articles that are relevant to climate change. In this study, we adopt the approach outlined by Engle et al. [2020] and create a climate change lexicon based on four official documents issued by Chinese authorities. These documents include the White Paper on China's Policies and Actions to Address Climate Change, Guidelines on Coordinating and Strengthening Work Related to Climate Change and Ecological and Environmental Protection, National Climate Change Adaptation Strategy 2035, and Xi Jinping's Speech at the Leaders' Climate Summit. We aggregate the content of these four documents to form a "Climate Change Vocabulary (CCV)," which consists of a unique list of terms (stemmed unigrams and bigrams) and their corresponding frequencies in the aggregated corpus.

Subsequently, we select the climate change-related words from the CCV to construct the climate change lexicon. To determine the relevance of each article to climate change, we calculate the frequency of climate change words within each article. An article is identified as being related to climate change when the number of climate change words exceeds zero.

#### 2.1.2. Article-level concerns

We adopt the definition of concern from [Ardia et al., 2022], which refers to the attention given to the risk of climate change and its associated negative consequences. To measure this concern, we assign a score derived from the information content of multiple sources. The score comprises two lexicons: a risk lexicon to gauge the degree of attention given to climate change risk events and a sentiment lexicon to determine the risk level. The sentiment lexicon is based on the approach used by [Jiang et al., 2019] and several commonly used Chinese lexicons $ ^{2} $. The risk lexicon is constructed and adjusted based on the risk dictionary in the LWIC dictionary [Kim, 2013]. The positive, negative, and risk lexicons constructed in this paper can be found in the online appendix $ ^{3} $.

We then define the "concern score" employing the lexicons we construct above and the details are as follows Equation 1:

 $$  concern_{n,t,s}=100\times\left(\frac{RW_{n.t,s}}{N_{n.t,s}}\right)\times\left(\frac{NW_{n.t,s}-PW_{n.t,s}}{NW_{n.t,s}+PW_{n.t,s}}+1\right)/2 $$ 

where n, t, and s respectively denote article, day, and information source,  $ RW_{n.t.s} $ is the number of risk words,  $ NW_{n.t.s} $ is the number of negative words,  $ PW_{n.t.s} $ is the number of positive words, and  $ N_{n.t.s} $ is the total number of words. The model consists of two parts: The first part is  $ \frac{RW_{n.t.s}}{N_{n.t.s}} $, which represents the percentage of risk words in the text. Using the percentage instead of the raw number of risk words accounts for the variability in the news articles' lengths. The second part is  $ \left(\frac{NW_{n.t.s}-PW_{n.t.s}}{NW_{n.t.s}+PW_{n.t.s}}+1\right)/2 $, which measures the sentiment of an article. The range is from 0 (the most positive) to 1 (the most negative). Thus, our article-level concern score can be interpreted as a weighted textual risk measure, where a higher (lower) weight is attributed when a text is more negative (positive).

#### 2.1.3. Daily- and monthly-level concerns

Next, we construct daily-level concerns for every information source and average the daily-level concerns to construct the monthly-level concern of every information source. We define the daily concern score at day t for a given source s as the sum of the article-level concern scores across the  $ N_{t,s} $ climate change-related articles Equation 2:

 $$ c o n c e r n_{t,s}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t,s}}c o n c e r n_{n,t,s}. $$ 

We compute the daily score by using the sum instead of the average because we want to capture the attention at a given time and for a given source. Moreover, if there is no news released about climate change  $ (N_{t,s}=0) $, the concern score in the Equation 2 is zero, which is the most positive. We assume that no news is good news following [Gerlagh and Liski, 2018].

Considering the heterogeneity between information sources, we compute the standardized daily concern scores following [Baker et al., 2016]. Specifically, we first compute the standard deviation of the concern index of a given source over a time period from  $ t_1 $ to  $ t_2 $ ( $ 1 < t_1 < t_2 < T $) Equation 3:

 $$ \sigma_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\mathrm{concern}_{t,s}-\overline{\mathrm{concern}_{s}}\right)^{2}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}} $$ 

where  $ \overline{concern}_{s} $ is the mean of the concern index from  $ t_{1} $ to  $ t_{2} $. Then, we employ the standard deviation to standardize the concern index of a given source over a time period from 1 to T Equation 4:

 $$ \mathrm{s c o n c e r n}_{t,s}=\frac{\mathrm{c o n c e r n}_{t,s}}{\sigma_{s}}. $$ 

To capture the monthly level of concern index, we compute the mean of the daily concern index in a given month Equation 5:

 $$ \mathrm{mconcern}_{m,s}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m,s}}\mathrm{concern}_{t,s}}{N_{m,s}}, $$ 

where  $ N_{m,s} $ is the number of days in a given month m.

### 2.2. Aggregate climate change concern

In this subsection, we aim to use the monthly individual concern indexes of different information sources collectively. Following Chen et al. [2022], we extract the common component of the concern indexes from different information sources by removing noise.

#### 2.2.1. Factor structure model

Following Chen et al. [2022], we construct a predictive model based on climate change concern Equation 6:

 $$ r_{t+1}=\alpha+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}C_{n,t}^{*}+\varepsilon_{t+1}, $$ 

where  $ r_{t+1} $ is the excess stock return in month  $ t+1 $,  $ C_{n,t}^{*} $ is the true but unobservable climate change concern in month t, and  $ \varepsilon_{t+1} $ is the noise term.

Next, we assume a linear factor structure for climate change concerns from different sources. Let  $ C_t = (C_{1,t}, \cdots, C_{N,t})' $ denote an  $ N \times 1 $ vector of individual climate change concerns at time period  $ t $, where  $ N $ is the number of information sources, which is five in our paper. The structural model for  $ C_{i,t} $ is as follows Equation 7:

 $$ C_{i,t}=\mu_{i,0}+\mu_{i,1}C_{t}^{*}+\mu_{i,2}E_{t}+e_{i,t}, $$ 

where  $ C_t^* $ is the true but unobservable climate change concern in Equation 6,  $ \mu_{i,1} $ is the factor loading that captures the sensitivity of climate change concern proxy  $ C_{i,t} $ to the true climate change concern  $ C_t^* $,  $ E_t $ is the common approximation error component of all the climate change concern proxies that are irrelevant to stock returns, and  $ e_{i,t} $ is the idiosyncratic noise associated with measure i only.

When constructing a climate change concern index from multiple information sources, previous methods have focused on summing standardized indexes individually to create an aggregate climate change concern (Choi et al., 2020; Engle et al., 2020; Ardia et al., 2022). However, the direct summing method used to construct the ACCC index may introduce much noise when forecasting returns. To mitigate this issue, we propose incorporating dimensionality reduction techniques that consider the relationship between individual climate change concern indexes and future returns, such as Partial Least Squares (PLS) (Huang et al., 2015) and sparse Principal Component Analysis (sPCA) (Huang et al., 2022) methods. The details of the two dimensionality reduction methods are introduced as follows.

#### 2.2.2. Partial least squares (PLS) model

Following Huang et al. [2015], the PLS approach extracts the PLS component  $ (A_t^{PLS}) $ from the climate change concern proxies according to their covariance with the future stock excess return and chooses a linear combination of the climate change concerns that is optimal for forecasting. First, we regress each climate change concern in month t on the future realized excess stock return in month  $ t+1 $ in a time-series regression Equation 8:

 $$ C_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}r_{t+1}+\varepsilon_{i,t}, $$ 

where  $ C_{i,t} $ is the ith individual concern proxy, the coefficient  $ \beta_i $ captures the sensitivity of the concern proxy  $ C_{i,t} $ to the climate change concern  $ C_t^* $ instrumented by future stock return  $ r_{t+1} $. As the future stock return  $ r_{t+1} $ is driven by  $ C_t^* $, climate change concern proxies are related to the predictable component of stock returns and are uncorrelated with the unpredictable errors.

Secondly, we regress each climate change concern proxy  $ (C_{i,t}) $ on the estimated  $ \hat{\beta}_{i} $ from Equation 8 for each month t Equation 9:

 $$ C_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+C_{t}^{P L S}\hat{\beta}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i,t}, $$ 

where  $ \hat{\beta}_{i} $ represents the estimated coefficient in Equation 8, and  $ C_{t}^{PLS} $, which is the coefficient for  $ \hat{\beta}_{i} $, is the PLS climate change concern measure at time t.

Following Kelly and Pruitt [2015], the PLS approach is employed to eliminate the measurement errors (noise) of the proxies. Specifically, the first-stage regression takes an errors-in-variables form, and the second-stage regression produces an estimation for a unique but unknown rotation of the latent factor  $ (C_{t}^{*}) $.

To estimate  $ C_t^{PLS} $, we employ the full sample data from July 2012 to December 2021. We then explore  $ C_t^{PLS} $'s in-sample return predictability according to Equation 6. In the out-of-sample forecasting, the PLS two-stage regressive approach is repeated to obtain the time-series  $ C^{PLS} $ at month t using the data no later than month t. Specifically, in the first stage, the returns we can use in Equation 8 are from month 2 to t, and the observations of the climate change concern proxy are from month 1 to t-1. In the second stage, we run the cross-sectional regressions for months 1 through t. Moreover, following Light et al. [2017] and Chen et al. [2022], due to the stable relationship between climate change concern and expected stock return over time, the coefficient  $ \beta_i $ can be estimated more precisely by using the mean value of  $ \beta_i $ over all previous periods rather than the most recent  $ \beta_i $.

#### 2.2.3. Scaled principal component analysis (sPCA) model

In addition to the PCA model, we use the sPCA model recently proposed by Huang et al. [2022]. The advantage of the sPCA model is that it scales each variable according to its effect on the target. The sPCA can be implemented in two steps.

Firstly, the predictability of each climate change concern  $ (C_{i,t}) $ from different sources for the realized stock excess return  $ (r_{t+1}) $ can be evaluated using the following regression Equation 10:

 $$ r_{t+1}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}C_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{t+1}. $$ 

From the above model, we obtain the scaled coefficient  $ \beta_{i} $, and a panel of scaled climate change concerns  $ (\beta_{1}C_{1,t}, \beta_{2}C_{2,t}, \ldots, \beta_{N}C_{N,t}) $.

In the second step, the conventional PCA is applied to  $ \left(\beta_{1}X_{1,t},\beta_{2}X_{2,t},\ldots,\beta_{N}X_{N,t}\right) $, and the panel of scaled predictors and the first principal component is the sPCA-based ACCC,  $ C^{sPCA} $. In out-of-sample forecasting, we recursively estimate the Equation 10 using the data no later than month t, which is consistent with the PLS method.

The null hypothesis is that  $ C_{n,t} $ has no correlation with the future stock market excess return, and  $ \beta_{n}=0 $. Following Barber and Odean [2008], due to limited investor attention, the high attention to climate change risk may lead to an overreaction of price crash in the short run and the price reversal (increase) in the long run. Moreover, the Chinese A-share stock market is mainly made up of individual investors (Liu et al., 2019; Carpenter et al., 2021), which can exacerbate this effect. However, previous literature investigates the market effect of investor attention using daily and weekly data, and the reversal often exists in one month. When using monthly data in our paper, the high attention to climate change risk leads to a high stock excess return in the next month(s), which supports the argument of [Barber and Odean, 2008]. We empirically test the economic mechanism in section 5.

Previous studies in the field of climate change concern have often focused on constructing a climate change risk index and examining the performance of green stocks relative to brown stocks as climate change concerns increase (Choi et al., 2020; Engle et al., 2020; Ardia et al., 2022) from a risk-exposure perspective. However, these studies have primarily considered the current impacts of climate change concerns. In contrast, our paper takes into account both the current and future impacts of climate change concerns. We find evidence of an overreaction (net selling) when climate change concerns increase, with subsequent stock price reversals discussed in subsection 5.1. We attribute this short-term overreaction to investors' limited rationality. In particular, in China, where there is a lack of sufficient and credible data on carbon emissions, coupled with the dominance of individual investors in the Chinese A-share stock market (Liu et al., 2019; Carpenter et al., 2021), we observe a pervasive fear among investors when climate change concerns intensify. This fear leads to net selling of their holdings, thus establishing a positive relationship between climate change concerns and future stock returns. Moreover, our findings in subsubsection 5.2.1 indicate that the price reversal effect is more pronounced in green stocks, providing support for our hypothesis.

Moreover, we conduct a comparative analysis in subsection 3.3 and subsection 3.4 to assess the predictability of our ACCC index against commonly used predictors such as economic variables and

economic policy uncertainty indexes. We employ bivariate regression to examine their respective performance. Our findings reveal that the ACCC index exhibits superior return predictability compared to the majority of commonly used predictors. Additionally, when including these existing predictors in the regression model, the predictability of the ACCC index further improves. This suggests that the ACCC index incorporates valuable additional information for forecasting returns than existing predictors.

### 4.2. Asset allocation performance

In this subsection, we evaluate the economic value of the predictive power of ACCC index from an asset allocation perspective. Consistent with [Campbell and Thompson, 2008] and Neely et al. [2014], we use the Sharpe ratio (SR) and certainty-equivalent return (CER) gains to assess economic value. We assume a mean-variance investor who optimizes asset allocation between risky stocks and risk-free assets $ ^{5} $ based on stock return forecasts. We calculate the portfolio equity weights as follows Equation 15:

 $$ w_{t}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{\hat{r}_{t+1}}{\sigma\widehat{\sigma}_{t+1}^{2}} $$ 

where  $ \hat{r}_{t+1} $ is the forecast of the stock excess return,  $ \hat{\sigma}_{t+1}^{2} $ is the forecast of the variance in a two-year moving window, and  $ \gamma $ is the investor's risk-aversion coefficient. Owing to our relatively short-period sample, we select a shorter-period moving window (two-year) to estimate the variance than five-year moving window of Campbell and Thompson [2008]. In addition, we also constrain

 $ w_{t} $ to lie between 0 and 1.5, to exclude short sales and to allow for at most 50% leverage, and set  $ \gamma $ to five, following Campbell and Thompson [2008]. Therefore, the investor then allocates  $ 1 - w_{t} $ of the portfolio to risk-free assets, and the  $ t+1 $ realized portfolio return is Equation 16:

 $$ r_{t+1}^{p}=r_{t+1}w_{t}+r_{t+1}^{f} $$ 

where  $ r_{t+1}^f $ is the risk-free return. The certainty equivalent return (CER) of the portfolio is Equation 17:

 $$ C E R_{p}=\hat{\mu}_{p}-0.5\gamma\hat{\sigma}_{p}^{2} $$ 

where, we compute the sample mean  $ \hat{\mu}_p $ and sample variance  $ \hat{\sigma}_p^2 $. The CER gains represent the difference between the CER of the given portfolio and that of the benchmark portfolio. To obtain the annual percentage, we multiply the CER gain by 1200. We also consider a proportion transaction cost of 50 basis points (bps) to ensure robustness. In addition, we employ the annualized Sharpe ratios of the portfolio  $ r_t^p $ to evaluate investment performance.

Table 5 also presents the asset allocation performance results in Panel B and C. In Panel B, we observe that the ACCC index ( $ C^{PLS} $) exhibits positive CER gains at all horizons, which is consistent with the out-of-sample results presented in Panel A of Table 5. Notably, at the monthly horizon,  $ C^{PLS} $ produces the largest CER gain (6.771%), indicating that an investor may be willing to pay up to 677 basis points annually to access  $ C^{PLS} $'s return forecasts. In Panel C, we find the positive CER gains of the ACCC index ( $ C^{PLS} $) persist after incorporating a transaction cost of 50 basis points. Furthermore, investment based on the return forecasts leads to sizable Sharpe ratios.

In summary, the out-of-sample return forecasts of  $ C^{PLS} $ have the potential to generate substantial investment profits for a mean-variance investor. This test further emphasizes the significance of climate change concern in the context of the aggregate stock market from an asset allocation perspective.

By demonstrating that the ACCC index has the potential to generate investment profits, our findings highlight the importance of considering ecological asset risks within investment decision-making processes. ## 5. Economic explanations

In this paper, we provide evidence that a high level of climate change is associated with a future increase in stock returns. Subsequently, we explore the potential economic mechanism underlying this predictability. The evidence we provide regarding the association between a high level of climate change and future increases in stock returns contributes to ecological risk management by highlighting the financial incentives for addressing ecological risks. It promotes sustainable investing practices, informs decision-making processes, and encourages stakeholders to actively engage in ecological risk management efforts. By recognizing the financial implications of climate change on stock returns, stakeholders are motivated to prioritize ecological sustainability and integrate environmental considerations into their risk management strategies.

### 5.1. Climate change concern and aggregate order imbalance

In Figure 1, we observe that policies and conferences related to climate change can cause an increase in investor climate change concern. From the perspective of information sources, climate change concern can be regarded as a form of investor attention. High climate change concern can result in a temporary price collapse and a subsequent price reversal in the long run, following Barber and Odean [2008], as a negative shock to the market. The positive impact of climate change concern on future stock excess returns in our empirical tests supports the aforementioned hypothesis. Specifically, there exists a temporary price collapse and a price reversal in the long run when high climate change concern is present for the following reasons. Since the Chinese A-share market is dominated by individual investors (Liu et al., 2019; Carpenter et al., 2021), the impact of policies and conferences on climate change may not be fully understood by all investors in the short run. Additionally, when investors observe policies and conferences related to climate change, climate change concern increases. Faced with complex and unfamiliar negative news, individual investors may be net-sellers, resulting in a short-term price collapse or overreaction due to limited processing and analytical ability (Howe, 1986; Da et al., 2011). In the long run, as the information gradually becomes reflected in stock prices, a reversal to the fundamentals will occur (Howe, 1986; Chen et al., 2022).

To test the economic mechanism, we investigate the impacts of climate change concern on aggregate order flow. Following Lee and Ready [1991], Baker and Wurgler [2006] and Chen et al. [2022], we define monthly order flow at the firm level as Equation 18:

 $$ OF_{i,t}=\frac{TNB_{i,t}-TNS_{i,t}}{TNB_{i,t}+TNS_{i,t}}, $$ 

where i denotes a given firm and t denotes a given month. OF is buy-sell imbalance. TNB is the total number of purchases of a stock and TNS is the total number of sales of a stock. The tick-by-tick transaction data employed in calculating OF are from CSMAR database over the time period from 2012:07 to 2021:12. The aggregate market-level buy-sell imbalance  $ AOF_{t} $ is the value-weighted  $ OF_{i,t} $. Following Chen et al. [2022], we design a regressive model to investigate the relationship between climate change concern and aggregate order flow Equation 19:

 $$ \Delta AOF_{t+h}=\alpha+\beta_{1}C_{t}+\beta_{2}\ Ret_{t}+\beta_{3}\ Ret_{t-1}+\beta_{4}\ Ret_{t-2}+\varepsilon_{t+h}, $$ 

where  $ \Delta AOF_{t+h} $ denotes the change in  $ AOF_t $ over the horizon h, and h=0, 1, 6, 12 months;  $ C_t $ denotes one of the aggregate climate change concern indices; and  $ Ret_t $,  $ Ret_{t-1} $, and  $ Ret_{t-2} $ are stock market return at month t, t-1, and t-2, respectively.

### [PLEASE INSERT Table 6 HERE]

Table 6 presents the results of the regression analysis for the aggregate climate change concern indices. The analysis reveals that the aggregate climate change concern index,  $ C^{PLS} $, has a significantly negative impact ( $ \beta_{1}=-0.006 $, and t-value=-3.358) on the aggregate order flow when h=0, indicating that high climate change concern increases net selling contemporaneously. Furthermore, there exists a significantly positive relationship between climate change concern and aggregate order flow at monthly, semi-annual, and annual horizons. The results suggest that the information related to climate change induces an overreaction in stock net selling in the short run and stock net buying in the long run, supporting our hypothesis and contributing to the positive return predictability of the ACCC index. Overall, these findings are consistent with the in-sample results, highlighting the rationality of examining the economic mechanism through the order flow channel.

### 5.2. Cross-sectional analysis

#### 5.2.1. Predictability in green and brown

To further explore the economic mechanism, we examine the cross-sectional variation in the impact of ACCC index on order flows and stock returns. Building on the findings of [Choi et al., 2020] and [Pástor et al., 2022], who suggest that the stock returns of green firms can outperform those of brown firms during periods of unexpectedly strengthened climate change concerns, we investigate this phenomenon. Due to limited access to carbon emission data in China, we classify firms operating

in heavily polluting industries, as identified by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People's Republic of China in 2010 $ ^{6} $, as brown firms. The remaining firms are categorized as green firms. This classification allows us to create two portfolios: a green portfolio and a brown portfolio. We then analyze the cross-sectional variation in the impact of ACCC index on stock returns.

Based on our analysis, we observe a contemporaneous net selling of stocks and a subsequent reversal over the long run. We attribute this reversal to an overreaction triggered by the shock of climate change risk. Considering that green stocks are less affected by climate change risk compared to brown stocks [Choi et al., 2020] and [Pástor et al., 2022], we assume that the climate change risk is already priced into the market, and the contemporaneous net selling of green stocks exhibits a greater degree of overreaction than that of brown stocks. Consequently, we anticipate a larger reversal in green stocks in the future, which results in more significant and positive impacts (indicating stronger positive return predictability) of climate change concern on returns in the green stock portfolio. where  $ r_{t+h}^{i} $ is the average excess returns of each portfolio (green or brown portfolio) over the horizon h, and  $ C_{t} $ is the aggregate climate change concern index,  $ C^{PLS} $.

### 5.3. Seasonal analysis

Similar to previous literature on business cycles [Neely et al., 2014], we take seasonal factors into consideration, as investors' risk perception of climate change varies seasonally. Specifically, high air pollution days mostly occur in winter in China, according to the official air quality level classification. Two main reasons cause high air pollution in winter. First, the Chinese government provides coal-based central heating in cities, and rural residents burn coal to keep warm in the north of China during winter, causing large amounts of polluting gas emissions. Second, in winter, the ground temperature drops sharply, resulting in low air temperature and high upper air temperature, forming the "temperature inversion" phenomenon, with slow air flow that makes it difficult to spread pollutants (Almond et al., 2009; Li et al., 2021). Previous studies demonstrate a negative linear relationship between air pollution and the stock market, indicating that air pollution has an exogenous negative effect on the financial market (Lepori, 2016; Fang et al., 2021). When facing air pollution, investors pay more attention to climate change risk and learn more about its effects on the stock market. Then, climate change concern is, to some extent, reflected in stock price.

Therefore, if negative climate change news (policies) are issued during high air pollution periods, investors will analyze new information more rationally, resulting in lower price overreaction in the short run and lower price reversal in the future. In other words, the positive return predictability is weaker during high air pollution periods than during low air pollution periods.

### 5.4. Predictability before and after the issue of the environmental protection law

On January 1, 2015, the newly revised Environmental Protection Law, known as the "strictest in history," came into effect, targeting persistent environmental problems such as low penalties for violations and weak awareness of environmental protection. A significant feature of the new Environmental Protection Law is the introduction of daily fines without a ceiling, allowing for administrative detention of individuals responsible for illegal activities. The law emphasizes the government's role in supervision and management, as well as the disclosure of environmental information to the public, to facilitate citizen participation in environmental monitoring. From Figure 1, it is evident that the climate change concern index peaked around the time of the newly revised Environmental Protection Law. Consequently, we define the period before the enactment of the law

as the "loose-regulation period" (July 2012 to January 2015), and all other months as the "serious-regulation period." We assume that during the serious-regulation period, climate change risks are already incorporated into stock prices, resulting in weaker impacts of climate change concern on stock prices compared to the loose-regulation period. In other words, the return predictability of climate change concern is more pronounced during the loose-regulation period.

To compare the degree of return predictability across loose- and serious-regulation period, we compute the following intuitive versions of the conventional  $ R^{2} $ statistic in Equation 21 following [Neely et al., 2014]:

Where  $ I_t^{Loose} $ ( $ I_t^{Serious} $) is an indicator variable that takes a value of 1 when month t is in the loose- (serious-) regulation period and 0 otherwise;  $ \hat{\varepsilon}_{i,t} $ is the fitted residual based on the full-sample estimates of the predictive regression Model in Equation 6;  $ \bar{r} $ is the full-sample mean of  $ r_t $; and T is the number of usable observations for the full sample.

### 5.5. Ecological risk management implication

Regarding the economic rationale behind the novel measure of climate change and its associated risks, the results indicate a heightened capacity of the index to forecast returns for green stocks, non-SOE (State-Owned Enterprises) stocks, and stocks located in regions with low air pollution. Additionally, it demonstrates enhanced return forecasting ability during periods characterized by low air pollution levels and relaxed regulatory conditions. These findings hold several political and practical implications for ecological risk modelling, ecological risk management, and addressing environmental change. Firstly, the stronger return forecasting ability of the ACCC index for specific segments, such as green stocks, non-SOE stocks, and stocks in low air pollution regions, suggests that environmental factors play a significant role in the financial performance of these assets. This finding contributes to ecological risk modelling by highlighting the relevance of climate change & risk concerns and environmental conditions in assessing the risks and potential returns associated with specific segments of the market. Incorporating these insights into ecological risk models enhances the ability to quantify and analyze the ecological risks embedded in different asset classes. Secondly, the quantified relationship between the ACCC index and the return forecasting ability for green stocks, non-SOE stocks, and stocks in low air pollution regions provides valuable

insights for ecological risk management. Asset managers, investors, and policymakers can leverage this information to make informed decisions regarding portfolio allocation, investment strategies, and risk management practices. By recognizing the impact of climate change concerns on financial performance, stakeholders can prioritize investments in environmentally sustainable assets, promote responsible business practices, and manage ecological risks more effectively. In the end, the finding that the aggregate climate change concern index exhibits a stronger return forecasting ability during periods of low air pollution and loose regulation underscores the importance of addressing environmental change from both ecological and economic perspectives. Policymakers and regulators can utilize this information to advocate for stricter environmental regulations, incentivize green investment, and promote sustainable practices. Integrating climate change change & risk into regulatory frameworks and policy decisions can facilitate the transition towards a more sustainable and resilient economy, addressing environmental challenges and mitigating ecological risks.

## 7. Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the impact of climate challenges on financial markets by introducing an innovative methodology to quantify climate risk. This pioneering approach, known as the aggregate climate change concern index, draws upon diverse textual information sources, including analyst reports, influential financial and economic media, CCTV news, and Internet forum posts. We find that the aggregate climate change concern index,  $ C^{PLS} $, can positively and significantly predict subsequent monthly stock market returns. Moreover, this predictability persists over one year, but weakens over time. However, individual climate change concern indexes exhibit limited return predictability. Additionally, the aggregate climate change concern index,  $ C^{PLS} $, demonstrates strong out-of-sample forecasting power for stock market returns, generating substantial economic value for mean-variance investors in asset allocation.

Our empirical findings highlight the significant role of climate change concern in the financial markets in general, as climate change concern exhibits strong positive forecasting power for stock market returns both in-sample and out-of-sample. We further investigate the economic mechanism behind this positive predictability. The positive return predictability of climate change concern may arise from the reversal of temporary price collapse (overreaction) caused by net selling when facing unexpected climate change risks. Moreover, the positive return predictability is stronger in green stocks, non-SOEs, stocks in regions with low air pollution, during periods of low air pollution, and in periods of loose regulation, providing additional empirical evidence for the economic mechanism.

Our research emphasizes the importance of considering climate change & risk concerns in ecological risk management practices, both at the political and practical levels. Firstly, our aggregate climate change concern index offers a timely snapshot of market perceptions, allowing policymakers to gauge the immediacy and magnitude of climate concerns. This can guide the prioritization of issues in policy agendas. By observing which sectors or regions are most affected by climate concerns, policies can be tailored to address specific vulnerabilities. For instance, sectors displaying higher ecological risks might warrant stricter environmental standards or enhanced monitoring. Policymakers can design fiscal incentives, such as tax breaks or subsidies, to stimulate investments in sectors that align with climate adaptation and mitigation goals. Secondly, financial institutions can incorporate our index as a leading indicator in their risk assessment models. This would offer a more holistic view of an asset's risk profile, especially in long-term investments. The index can serve as a guide for institutions to diversify their portfolios, ensuring they're adequately hedged against emerging ecological risks. This might involve an increased allocation to sectors or regions that exhibit resilience to climate concerns. Given the growing interest in sustainable finance, our research can inform the development of new financial products or services that cater to environmentally-conscious investors. In the end, understanding regions or communities that are particularly vulnerable allows for more targeted community engagement and education efforts, emphasizing local actions that can mitigate climate risks. Moreover, regions displaying heightened

climate concerns may require infrastructure enhancements, such as improved water management systems in drought-prone areas or fortified constructions in regions susceptible to extreme weather events. In short words, our research not only underscores the importance of climate change & risk concerns but also provides a tangible framework for integrating these insights into practical ecological risk management efforts. We believe that by intertwining the knowledge garnered from our study with on-ground actions, we can effectively address the looming environmental challenges in the 21 $ ^{st} $ century.

There are several potential future research directions that can further contribute to ecological risk management in the 21st century. Firstly, future research can focus on developing more sophisticated ecological risk assessment models that incorporate the ACCC index and its impact on stock market returns. This can involve exploring advanced econometric techniques, machine learning approaches, or hybrid models to better understand the complex relationships between climate change concern, environmental factors, financial markets, and ecological risks (see, e.g., Frankel et al., 2022; Garcia et al., 2023). Enhancing risk assessment models can improve the accuracy and reliability of predicting ecological risks and their financial implications. Using advanced computational techniques, including deep learning or neural networks, we believe other researchers can gain a nuanced understanding of the intricate relationships we have explored. Moreover, our reliance on specific textual information sources for constructing the ACCC index might not capture the entirety of investor sentiment. Future studies might benefit from integrating a broader range of data sources, including non-textual information, to develop a more comprehensive understanding. We encourage researchers to expand the data scope, integrating diverse datasets, potentially incorporating non-traditional data sources like satellite imagery or social media sentiment, to get a holistic view of climate change concerns. Secondly, investigating the long-term economic and ecological effects of climate change concern and its influence on stock market returns can be a valuable avenue for research. This can involve studying the persistence of the relationship between climate change & risk concerns and financial markets' returns over extended time periods, assessing the ecological impacts of investor sentiment, and examining the feedback loops between environmental changes and financial markets. Understanding the long-term dynamics can provide insights into the sustainability of financial systems and the resilience of ecological assets (see, e.g., Avramov et al., 2021; Evgeniou et al., 2021). Thirdly, further research can explore the policy and regulatory implications of climate change & risk concerns on ecological risk management. This can involve examining the effectiveness of existing climate policies, evaluating the impact of regulatory frameworks on investor behavior and market outcomes, and identifying potential policy interventions to mitigate ecological risks. Investigating the interactions between climate change & risk concerns, policy interventions, and market dynamics can inform the design and implementation of effective policies for promoting sustainable investments and managing ecological risks. In the end, due to our research focuses on the Chinese markets, future research can expand to include international perspectives on climate

change & risk concerns and its implications for ecological risk management. Comparative studies across different countries or regions can shed light on the variations in investor behavior, policy approaches, and financial market responses to climate change & risk concerns. Understanding the international dimensions of ecological risk management can contribute to global efforts in addressing climate change & risk and promoting sustainable development.

##### • 2012

1). China air pollution crisis (Jan/2012 - Feb/2012)

Explanation: In 2012, China launched several pilot carbon trading programs in various cities and provinces. 3). The United States passed new fuel efficiency standards (Aug/2012)

Explanation: In August 2012, the United States passed new fuel efficiency standards for cars and light-duty trucks, requiring them to achieve an average of 54.5 miles per gallon by 2025. The standards are expected to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 6 billion metric tons over the lifetime of the vehicles.

4). The European Union implemented a carbon tax on airlines (Nov/2012)

Explanation: In November 2012, the European Union began implementing a carbon tax on airlines flying to and from European Union countries. The tax was designed to encourage airlines to reduce their greenhouse gas emissions and was controversial, with some countries and airlines opposing the tax.

The main goal of the conference was to make progress on several key issues related to climate change, including the extension of the Kyoto Protocol, which was set to expire at the end of 2012. The Kyoto Protocol is an international agreement that sets binding targets for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. At the conference, parties agreed to extend the protocol until 2020.
