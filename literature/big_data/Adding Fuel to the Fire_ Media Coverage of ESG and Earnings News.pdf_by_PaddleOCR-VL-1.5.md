## Abstract

In this study, we examine the extent to which financial media outlets shift their coverage during periods of heightened public attention and sentiment regarding ESG issues. Specifically, we examine coverage of corporate ESG news vis-à-vis a traditional corporate finance topic, earnings news. Our findings suggest that financial media shift focus away from earnings news and towards corporate ESG issues in response to broader ESG attention. Moreover, the sentiment of the remaining earnings news is positively associated with the sentiment of ESG news, even when the ESG sentiment conflicts with the underlying earnings. In examining the capital market implications of such shifts, we find that increased media focus on ESG news results in a weaker price reaction but larger trading volume during the earnings announcement window, particularly when the firm is held by more ESG-oriented investors, suggesting that investors are trading on mixed signals. Taken together, our findings suggest that financial media outlets respond to and amplify ESG sentiment, which undermines their role in facilitating the incorporation of key financial news into market prices.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, market participants, policy makers, and academics alike have devoted substantial attention and resources to environmental, social, and governance (ESG) issues. However, such issues, including the term “ESG” itself, have become controversial and fallen out of favor in many corners. One commonly cited concern (e.g., Friedman 1970, McKinsey 2022) is that corporations’ focus on ESG issues (often referred to as the “stakeholder view”) has distracted attention from critical financial issues, including profit maximization (often referred to as the “shareholder view”). Of course, ESG issues often are financial issues (Edmans 2022), but they need not be according to the SASB’s “financial materiality” principle and a vast literature on the subject (e.g., Li, Watts, and Zhu, 2024). In this study, we examine the extent to which financial media outlets shift their coverage during periods of heightened public attention and sentiment regarding ESG issues. Specifically, we examine coverage of corporate ESG news vis-à-vis a traditional corporate finance topic, earnings news. We also explore the capital market implications of such shifts.

The media serves as an information intermediary in capital markets by creating and disseminating information that can influence the perceptions of investors and the broader public (e.g., Blankespoor, deHaan, Zhu. 2018; Bushee, Core, Guay, and Hamm 2010; Guest 2021). For example, media coverage is effective at raising awareness of focal issues (e.g., Boykoff and Boykoff 2007, Sampei and Aoyagi-Usui 2009), sparking broader discussions (e.g., King, Schneer, White 2017), and stipulating appropriate behavior (e.g., Miller 2006). Similarly, media coverage can induce and amplify public sentiment. $ ^{1} $

However, media reporting can also reflect, and is often used to measure, the information of investors and the public (Chen, Hoberg, and Zhang 2024). Thus, the media can suffer from biases that arise from the need to be informed by, as well as the incentive to cater to, reader demand and tastes (Gentzkow and Shapiro 2006; Mullainathan and Shleifer 2005). With the growing popularity of and controversies around ESG issues, news reporting of these issues may be more vulnerable to biased reporting as outlets seek to agree with certain readers' ESG preferences or promote a specific agenda, especially during periods of heightened public attention and intense sentiment. Given the benefits of impartial news reporting discussed above, it is critical to understand how media coverage shifts in response to stronger reader demand for ESG news and how such shifts shape investor perceptions.
Our media data is from RavenPack and spans the years 2007 to 2023, for which RavenPack's ESG news flags are available. We construct two indexes of public (i.e., society-wide) ESG sentiment. First, following Engle, Giglio, Kelly, Lee, and Stroebel (2020), we construct ESG_AttentionIndex, which equals the percentage of all news articles in a quarter that are flagged as ESG news articles. Second, similar to Ardia, Bluteau, Boudt, and Inghelbrechi (2022), our second measure, ESG_SentimentIndex, is the average RavenPack article-level sentiment score of all ESG articles in a quarter. Note that articles are not required to be, and indeed are often not, related to a specific company in order to be included in constructing these indexes.
In examining the relationship between society-wide and company-specific ESG coverage, we acknowledge that causality likely goes both ways. Many events that get society-wide attention clearly influence firm-specific policies. For example, many firms appointed more black directors and established diversity, equity, and inclusion programs after the death of George Floyd (Balakrishnan, Copat, De la Parra, Ramesh 2023). However, there are also events that are firm-

specific, or at least originate at a particular firm, that end up influencing overall public sentiment (e.g., 2010 BP oil spill). Thus, our intention in using the society-wide indexes is not to establish whether, e.g., ESG issues originate at the societal- vs. firm-level. Rather, we aim to identify ESG shocks that are plausibly exogenous to the focal firm (in that they originate outside the focal firm), would influence firm-specific ESG coverage, and (by extension) would likely influence the mix of firm-specific ESG and/or financial coverage.

Prior literature suggests that in times with high market uncertainty news outlets shift their reporting resources from non-earnings news towards earnings news, which helps them to meet the increasing investor demand for key financial information (Bonsall, Green, and Muller 2020). Similarly, in times with heightened public ESG attention and sentiment, media outlets have an incentive to expand their coverage of corporate ESG news to meet the growing reader demand. To the extent that media outlets face resource constraints, the increasing focus on corporate ESG news would crowd-out non-ESG news. In this study, we operationalize non-ESG news using earnings announcements, which are among the most important events for public companies and typically center on financial as opposed to ESG performance.

Moreover, to attract reader attention, the media may connect their coverage of corporate ESG news to public sentiment, resulting in firm-specific ESG sentiment that is reflective of societal trends. Such injection of public sentiment into corporate ESG news coverage may also affect how they report earnings news. Moreover, prior studies have documented that media bias can arise due to the views of the owners, editors, and journalists (e.g., Baron 2006, Djankov, McLiesh, Nenova, Shleifer 2003). Consistent with this line of research, coverage of earnings news may be shaped by concurrent ESG news, particularly when media outlets possess a strong editorial stance on ESG issues. As a result, we expect earnings news sentiment to be positively associated

with ESG news sentiment, leading to inconsistency between the underlying earnings and the way it is covered by the media.

Consistent with our predictions, we identify a positive correlation between firm-level ESG news coverage (sentiment) and public ESG attention (sentiment). Moreover, our findings suggest that an increase in firm-level ESG news coverage crowds out earnings coverage. In particular, a one standard deviation increase in firm-level ESG news coverage is associated with a 2.2% decrease (or a 3.6% decrease from the mean) in earnings news coverage. We find similar results when we separately examine E, S, and G news. In addition, conditional on ESG news coverage, we find that ESG news sentiment is positively correlated with earnings news sentiment. Relatedly, we find that when ESG sentiment conflicts with the underlying earnings news (i.e., the direction of SUE) the association between the underlying earnings and the sentiment of earnings news is attenuated. Taken together, these findings suggest that greater society-wide ESG attention and sentiment are reflected in firm-specific news coverage, resulting in less earnings coverage (more ESG coverage) overall and making the remaining earnings news coverage more consistent with society-wide ESG sentiment instead of firm-specific financial performance.

Finally, we explore the capital market consequence of the increases in corporate ESG news coverage. Prior literature suggests that greater business press coverage of earnings news reduces mispricing and speeds up price discovery around earnings announcements (e.g., Drake et al. 2014; Guest 2021). Moreover, increased media coverage of extraneous news can distract investors and inhibit incorporation of earnings news (Hirshleifer, Lim, Teoh 2009). Thus, a shift in media focus from earnings news to ESG news may reduce the availability of earnings-related information and divert investor attention away from earnings news, leading to weaker immediate price and volume reaction to earnings surprises. On the other hand, heterogeneity in investors' ESG preferences

predicts higher abnormal trading volume during the earnings announcement window, as ESG investors who care about both ESG news and financial news trade against traditional investors who only care about financial news (e.g., Goldstein, Kopytov, Shen, and Xiang 2024).

Consistent with the hypothesis that the shift in media reporting from earnings news to ESG news may impede the price discovery process, we document smaller earnings response coefficients (ERC) for firms with more ESG news coverage. Specifically, a one standard deviation increase in ESG news coverage is associated with an 8.67% decrease in ERC. However, we also document higher abnormal trading volume during the earnings announcement window in firms with greater ESG news coverage, particularly when their stock is held by more ESG-oriented investors. Nonetheless, we do not find significant differences in the effect of ESG news coverage on ERC between firms with high and low ESG ownership. The latter findings are consistent with the notion that in firms with substantial ESG ownership, ESG investors and traditional investors respond to different signals, resulting in higher trading volume but a muted price impact (Goldstein et al. 2024).

This paper makes the following contributions. First, this study adds to the literature on how and when media coverage can enhance or undermine market efficiency (e.g., Blankespoor et al. 2018; Bushee et al. 2010; Bonsall et al. 2020; Guest 2021). Specifically, our evidence sheds light on how the incentive to inject public ESG attention and sentiment into corporate (i.e., firm-specific) news reporting interferes with media's role as a financial information intermediary. Consistent with the notion that the expansion of ESG news coverage crowds out earnings coverage and diverts investor attention, thereby leading to slower incorporation of earnings news, we document a lower ERC for firms with greater ESG news coverage during the earnings announcement window.

Second, by examining the interplay between the level of ownership by ESG-oriented investors, the increase in ESG news coverage, and information incorporation during earnings announcements, this study contributes to the literature on heterogeneous investor preferences (e.g., Barron, Richard, and Douglas 2018; Booker, Curtis, and Richardson 2023), especially in the context of ESG investing (e.g., Goldstein et al. 2024). Our evidence reveals that the positive association between ESG news coverage and abnormal trading volume during the earnings announcement window is more pronounced in firms with greater ESG fund ownership, which is consistent with investor disagreement leading to increased trading.

Finally, by providing novel evidence that the media shift their focus from covering earnings news to covering corporate ESG issues in periods of heightened public ESG attention and sentiment, we extend the literature on how the media’s incentive to cater to reader demand affects their reporting choices (e.g., Gentzkow and Shapiro 2006; Bonsall et al. 2020). $ ^{2} $ Our findings also add to the literature that examines the value-relevance of ESG news and uses ESG news sentiment as measures of firms’ ESG performance (e.g., Kruger 2015; Li, Watts, Zhu 2024; Moss, Naughton, Wang, Yeung 2025; Serafeim and Yoon 2022). Our evidence informs about the media’s incentives and biases with regards to ESG news reporting, i.e., we argue and find that the coverage and sentiment of corporate ESG (and earnings) news may reflect public ESG attention and sentiment as well as the firms’ actual ESG (and earnings) performance. These results imply that investors, analysts, and other market participants should use media coverage cautiously, especially during times of intense public ESG sentiment.

## 2. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

### 2.1 Public Sentiment and Corporate ESG News Coverage

Media outlets have strong incentives to produce content that resonates with their readers' views and interests (Gentzkow and Shapiro 2006; 2010; Mullainathan and Shleifer 2005). Prior studies document that financial media outlets expand reporting of earnings news to meet reader demand in times with high market uncertainty (Bonsall IV et al. 2020). Similarly, intense public ESG sentiment boosts readers' demand for ESG information and increase the news value of ESG issues, which, in turn, incentivize news outlets to expand coverage of ESG news. $ ^{3} $ To the extent that corporations are seen as relevant or responsible in the specific ESG issue being discussed, the increase in general ESG news articles (e.g., climate change, natural disaster, diversity issues, etc.) should lead to more coverage of corporate ESG news.

Moreover, intense public ESG sentiment can reinforce reader preferences of a particular view of corporate social responsibility, which motivates more media slant to meet with readers' taste (i.e., the “echo chamber effect”). In other words, news outlets may magnify ESG news sentiment to attract reader attention, particularly during heightened public scrutiny of ESG issues. On the other hand, media slant may also arise due to the preferences of the journalists (Baron 2006), editors, or owners (Besley and Andrea Prat, 2004; Djankov et al. 2003). Consistent with the supply side of media bias, studies have shown that the reporting of corporate ESG news depends heavily on the preferences of the journalists, editors, and media owners. For example, prior studies document differences in the number of stories and slanted language about climate change between liberal and conservative outlets (Chinn et al. 2020; Mastrorocco et al. 2023; Djourelova, Durante, Motte, and Patacchin 2023). In particular, Djourelova et al. (2023) find that after a natural disaster,

the liberal outlets increase the number of climate change-related news articles significantly and suggest a causal link between the disaster and climate change, whereas the conservative outlets do not increase their coverage of climate change and tend to negate the causal link. During periods of intensified public focus on ESG issues, the journalists, editors, or owners of media may feel more need to embed personal or organizational viewpoints in their reporting, which often results in amplifying the tone of corporate ESG news.

Our first set of hypotheses are stated below in alternative form:

H1a: In times with heightened public ESG sentiment, financial media outlets will expand coverage of corporate ESG news.

H1b: In times with heightened public ESG sentiment, corporate ESG news sentiment may be magnified.

### 2.2 Corporate ESG News Coverage and Earnings News Coverage

The impact of heightened public ESG sentiment on media coverage of ESG news may also spill over into their financial news coverage. Bonsall IV et al. (2020) find that media outlets substitute non-earnings news with earnings news to meet increased investor demand for financial information during periods with heightened market uncertainty. Similarly, to the extent that media outlets face resource- and space-constraints, expanding ESG news coverage during times with heightened public ESG sentiment may also crowd out coverage of financial news. $ ^{4} $

Moreover, with stark partisan differences emerging across many ESG issues, the debate surrounding these topics has become increasingly polarized. The divergence between ESG

perspectives of media outlets and those of firms can signal broader political incongruence. The findings from Rees and Twedt (2022) suggest that media coverage of firms' earnings announcement can be negatively biased when the political stance of the firm is incongruent with that of the media outlet. Similarly, media perceptions of firms' ESG practices may shape their expectations about the firms' future financial performance. As a result, we predict that sentiment expressed in earnings news will intensify in the same direction as the firm's ESG news sentiment. We state our second set of hypotheses below in alternative form:

H2a: Expanded reporting of corporate ESG news may crowd out the reporting of earnings news.

H2b: Corporate earnings news sentiment may reflect sentiment in ESG news.

### 2.3 Capital Market Consequences of Increased Corporate ESG News Coverage

Media plays the role as important information intermediary as they originate and disseminate information. For example, Bushee et al. (2010) find that greater media coverage during earnings announcement periods reduces information asymmetry and results in lower spreads and greater depth. Blankespoor et al. (2018) examine the effects of media synthesis and dissemination using the staggered implementation of robo-journalism at the Associated Press, and find that automated articles improve trading volume and liquidity. Guest (2021) examines the Wall Street Journal (WSJ) articles that have substantive editorial content and documents a positive relation between news articles covering earnings announcements and the earnings response coefficient and trading volume. The reduction in financial news coverage due to the expansion of ESG news coverage in times with heightened public ESG sentiment can exacerbate information asymmetry and impede market reaction.

Moreover, the investor distraction hypothesis also suggests that extraneous news may divert investors' attention, leading them to underreact to important financial news (Hirshleifer, Lim, Teoh 2009). The exposure to intense public ESG sentiment and expanded coverage of corporate ESG news may distract investors from incorporating signals embedded in earnings news, leading to weaker immediate price and volume reaction to earnings surprises.

In addition, recent studies provide evidence on the preference heterogeneity among socially responsible investors and traditional investors (e.g., Cao et al. 2023; Glebkin and Kuong 2023; Goldstein et al. 2024). In particular, consistent with the notion that socially responsible investors may overweight ESG-related signals and underweight financial signals, Cao et al. (2023) document larger abnormal returns associated with quantitative mispricing signals among stocks with larger responsible institutional ownership. Glebkin and Kuong (2023) and Goldstein et al. (2024) provide theoretical models showing that stocks with high responsible ownership tend to have lower price informativeness to future financial outcomes. Following these studies, we predict weaker market reactions to earnings announcements as media shift their attention to covering more ESG news and less earnings news. We state our third hypothesis below in alternative form:

H3: Firms with more ESG news coverage tend to have weaker price reactions to earnings surprises.

## 3. Sample and Research Design

### 3.1 Sample Construction

We begin with all company-related news articles on RavenPack’s Edge platform during 2007 to 2023. Then, we restrict our sample to full-size articles with a relevance score of 75 and above and country equals United States. Using the RavenPack Edge Taxonomy, we identify ESG

news articles as those with at least one event with TAGS containing "esg". $ ^{5} $ We identify earnings news as those with event GROUP equals "earnings". Aggregating all corporate news coverage to firm-quarter yields a sample with 267,058 observations from 10,489 unique firms (Table 1, Panel A). Next, we exclude observations with missing control variables or having insufficient degrees of freedom due to the inclusion of fixed effects. Our final quarterly corporate news coverage sample contains 146,097 observations from 5,167 unique firms.

To construct the sample for our earnings announcement tests, we start with all earnings announcements for firms in the quarterly news sample (Table 1, Panel B). Then, we drop observations from delisting years, those with missing key variables, and those with insufficient degrees of freedom due to the inclusion of fixed effects. Our final sample for earnings announcement analyses contains 142,136 firm-quarter observations from 4,934 unique firms.

Table 2, Panel A provides the summary statistics for the quarterly news coverage sample. On average, 11.11% of all articles in a quarter are ESG news, with 9.6% focusing on social issues, 1.94% on governance, and only 0.127% on environmental issues. $ ^{6} $ The average sentiment of ESG news is close to zero, suggesting neural sentiment. Similarly, firm-specific ESG news coverage accounts for 14.59% of total corporate news coverage, with 9.91% focusing on social issues, 4.92% focusing on governance issues, and 0.037% focusing on environmental issues. In contrast, significantly more attention is paid to earnings news, which accounts for 23.21% of total corporate news coverage. Corporate ESG news sentiment tends to be mildly positive, similar to the earnings news sentiment.

Table 2, Panel C presents the correlation table. Consistent with our hypothesis, we observe a positive and significant correlation between  $ ESG\_Coverage\% $ (ESG_Sentiment) and  $ ESG\_AttentionIndex $ (ESG_SentimentIndex), suggesting that media outlets expand corporate ESG news coverage in times with heightened public ESG attention, and corporate ESG news sentiment reflects society-wide ESG sentiment. Moreover, consistent with the crowding-out hypothesis, the univariate correlation between  $ Earnings\_Coverage\% $ and  $ ESG\_Coverage\% $ is negative and significant. Finally, the correlation between  $ Earnings\_Sentiment $ and  $ ESG\_Sentiment $ is also positive and significant. Because the correlations do not control for confounding factors such as firm characteristics, we refrain from drawing conclusions based on this univariate analysis alone and continue with the following regression analyses.

### 3.2 Corporate ESG News Coverage in Times with Heighted Public ESG Sentiment

Our first set of empirical tests examine whether media outlets tend to expand ESG news coverage and magnify ESG news sentiment in times with heightened public ESG sentiment. Specifically, following Bonsall IV et al. (2020), we adopt the following regression model:

 $$ ESG_{-}Coverage_{i,q}\%\ =\beta_{1}ESG_{-}AttentionIndex_{q}+\beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}+\gamma_{i}+\epsilon_{i,q} $$ 

 $$ ESG_{-}Sentiment_{i,q}=\beta_{1}ESG_{-}SentimentIndex_{q}+\beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}+\gamma_{i}+\epsilon_{i,q} $$ 

where  $ ESG Coverage\%_{i,q} $ is the proportion of ESG news articles among all news articles about firm i in quarter q, and  $ ESG\_Sentiment_{i,q} $ is the average of the sentiment of ESG news articles written on company i during quarter q. We construct two measures to capture public ESG attention and sentiment. First, to capture public attention towards ESG issues, we construct  $ ESG\_AttentionIndex_{q} $, which equals the proportion of ESG news articles among all news articles

during quarter q. This measure is akin to the public climate sentiment index constructed in Engle et al. (2020). Second, to capture public sentiment towards ESG issues, we construct ESG_SentimentIndex_q, which equals the average ESG news sentiment score.

Following prior literature, we control for the factors that affects media coverage decision of earnings announcement news (e.g., Fang and Peress 2009; Bushee et al. 2010; Drake et al. 2014; Guest 2021), including the natural logarithm of market capitalization (LMktCap), leverage ratio (Leverage), market-to-book ratio (Market-to-Book), past return (Return) and volatility (ReturnVolatility), return on assets (ROA), membership of major index (S&P 500), the natural logarithm of the number of employees (LEmployee), the natural logarithm of the number of common shareholders (LOwn), natural logarithm of analyst coverage (LFollow), earnings surprise (SUE), and institutional ownership (InstOwn). To mitigate the concern that both public ESG sentiment and firm-specific ESG news coverage can be affected by macroeconomic factors (e.g., Bushee et al. 2020), we control for the market volatility index (VIX), a recessionary indicator (NBERRecession), the Baker and Wurgler (2006) investor sentiment measure (InvestorSentiment), and the Economic Policy Uncertainty Index developed in Baker, Bloom, and Davis (2016) (EPU). All financial variables are constructed using data from the previous quarter. All firm-level continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% and 99% levels by year-quarter. All continuous variables, except for SUE, are standardized. Finally, we include firm fixed effects ( $ \gamma_{i} $) to control for firm-specific, time-invariant factors, and cluster standard errors at the firm and year-quarter levels to adjust for intra-firm and year-quarter correlations in the error terms.

### 3.3 Corporate ESG News Coverage and Earnings News Coverage

To examine whether the increased attention on corporate ESG news divert media away from covering earnings news, we adopt the following regression models:

 $$ Earnings_{-}Coverage_{i,q}\%\ =\alpha+\beta_{1}ESG_{-}Coverage_{i,q}\%\ +\ \beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}+\gamma_{i}+\epsilon_{i,q} $$ 

 $$ Earnings\_{S}entiment_{i,q}\;=\alpha+\beta_{1}ESG\_{S}entiment_{i,q}+\beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}+\gamma_{i}+\epsilon_{i,q} $$ 

where  $ Earnings\_Coverage_{i,q}\% $ is the proportion of earnings news articles among all news articles about firm i in quarter q, and  $ Earnings\_Sentiment_{i,q} $ is the average sentiment of earnings news articles written on company i during quarter q. All other variables are defined in the same way as in Equation (1a) and (1b). We also adopt the same set of fixed effects and clustering as before. If ESG news coverage crowds out earnings coverage,  $ \beta_{1} $ in Equation (2a) should be negative and significant. On the other hand, if earnings news sentiment reflects ESG news sentiment,  $ \beta_{1} $ should be positive and significant in Equation (2b).

### 3.4 Market Response to Changes in Media Coverage of ESG News

To examine whether the shift in media coverage of corporate ESG news during times with heightened public ESG sentiment impedes reactions to earnings news, we adopt the following model:

 $$ \begin{aligned}AbReturn_{i,q}&[-1,1]\\&=\beta_{1}ESG_{-}Coverage_{i,q}\%+\beta_{2}SUE_{i,q}+\beta_{3}ESG_{-}Coverage_{i,q}\%\times SUE_{i,q}\\&\quad+\beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}+\beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}\times SUE_{i,q}+\gamma_{q}+\gamma_{q}\times SUE_{i,q}+\epsilon_{i,q}\quad(3)\end{aligned} $$ 

where  $ AbReturn_{i,q}[-1,1] $ is the market-adjusted buy-and-hold returns for firm i during  $ t-1 $ to  $ t+1 $ of the earnings announcement window in quarter q in percentage terms.  $ SUE_{i,q} $ is the contemporaneous earnings surprise for firm i. All other variables are defined in the same way as in Equation (1a) and (1b). Since we include year-quarter fixed effects, the macroeconomic control variables are dropped. To allow the ERC to vary by firm characteristics and by time, we interact SUE with the controls and year-quarter fixed effects. The earnings response coefficient (ERC),  $ \beta_{2} $,

captures the relationship between earnings surprises and stock returns. The coefficient,  $ \beta_{3} $, compares the changes in ERC of the earnings announcing firms that have more ESG news coverage to those with less ESG news coverage. A negative  $ \beta_{3} $ coefficient would suggest that increasing ESG news coverage impedes market reaction to earnings news.

### 4.1 Corporate ESG News Coverage in Times with Heighted Public ESG Sentiment

The two panels of Table 3 present findings from Equation (1a) and (1b), respectively. In column (1), we only include the variable of interest (ESG_AttentionIndex or ESG_SentimentIndex) in the regression. In columns (2) to (4), we gradually add firm-level controls, measures of macroeconomic conditions, and firm fixed effects. Overall, the findings corroborate the media's incentive to cater to the demand and tastes of the readers (Gentzkow and Shapiro 2006; Mullainathan and Shleifer 2005).

### 4.2 Corporate ESG News Coverage and Earnings News Coverage

Table 4 presents findings from Equation (2a). Consistent with the crowding-out hypothesis, our evidence suggests that media outlets shift attention away from earnings news coverage as they cover more corporate ESG news. This crowding-out effect is robust when we examine corporate E (environmental)-, S (social)-, and G (governance)-related news separately, as shown in Panel B of Table 4. However, as shown in Table 5, conditional on ESG news coverage, the sentiment expressed in ESG news is reflected in earnings news sentiment. Furthermore, we present evidence that the sentiment of ESG news moderates the relationship between earnings surprise and the

sentiment of earnings-related articles. In Table 6 column (1), the negative coefficient on  $ SUE \times LowESGSentiment $ suggests that, when earnings news is positive, the sentiment of earnings articles is less positive when the ESG news sentiment is lower. Similarly, column (2) shows that when earnings news is negative, higher ESG news sentiment is associated with media reporting the earnings news more positively. Overall, the findings in this section provide preliminary evidence on how media's role as a key financial intermediary may be influenced by the growing society-wide focus on ESG. In particular, the reporting of earnings news decreases and becomes increasingly impacted by the coverage of ESG news, which may affect how earnings news is perceived and interpreted by investors.

### 4.3 Corporate ESG News Coverage and Earnings Responses

Table 7 Panel A reports our findings in Equation (3). Column (1) shows the result without control variables or fixed effects. In columns (2) to (5), we gradually include additional control variables and fixed effects. The coefficient of interest,  $ \beta_{3} $, is negative and significant across all columns, suggesting a smaller ERC for firms with higher media coverage of ESG news. In terms of economic magnitude, focusing on column (3), a one standard deviation increase in ESG_Coverage% is associated with 8.67% decrease in ERC. $ ^{7} $ This finding is robust when we separately examine the results by E, S, and G news coverage.

There are three potential explanations for the smaller ERC. First, availability of earnings-related information may decline as the media expands coverage of ESG news at the expense of earnings news coverage (Table 4), which results in higher information asymmetry and slower incorporation of earnings news. Moreover, consistent with the limited attention theory (Hirshleifer,

Lim, Teoh 2009), investors may be distracted by the growing focus on ESG-related issues and underreact to the earnings news. Finally, a smaller ERC can also result from investors preference heterogeneity. In particular, consistent with the notion that socially responsible investors may overweight ESG-related signals and underweight financial signals (e.g., Cao et al. 2023; Goldstein et al. 2024), stocks with high responsible ownership may experience a weaker association between stock returns and financial news for firms.

Although the crowding-out hypothesis, the limited attention hypothesis, and the investor preference heterogeneity hypothesis yield the same prediction about ERC, they lead to distinct predictions regarding trading volume. Under the crowding-out hypothesis and the investor distraction hypothesis, trading volume would be lower as investors' information set shrinks or their attention being diverted away. Under the preference heterogeneity hypothesis, trading volume would be higher due to investor disagreement (e.g., Barron et al. 2018; Booker et al. 2023; Goldstein et al. 2024). To assess which explanation is more dominant, we next turn to examining the relation between trading volume and ESG news coverage during earnings announcement windows.

### 4.4 Corporate ESG News Coverage and Trading Volume

To test the effects of more ESG news coverage on abnormal trading volume, we adopt the following model:

 $$ AbVolume_{i,q}\left[-1,1\right]=\beta_{1}ESG_{-}Coverage_{i,q}\%+\beta_{2}SUE_{i,q}+\beta^{\prime}Z_{i,q}+\gamma_{q}+\epsilon_{i,q} $$ 

 $ AbVolume_{i,q}[-1,1] $ is the difference between average daily share turnover for firm i during  $ t-1 $ to  $ t+1 $ of the event window and during 60 prior trading days, multiplied by 10,000. All other variables are defined in the same way as Equation (3). A negative (positive)  $ \beta_{1} $ would suggest that increasing ESG news coverage reduces (increases) market reaction to earnings news.

Table 8 Panel A reports our findings in Equation (4). Column (1) shows the result without control variables other than SUE, Column (2) adds control variables, and Column (3) includes the year-quarter fixed effect. In all three columns, the coefficient on  $ ESG\_Coverage\% $ is positive and significant, suggesting that firms with higher ESG news coverage also have larger trading volume during the earnings announcement window. Specifically, one standard deviation increase in the coverage of ESG news is associated with about 2% higher abnormal trading volume (Column 3). $ ^{8} $

In Panel B of Table 8, we report the results with E, S, and G news respectively. Interestingly, we find that abnormal trading volume the earnings announcement window is significantly larger for firms with more S news coverage but significantly smaller for firms with more G coverage. One potential explanation of these findings is that investor disagreement regarding the nature of S-related news (e.g. layoffs, workforce salary increases) than G-related news (e.g., product development, conference participation/organization).

### 4.5 ESG Ownership and Market Reactions

A key premise of significant investor disagreement and opposing trading behavior is the heterogeneity in investor preferences, which, in our setting, is more pronounced in firms with substantial ESG ownership (Goldstein et al. 2024). Thus, in this subsection, we examine whether the market reaction results documented in section 4.3 and 4.4 vary based on the firm's ESG ownership levels. We identify ESG funds using Morningstar's ESG Risk Ratings for Funds. $ ^{9} $ Specifically, a fund is considered an ESG fund if the fund receives a rating of five “globes”, i.e., the highest sustainability level (Hartzmark and Sussman 2019). $ ^{10} $ Focusing on U.S. domestic

equity mutual funds, we identify 257 unique ESG funds. Using mutual funds’ holdings from CRSP’s Mutual Fund Database, we calculate ESG ownership as the number of a company’s shares owned by ESG funds divided by the total number of shares outstanding.

We classify sample firms as having high ESG ownership (HighESGOwn) if their ESG ownership exceeds the median level of a given year. For the earnings response analysis, we modify equation (3) by interacting HighESGOwn with ESG_Coverage% and SUE, as well as their interaction. Column (1) of Table 9 presents the results. The coefficient on the triple interaction term, ESG_Coverage% x SUE x HighESGOwn, is negative but insignificant, indicating that the ESG news coverage's impact on the earnings response coefficient does not differ significantly between the firms with higher ESG ownership and those with lower ESG ownership.

For the trading volume analysis, we modify equation (4) by interacting HighESGOwn with ESG_Coverage%. As Column (2) of Table 9 shows, the coefficient on ESG_Coverage% x HighESGOwn is also positive and significant, indicating that the positive association between ESG news coverage and abnormal trading volume is more pronounced in the firms with higher ESG ownership (i.e., more diverse investor base). The findings of this subsection corroborate the prediction that in firms with substantial ESG ownership, having more ESG information exacerbate investor disagreement, leading to a larger trading volume (Goldstein et al. 2024).

## 5. Conclusion

This study provides evidence on how the media responds to heightened public sentiment of ESG issues; specifically, we find expanded coverage of corporate ESG news and firm-specific ESG news sentiment that largely aligns with the public sentiment. More importantly, we provide novel evidence on the capital market consequences due to shifting media attention towards ESG

news coverage. Our findings suggest that greater ESG news coverage crowds out coverage of earnings news. Moreover, earnings news sentiment is reflective of ESG news sentiment, even when the ESG news sentiment and the underlying earnings are inconsistent. These changes in media reporting impede price discovery during the earnings announcement window but are associated with higher abnormal trading volume. While the weaker ERC findings may result from decreased availability of earnings news or investor distraction/inattention, the trading volume findings suggest that investor disagreement, potentially stemming from divergent views of the value-relevance of ESG issues, may help explain the heightened trading. Additional analyses show that the positive relation between ESG news coverage and abnormal trading volume is more pronounced in firms with higher ESG ownership, which further corroborates the role of heterogeneity in ESG preferences.

Our paper has implications for both academics and practitioners seeking to understand the role of the media in contributing to efficient markets and the role of ESG information vis-à-vis financial information about firm fundamentals (e.g., earnings). Overall, our findings suggest that financial media outlets respond to and amplify ESG sentiment in a way that undermines their role in facilitating the incorporation of key financial news into market prices.
