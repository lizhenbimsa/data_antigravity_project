## ABSTRACT

To what extent does the narrative context surrounding the numbers in financial statements alter the informativeness of these numbers, that is, contextualize them? Answering this question empirically presents a methodological challenge. Leveraging recent advances in deep learning, we propose a method to uncover the value of contextual information learned from the (deep) interactions between numeric and narrative disclosures. We show that the contextualization of accounting numbers makes them substantially more informative in shaping beliefs about a firm's future, especially when numeric data are less reliable. In fact, the informational value of interactions dominates the direct informational value of the narrative context. We corroborate this finding by showing that stock markets and financial analysts incorporate the interactions between narrative and numeric information when making forecasts. We also demonstrate the value of our approach by identifying rich

firm-year-specific heterogeneity in earnings persistence. We discuss a number of avenues for future research.

## 1. Introduction

Firms disclose information to stakeholders using different modes of communication, including numbers, text, images, voice, video, and nonverbal cues. With recent advancements in computing, multimodal data are becoming increasingly important in decision making (e.g., Zhu [2019]). However, the extent to which multimodal disclosures complement each other is yet to be understood empirically. We study the economic importance of the complementarities (interactions) between numeric and narrative disclosures. We focus on the narrative disclosures because they “provide the context within which financial information should be analyzed.” $ ^{1} $ Specifically, the Security and Exchange Commission explicitly requires an MD&A discussion of nonquantitative factors that facilitate the interpretation of the reported numbers, thus, allowing investors to form more accurate expectations of future cash flows. $ ^{2} $ In this study, we introduce a deep learning-based method to measure the contextuality of accounting numbers, that is, the degree of their dependence on the context stemming from the interactions between numeric and textual information.

As an illustration, imagine that two distinct companies announce identical numeric performance. Even though the reported numbers are the same, their interpretation may vary widely depending on the narrative commentary pertaining to business strategy, competition, product demand, operating frictions, future plans, and so forth, collectively referred to as (narrative) context. Although the direct implication is that numeric and textual information interact with each other, such interactions are often complex and highly nonlinear, making them obscure empirically. How economically important are these complementarities in shaping investors' beliefs about a company's future, and when does the context matter most?

Answers to these questions are important to know. Accounting standard setters are actively considering ways to enhance the disclosures of contextual information to complement numeric information. For example, the SEC's comment letters frequently identify inadequate discussions of drivers of economic performance by the companies (E&Y [2018]). Additionally,

IASB is currently revising the requirements for Management Commentary Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A analog) because companies sometimes “fail to focus on matters important to the entity’s prospects,” among other things (IASB [2021]). $ ^{3} $ Evaluating whether a new disclosure standard improves the informativeness of contextual information presents a significant challenge. To address this challenge, one needs to quantify the value added by narrative disclosures, which involves measuring nonlinear interactions between unstructured, highly multidimensional textual data and numeric information. The approach we introduce allows us to readily tackle this issue.

A number of prior studies show that textual information, and MD&A in particular, is incrementally informative about a firm's future prospects (e.g., Tasker [1998], Hutton, Miller, and Skinner [2003], Baginski, Hassell, and Kimbrough [2004], Li [2010], Muslu et al. [2015], Mayew, Sethuraman, and Venkatachalam [2015], Bochkay and Levine [2019]). However, such incremental information content is moderate at best when it comes to explaining a firm's future performance (we refer to these results as direct informational value). Besides the direct informational value, narrative disclosures can also be valuable because they contextualize (interact with) the numbers. The economic importance of contextualization in shaping expectations about future cash flows can range from trivial to large. Several prior studies have shown that textual attributes, for example, readability, exhibit statistically significant interactions with numeric disclosures (Li [2008], Li, Lundholm, and Minnis [2013]). However, such (shallow) interactions combined with relatively coarse ways to encode textual information translate into rather trivial increments to the explanatory power, casting a negative light on the importance of narrative context for numeric interpretations. $ ^{4} $

In quantifying the role of context in the interpretation of numeric data, we are facing two primary challenges. First, we need to systematically encode granular semantic information (features) contained in textual disclosures. Second, we require a model of the interpretation process within which the narrative and numeric information can interact in rich and highly nonlinear ways. $ ^{5} $ We turn to deep learning and language modeling to tackle these challenges. To address the first challenge, we use Bidirectional

Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)—a large language model—to encode narrative information that surrounds earnings disclosure in a high-dimensional space. To tackle the second challenge, we model the interpretation process by means of an artificial neural network (ANN). ANN is a mathematical function that relies on a large network of parameterized elementary units, “neurons,” to model complex relationships. A neuron is activated under certain conditions and carries a small quantity of information. The power of ANN comes from its ability to learn deep interactions between neurons to generate useful signals. Analogous interactions occur during cognitive information processing by a human brain, which activates numerous neurons and lets them interact in complex ways along synapses. $ ^{6} $

The key step involved in most investors' decisions is the formation of beliefs about a firm's future prospects. We design an ANN that learns to contextualize accounting numbers (earnings and cash flows) depending on their narrative discussion when making inferences about firms' future prospects. To quantify contextuality or the importance of context in the interpretation of numeric data, our primary methodological innovation is to allow versus restrict the communication channels through which narrative information (textual neurons) affects the activation of neurons stemming from numeric input and vice versa. In other words, we allow (or restrict) textual input to shape the interpretation of accounting numbers. The predictive power of interactions between the contextual and numeric neurons determines the value of narrative disclosures in the interpretation of accounting numbers, referred to as contextuality.

To extract the narrative context related to disclosed earnings, we create a comprehensive data set that contains the vast majority of machine-readable MD&As filed between 1995 and 2021. For each MD&A, we extract sentences that contain earnings-related terms, such as net income, profit, and earnings. $ ^{7} $ We then extract the adjacent sentences on each side to capture the earnings context more fully. $ ^{8} $ We pool all extracted sentences in the order they appear in the document and pass them through BERT to encode them in the form of a textual vector. $ ^{9} $

We begin by analyzing two benchmark models. The numeric input model is an ANN that uses the accounting variables only: current earnings and operating cash flows (scaled by assets), and four annual lags for each variable. The textual input model is an ANN that uses BERT vectors that encode the narrative context as inputs. $ ^{10} $ The numeric and textual input models share a similar architecture. We train the models to infer changes in future earnings, cash flows, or stock price movements by leveraging numeric or narrative information (or both). In particular, we use year t earnings and cash flows (and their lagged values) and combine them with the disclosed context to predict  $ t + 1 $ target variables: directional changes in future earnings, cash flows, or stock prices (returns). $ ^{11} $ We evaluate the model out of sample.

Our benchmark models indicate that accounting information, when used alone, predicts the directional earnings changes with an accuracy of about 56.10%. Textual information, on its own, achieves a slightly lower average accuracy of 55.19%. To evaluate the incremental information added by the narrative context, we then combine the textual and numeric inputs into a single partially connected model. In this model, the contextual information is not involved in the processing of the numeric input (and vice versa), and hence it does not affect the interpretation of numeric information. The two types of inputs propagate via their own layers of neurons (like in the benchmark models) and map directly into the predicted variable. When we take this model to the data, we find that the narrative context is incrementally informative and, in line with prior studies, its direct informational value is moderate. In particular, the joint accuracy of this model in predicting directional earning changes increases to 58.57% or a 2.48 percentage point increase relative to the numeric input model. Analogous results hold for other target variables.

In contrast, our fully connected model allows for interactions through which text contextualizes the numeric input. We find that the contextualization of numbers significantly improves the inferences about future prospects, both statistically and economically. In the case of directional earnings changes, the model achieves 64.86% accuracy, which in relative terms constitutes an 11% (16%) increase compared to the accuracy of 58.57% (56.10%) achieved by the partially connected (numeric input models).

These differences are equally stark when we predict the direction of changes in operating cash flows or stock returns. For example, in the

case of 60-day returns, augmenting accounting variables with contextual information yields about 2.5 percentage point improvement in accuracy, whereas allowing for contextual interactions yields a further 6 percentage point improvement. In all cases, in line with prior studies, narrative information on its own carries useful information. However, the primary value of narrative information comes not from its direct incremental informativeness but from the interactions with the numbers, that is, allowing for contextualization.

Our analysis reveals a positive time trend in numeric disclosure contextuality. The largest increase is observed when explaining annual returns: It increases from around 1 to around 10 percentage points over the two decades. Narrative context thus plays an increasingly important role in the interpretation of accounting numbers. These patterns are consistent with the improvements in the quality of MD&A discussions over time.

Our evidence suggests that interactions stemming from the narrative context help explain how the stock market processes accounting information. To shed additional light on whether human decision-makers incorporate context when making decisions, we examine whether the context helps to explain analysts' forecast revisions triggered by MD&A disclosures. Indeed, we find that the interactions between narrative and numeric disclosures are useful in explaining how human decision-makers interpret accounting information.

We also investigate how the importance of narrative context changes under different economic scenarios. We find that contextual information becomes more crucial when numeric data are less reliable, such as when past performance is not indicative of future results due to high earnings volatility or extreme accruals or when valuation is relatively challenging. Context is also more valuable during periods of macroeconomic uncertainty, such as during the COVID-19 pandemic or the financial crisis of 2008, when companies' textual disclosures are especially useful to interpret reported performance. The results indicate that narrative context adds the most value when economic conditions make numeric data harder to interpret.

Finally, to further demonstrate the practical relevance of our findings, we exploit the value of contextual interactions in quantifying earnings persistence—the primary indicator of earnings quality (e.g., Dichev et al. [2013]). We construct a firm-year measure of earnings persistence based on the narrative discussion accompanying earnings disclosures by following the nonparametric approach in Farrell, Liang, and Misra [2020,2021]. Specifically, we regress earnings on its lagged values and model the slope coefficient as a deep neural network that condenses contextual information into a single number. In line with our prior findings, earnings context identifies substantial heterogeneity in earnings persistence, which helps to make more accurate forecasts of future earnings dynamics. An alternative, context-free approach to measuring firm-level persistence relies on rolling window regressions and is dominated by our context-based measures.

Our study contributes to the literature in four ways. First, we introduce a new approach to quantify contextuality of textual disclosures, that is, the change in the predictive value of the numeric disclosures stemming from their interactions with accompanying narrative context. Interactions between textual and numeric information are fundamental to accounting because numbers in financial statements should not be interpreted in isolation and are enriched by management commentary. We offer a novel way of measuring these complementarities by exploiting modern technologies that resemble human information processing. Our approach allows one to study economic circumstances where contextual information is more relevant and also enables the comparisons of the usefulness of context across different disclosure regimes (e.g., one-fits-all requirements vs. discretionary contextual disclosure). The value of this approach extends beyond textual disclosure, however, and can be readily extended to quantify complementarities (complex interactions) among other modes of communication (see section 7 for a discussion).

Second, we quantify the collective value of interactions between numeric disclosures and their narrative context. Prior studies document that MD&A are incrementally informative to numeric data (Bryan [1997], Li [2010], Mayew, Sethuraman, and Venkatachalam [2015], Frankel, Jennings, and Lee [2016], Bochkay and Levine [2019]), and that textual attributes interact with accounting numbers (Li [2008], Li, Lundholm, and Minnis [2013]). However, prior evidence is consistent with economically insignificant improvements in explanatory (predictive) power derived from the interactions of textual attributes and numeric information. In contrast, our analysis suggests that the interactions between the two modes of disclosure are economically large and vary depending on a firm's economic circumstances; they are also reflected in investors' (and analysts') decisions. Furthermore, the value of these interactions consistently and substantially exceeds the direct (noninteracted) informational value of the narrative information. These results enhance our understanding of the role of text in informing decision making by showing that the primary source of value in contextual disclosures lies in its interactions with numeric data. $ ^{12} $

Third, we contribute to the growing literature in accounting and finance on textual analysis of corporate communications. Prior studies mainly explore unidimensional features, such as sentiment (Loughran and McDonald [2011]), readability (Li [2008], Bonsall et al. [2017]), complexity (Loughran and McDonald [2024]), and similarity (Brown and Tucker [2011], Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen [2020]). In their recent review, Bochkay et al. [2023] call for applications of deep learning techniques to

further advance textual analysis in accounting. $ ^{13} $ In particular, they advocate for the use of deep learning when unknown multidimensional features need to be learned from that data (textual feature engineering), and when complex nonlinearities are essential. Our study fits perfectly with this description and demonstrates how deep learning can be useful in answering questions not reachable previously (i.e., measuring contextuality and context-based persistence).

Last but not least, we introduce a context-based measure of earnings persistence. Persistence is of considerable interest to accounting researchers, being a key indicator of earnings quality. Unlike the existing measure of persistence, our measure is firm-year specific. It dominates the firm-level context-free measure persistence out of sample. Our measure expands a set of research questions that one can explore. We are also the first study in the accounting literature to apply the methodology in Farrell, Liang, and Misra [2020, 2021] to learn firm-level parameter heterogeneity. This approach can be used in many other settings to model cross-sectional heterogeneity among firms.

## 2. Background and Hypothesis

When investors interpret accounting data to make decisions, this process typically involves a critical step of assessing future expected cash flows. Narrative disclosures can facilitate this process by (1) directly providing incremental information about future cash flows beyond the numeric disclosures and (2) helping investors to interpret numeric information when assessing its implications for future cash flows. If text indeed alters the interpretation of numeric data, it should generate economically significant interactions between textual and numeric information and, in turn, alter investors' decisions and beliefs. The degree to which these interactions contextualize the meaning of numeric disclosures, however, still remains to be understood empirically.

Management Analysis and Discussion is an ideal setting to study the interactions of textual and numeric disclosures. MD&A provides a narrative explanation relevant to the assessment of firms' operating performance and financial position and its closely intertwined objectives are (1) to enable investors to view the company's reported performance through the eyes of its management by communicating the management's beliefs, views, and thinking; (2) to provide investors with relevant context within which financial numbers are to be interpreted; and (3) to provide information about the quality and variability of a company's earnings and cash flows to facilitate assessments of whether past performance is indicative of future performance (SEC [2008]).

To meet these objectives, management is explicitly required to discuss the results of operations in a way that goes beyond numeric amounts. The focus is on the nonquantitative factors that caused these amounts, including nonfinancial factors. The required disclosures include the discussion of significant economic changes that affect reported income, including changes in business and competitive strategy, unusual or infrequent events, significant changes to sources of revenues and expenses, changes in business segments and launch of new products, known trends and uncertainties that have a material impact on the income statement components, changes in the relation between revenues and expenses (e.g., increases in materials and labor costs), changes in demand for products or output prices, among others. These disclosures provide a rich context aiming to help investors to understand the reported amounts.

A number of prior studies examine whether MD&A disclosures contain useful information. Bryan [1997] manually codes the sentiment in MD&A disclosures and shows that it is associated with (some but not others) future performance measures while controlling for financial statement ratios. Li [2010] uses a naive Bayes classifier to measure MD&A tone on a large scale and shows that it is incrementally informative about future performance. Mayew, Sethuraman, and Venkatachalam [2015] show that the tone of MD&A contains incremental information about a firm's ability to continue as a going concern. More recently, two studies used machine learning (ML) techniques to detect words (or word pairs) in MD&A that are informative. Frankel, Jennings, and Lee [2016] use the support vector machine technique to extract words that explain accruals and predict cash flows. Bochkay and Levine [2019] use ridge regressions to identify MD&A words that are useful in predicting future earnings. $ ^{14} $

There are two important takeaways from the above studies. First, it is clear that MD&A contains incremental information useful in predicting firms' performance. Second, the value added by the incorporation of textual information is small or, at best, moderate. For example, the ML algorithm in Bochkay and Levine [2019] yields a less than 2% improvement in the accuracy of earnings prediction due to the incorporation of textual disclosures.

The notion that textual characteristics interact with numeric disclosures is not new. Li [2008] is a seminal study that shows that textual readability (Fog index) interacts with current earnings when predicting future earnings. Similarly, Li, Lundholm, and Minnis [2013] show that earnings interact with the text-based measure of “competition.” These unidimensional attributes, however, have a very limited ability to explain meaningful variation in future earnings or other outcome variables. This happens because of

their inability to capture the disclosures' semantic meaning, which is inherently multidimensional. For example, textual readability can hardly inform the reader about the disclosure content. Furthermore, the prior methodology does not allow for the deep nonlinearities and interactions present in the textual data to be taken advantage of Bengio, Ducharme, and Vincent [2000].

Importantly, the above studies do not quantify the collective economic importance (predictive power) of the interactions between textual and numeric disclosures in explaining future cash flows and earnings. On the one hand, potential interactions between numeric and textual inputs can shed substantial light on the value of textual information. The presence of such interactions is expected based on the notion that narrative disclosure provides context to qualify the interpretation of reported numbers. On the other hand, to date, we do not have direct empirical evidence on the collective explanatory power of textual-numeric interactions. In fact, accounting regulators have expressed repeated concerns about the lack of informative commentary on management discussions of reported performance (E&Y [2018], IASB [2021]). Further, the existing empirical evidence appears to be at odds with this notion, given that the interactions between textual attributes and numeric characteristics, although statistically significant, explain an economically trivial portion of the variance in future outcomes variables, such as earnings (see the Online Appendix for further discussion).

## 3. Methodology and Research Design

In this section, we start by discussing how we quantify the context surrounding the disclosure of earnings. We then discuss the model of the interpretation process that allows text to contextualize numeric information.

### 3.1 ENCODING NARRATIVE INFORMATION

Over the past decades, textual analysis has rapidly evolved from keyword searches to large language models, such as BERT and GPT. An early approach to encoding information in a given text (e.g., paragraph) relies on dictionaries and word counts (e.g., Loughran and McDonald [2011], Li, Lundholm, and Minnis [2013]) and mainly involves unidimensional properties, such as sentiment or uncertainty. Conventional ways to capture multidimensional information in a given text rely on a bag-of-words representation, which constitutes a vector of word frequencies. Similarly, tf-idf representation relies on word frequencies but adjusts them for how common the words are across the corpus. These methods are useful when measuring the similarity across documents (Brown and Tucker [2011], Hoberg and Maksimovic [2015]) or classifying documents into categories using naive Bayes classifier (Li [2010]).

Although the frequency-based approaches offer simplicity, they have a limited ability to capture and encode information contained in a given text. One reason for this is that they treat all the words as independent from

each other to overcome the “curse of dimensionality” (Li [2010]). As a result, they do not account for how words are used in a sentence and how different words are related to each other. To appreciate the loss of information, imagine the task of reconstructing a text based on its bag-of-word frequencies—a virtually impossible task.

A critical development in natural language processing in the last decade is the emergence of machine-based algorithms that “learn” to represent word meaning in the form of a dense vector (e.g., Bochkay et al. [2023]). These include the Word2Vec model (Mikolov et al. [2013]) or the GloVe model, among others. For example, Word2Vec uses an ANN trained on a large corpus to learn word vectors (embeddings). The embeddings exhibit some attractive properties. A prominent example is that “king” – “man” + “woman” is approximately equal to “queen” when the mathematical operations are performed over the corresponding word embeddings (Mikolov et al. [2013]). These models, however, are not sophisticated enough to capture how a word’s meaning changes depending on other words used in the passage of text and hence they do not fully capture the relevant dimensions representing narrative information. $ ^{15} $

BERT—viewed as a breakthrough in Natural Language Processing and conceptually similar to the GPT model used by ChatGPT (both rely on transformer architecture)—is different from preceding models in that it learns a word's semantic meaning based on the context in which the word is used (Devlin et al. [2019]). Unlike prior language models, BERT uses a self-attention mechanism that encodes words' meaning depending on where and how these words are used (Devlin et al. [2019]). BERT is pretrained on a large corpus of English Wikipedia texts and can process relatively long text passages of up to 512 tokens. The model outputs word vectors that vary depending on the presence of other words in the sentence. Furthermore, BERT encodes the composite meaning of the entire input sequence captured by the hidden state (vector) associated with the classification (CLS) token. The CLS token is inserted at the beginning of each input sequence (sentence or sentences). Although not directly interpretable, it encodes the information in the entire sequence.

BERT is used in a large number of applications, including machine translation, text classification, and text generation. In the accounting literature, BERT has recently been used in Siano and Wysocki [2021] and Huang, Wang, and Yang [2023] for sentiment analysis and Lee and Zhong [2022] to perform topic classification.

The ability of BERT to encode the multidimensional information conveyed in sentences discussing operating performance makes this model particularly well-suited for our study. $ ^{16} $ In contrast to the above studies (and in

line with our objective), we extract the entire 768-dimensional vector that summarizes information in a given document (the classification token's last hidden state vector). $ ^{17} $

### 3.2 MODELING CONTEXTUALIZED INTERPRETATION OF NUMERIC INFORMATION

Our maintained assumption is that the narrative discussion of a firm's reported performance contains bits of qualitative information that are often immaterial on their own, but that can jointly form a "mosaic." In line with this idea, our design allows bits of contextual information to interact with the reported numbers in ways that uncover useful information. ANNs are ideally suited for this task as they enable deep interactions of high-dimensional textual information with numeric information in complex and highly nonlinear ways (Bengio, Ducharme, and Vincent [2000]).

To capture contextuality, or the importance of narrative context for the interpretation of numeric disclosures, we enable versus restrict the ability of the textual input to influence the activation of neurons stemming from the numeric input and vice versa. This is illustrated in figure 1. In particular, information in ANN's input layer propagates toward the target variable via several hidden layers each of which is represented by multiple information units (neurons). A neuron processes the information from the prior layer and is activated under certain conditions. In this case, it passes the signal forward to the next layer. This process is a simplified analogy of how the information is processed in a human brain, which relies on multiple layers of biological neurons connected to each other via a neural network. Each individual neuron behaves in a simplistic way, but when combined, neurons are capable of performing highly complex tasks.

We design the model to enable and restrict the ability of narrative context to contextualize (affect the activation of neurons stemming from) the numeric input, thus, making the textual information directly responsible for the interpretation of numeric information.

### 4.1 MODEL DESIGN

We model the interpretation process as a complex nonlinear function that learns to contextualize the accounting numbers depending on the narrative disclosures when forming an expectation of a firm’s future prospects:

 $$ \mathbf{E}_{t}\big[T_{t+1}|A_{t},C_{t}\big]=\Lambda_{t}(A_{t},C_{t}), $$ 

where  $ T_{t+1} $ is the target variable—a directional change in future earnings, cash flows, and returns,  $ A_{t} $ is an accounting information vector,  $ C_{t} $ is a context information vector, both available at time t,  $ E_{t} $ is an expectation operator, and  $ \Lambda_{t}(\cdot) $ is the function whose parameters depend on time t.

As discussed previously, context-based interpretation of financial information assumes the presence of highly nonlinear interactions between bits of information summarized by the vectors  $ A_t $ and  $ C_t $. These interactions are a key part of  $ \Lambda_t(\cdot) $ and are modeled via deep network connections between neurons stemming from  $ A_t $ and  $ C_t $ inputs. Allowing for these interactions enables the context to influence the interpretation of financial numbers.

We use current operating cash flow, current earnings, and their four annual lags to construct  $ A_t $ (therefore,  $ \dim(A_t) = 10 $). For  $ C_t $, we use the averaged CLS vectors as described previously. Because the original dimension of the context vectors is 768, which is disproportionately larger than that of  $ A_t $, we add an additional layer into the model that learns to reduce the contextual vectors to 10 dimensions (we choose 10 dimensions for symmetry with numeric input).

To test for the role of context in interpreting numeric information, we compare the performance of fully and partially connected ANN models, which will be discussed next. Our fully connected model is visualized in figure 1(a). It has an input layer, two hidden layers, and an output layer: The first and second hidden layers contain 256 and 20 neurons, respectively, and the output layer has two neurons (increase or decrease). We allow full interactions between all neurons and use the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function for neurons populating the two hidden layers. The model outputs the logit probability that the target variable (e.g., earnings) will decrease or increase in the subsequent period. We discuss the prediction of continuous variables in online appendix table OA-2.

To assess the power of the interactions between  $ A_t $ and  $ C_t $, we also construct a partially connected model. This model is illustrated in figure 1(b). In this model, the structure of the layers is the same as in the fully connected model. However, the network features no interactions between  $ A_t $ and  $ C_t $. This means that  $ C_t $ is not involved in the activation of neurons stemming from  $ A_t $, and vice versa. Accordingly, in the first hidden layer, 128 neurons are assigned to  $ A_t $, and the remaining 128 neurons are assigned to  $ C_t $. Similarly, 10 neurons in the second hidden layer are assigned to  $ A_t $ and the remainder to  $ C_t $. The second hidden layer has 20 neurons that connect to the two neurons in the output layer. As the figure illustrates, the only difference between these two models is the presence of connections between neurons stemming from  $ A_t $ and  $ C_t $.

The difference between the accuracy of the fully connected model and that of the sparsely connected model is referred to as contextuality of accounting information. High contextuality implies that narrative context adds greater value when combined with numeric information. To evaluate the accuracy of the model, we calculate the percentage of correctly classified observations. An observation is classified as an “increase” when an increase is more likely than a decrease based on the model’s prediction. We

then compare the true and predicted target variable outcomes to evaluate the accuracy.

### 4.2 TRAINING AND HYPERPARAMETER SELECTION

We train the model to predict time $t$ target, $T_t$, based on $A_{t-1}$, and $C_{t-1}$. We use four preceding years as rolling training windows and use the estimated model parameters to create an out-of-sample forecast $\hat{T}_{t+1|t} = \Lambda_{t-1}(A_t, C_t)$. We repeat this procedure each year with $t$ going from 1999 to 2020 and have four target variables (earnings, cash flow, short-term, 60-day returns, and long-term, 360-day returns).

Training an effective ML model involves an important step of hyperparameter selection (Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville [2016], Géron [2022]). The hyperparameters are selected prior to training to guide the learning process. For each target variable, we perform a grid search to optimize several hyperparameters and maximize the performance of the fully connected model. Specifically, we experiment with three batch sizes (zero, 64, and 128), which determine how much data are passed to the optimizer to compute gradients, three dropout rates (0, 0.2, and 0.4), which control the degree of regularization, and three learning rates ( $ e^{-1} $,  $ e^{-2} $, and  $ e^{-4} $), which control the speed of the optimization process. For each of our four target variables, we estimate 27 different combinations of hyperparameters (108 models in total), we choose the set of hyperparameters that yields the highest averaged accuracy of the fully connected model for each task. For earnings, cash flow, and long-term return predictions, our procedure selects a batch size of 64 observations, 20% dropout rate, and learning rate of  $ e^{-4} $. For short-term return predictions, we select a learning rate of  $ e^{-1} $, a dropout rate of 20%, and a batch size of 64. The details of our hyperparameter selection process are provided in online appendix table OA-1.

For all experiments, we use Adam optimizer and train the model based on self-entropy loss function in combination with early stopping. We randomly assign 20% of observations from the training set as a validation set to assess the improvement of loss function after each epoch. If the loss function does not improve for five consecutive epochs after epoch n, we use the estimated parameters from epoch n. Our training procedure resets and reinitializes the starting values of parameters for each year t.

Because the neural network models rely on stochastic initialization, the same training and test sets may yield slightly different outputs. To improve the generalizability of results and reduce the noise from random initialization (which may lead to finding local minima), for each training set, we repeat the estimation 30 times. We then report the average accuracy.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 USING NARRATIVE CONTEXT TO MAKE INFERENCES ABOUT EARNINGS

We first examine the importance of narrative context to make inferences about future earnings. The model predicts an increase (vs. decrease) in

earnings scaled by total assets (for continuous earnings variable, see section 1.2 of the online appendix). Table 3 presents the models' performance by year and for the entire sample. Column 1 reports the numeric input model (based on accounting information only), whereas column 2 presents the contextual input model (that relies on contextual information only). The models' structure and training schedules are the same. Column 1 shows that the average accuracy of the model based on the accounting input is 56.10%, whereas column 2 reveals a slightly lower accuracy of 55.19% based on the contextual input. $ ^{23} $

The next two columns rely on both accounting and contextual inputs. Columns 3 and 4 report the accuracy of the partially and fully connected models, respectively. Recall that the partially connected model precludes interactions between contextual and numeric inputs, allowing the assessment of incremental information content of textual data. On average, we observe that the partially connected model achieves 58.57% accuracy, which is roughly 2.5 percentage points higher relative to the numeric input model. This is a moderate improvement that aligns, on average, with the improvement in accuracy due to text documented in Bochkay and Levine [2019]. $ ^{24} $ In contrast, the fully connected model achieves an accuracy of 64.86%, which outperforms the partially connected model by 6.29 percentage points (or roughly 10.7% increase). This increase in accuracy is both statistically and economically significant (t-value = 4.66). Furthermore, the fully connected model outperforms the other three models every year in our sample, with contextuality ranging between 4.43% and 8.72%. To put these numbers in perspective, note that relative to the numeric inputs benchmark (56.1% average accuracy), the inclusion of textual input into the model increases its performance by 2.47 percentage points (58.57% accuracy). This improvement nearly quadruples to yield an accuracy of 64.86% when the interactions between numeric and textual data are taken into consideration. This result establishes that contextualized numbers are substantially more valuable in shaping beliefs about the future than context-free numbers. In other words, the interactions between numeric and the associated narrative context have considerable economic importance. $ ^{25} $

In addition to forecast accuracy, we confirm our results using two additional performance measures: (1) area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and (2) macro F1-score. Both measures are commonly used to evaluate binary classifiers and are functions of Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors. ROC curve shows the true positive rate plotted for each admissible rate of false positives. A perfect model ranks positive instances higher than negative ones at every threshold and thus has an AUC of 1, whereas a random classifier does distinguish between true and false positives, resulting in an AUC of 0.5. The F1-score is the inverse harmonic mean of precision (how many predicted positives are correct) and recall (how many positives are true positives) and works well when classes are imbalanced. $ ^{26} $ The two measures thus complement each other in assessing the performance of a classifier. Both metrics indicate that the fully connected model significantly outperforms the partially connected model, implying considerable contextuality of numeric data. Similar conclusions follow from the F1-score, confirming that the fully connected model performs well in terms of both Type I and Type II errors.

### 5.3 NARRATIVE CONTEXT AND STOCK RETURNS

Showing that contextualization matters in predicting future earnings or cash flows does not necessarily imply that investors recognize this, that is, incorporate context when interpreting the accounting numbers. To get at this issue, we examine the relevance of narrative context for explaining stock price movements, which capture the way investors incorporate earnings context into pricing decisions.

We use the same approach as previously, except that our target variable is now stock returns cumulated to capture information processing. More specifically, we focus on two return outcomes: (1) an annual buy-and-hold return measured over the fiscal year during which earnings are announced (i.e., subsequent to the year over which they are earned) and (2) a 60-day return measured from the 10-K filing date. The long-window measure aims to include the earnings surprise (including any information leakage) released into stock prices around the earnings announcement date and also to allow for the slow incorporation of contextual information into stock prices (Cohen, Malloy, and Nguyen [2020]). In contrast, the 60-day return window aims to exclude the actual earnings surprise but instead focuses on the most active period during which investors are processing MD&A information. In both cases, the model is trained to predict whether the return is positive or negative.

The table reports the predictive accuracy of four ANN models that predict the direction of future annual stock returns. Returns are defined as the one-year buy-and-hold returns from the fiscal year-end dates. The models predict the direction of future returns (positive or negative). We measure predictive accuracy as the percentage of correct predictions. Model (1) relies on accounting information only (current earnings, current operating cash flow, and their lagged terms of up to four years). Model (2) relies on contextual information only. Models (3) and (4) use both accounting and contextual information in their initial layer. Model (3) is a partially connected model; it does not allow for interactions between accounting and context information, whereas model (4) is a fully connected model; it allows for such interactions. Contextuality, the importance of context for the interpretation of numeric information, is measured as the difference in the predictive accuracy between models (4) and (3). Each model is estimated 30 times, and we report the averaged values for prediction accuracy. To test for statistical significance, we first calculate the pooled standard deviation across all years and test for equality in variances between models (3) and (4). Trend denotes a slope coefficient from a time series regression of predictive accuracy on a linear time trend. Turning to the analysis of time trends, our findings are the following. Although columns 1–4 do not exhibit statistically significant time trends, just like in the case of earnings prediction (table 3), we observe clear evidence of increasing time trend in contextuality ( $ \lambda_{1} $ of 0.0032, t-value = 4.22). This can also be seen in figure 4. The figure also reveals a positive trend in the amount of information conveyed by the narrative context on its own (not interacted) relative to the reported earnings and cash flow numbers, that is, the difference between column 1 and column 2.

Table 6 presents the results based on the 60-day return window. Because earnings are announced via press releases before MD&A filing, the 60-day return window generally excludes the earnings surprise on the announcement date. Instead, it aims to capture the processing of MD&A details by investors as the information gets impounded into prices. Column 1 indicates that accounting information alone leads to the predictive accuracy of 57.03%. The higher explanatory power is intuitive because the disclosed information continues to flow into the stock prices during this window. This number increases slightly in columns 2 and 3, and our reported prediction accuracy is similar to other recent return prediction models using textual input (Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu [2022]). However, it jumps considerably when the model allows for the interactions between the contextual and numeric inputs, reaching an accuracy of 64.80% or an 11% increase. A similar effect is observed across the two alternative performance measures, ROC AUC and F1-score. The evidence confirms that the context within which earnings are disclosed has both a statistically significant and economically pronounced effect on the way the stock market processes accounting information.

Finally, the analysis also confirms the presence of time trends in the importance of contextual interactions when interpreting accounting numbers. Indeed, contextuality, which is reported in the last column of table 6, exhibits a positive and statistically significant time trend, which can also be seen in figure 5. As previously, we observe a positive trend in the incremental information content of contextual information (not interacted), that is, in the difference between columns 2 and 1.

Our results contrast with those in Brown and Tucker [2011], who report decreasing short-term market reactions to MD&A disclosures and conclude that they lose informativeness (due to overlapping content) over time. Although we do not focus on short-term market reactions, our evidence reconciles with their findings in that we observe a negative (although statistically insignificant) trend in the predictive ability of contextual information to explain 60-day stock returns. At the same time, our evidence indicates the growing importance of the interactions between reported numbers and their context.

### 5.4 WHAT CONTRIBUTES TO CONTEXTUALITY?

Does the importance of textual context change under different economic scenarios and when does context matter most? Our methodology allows answering this question by measuring changes in contextuality depending on a firm's economic circumstances. We explore several economic scenarios where taking contextual information into account is likely more important. $ ^{27} $

First, we expect that narrative context is more likely to be important when the company is more difficult to evaluate (value) based on numeric information. We expect this to be the case when (i) a firm's past performance may not be representative of the future and (ii) accounting numbers do not easily map into valuations (assets are harder to measure). To proxy for the first condition, we partition the sample based on past earnings variability (Dichev and Tang [2009]), the presence of unusually high (top 20%) or low (bottom 20%)—thus expected to reverse—accruals (Sloan [1996]), and an indicator for a reported net loss (Hayn [1995]). To proxy for the second condition, we partition the sample on market-to-book ratio (Beaver and Ryan [2005]) and political risk (Hassan et al. [2019]). We use the same methodology as in the main analysis and report the mean value of contextuality for each group and for each target variable.

The results are presented in table 7, panel A. t-statistics for the differences in average contextuality across subsamples are in parentheses. In line with our expectations, context is more important when earnings are less likely to be forward-looking. In particular, context generally matters more when firms have more volatile earnings, when accruals are more extreme, and when an operating loss is reported. In addition, contextuality is higher when firms are more difficult to value or are subject to increased uncertainties. Intuitively, context is more important in these cases compared to instances where the company operates in a steady state. Taken together, our results imply that context tends to matter more when firms' numeric information is more difficult to evaluate.

Second, we expect that numeric disclosure is more difficult to interpret during the period of increased macroeconomic uncertainty or during economic downturns. As a result, context is expected to be more valuable and to enhance the interpretation of numeric information in such situations. ## 6. An Application: Contextualized Earnings Persistence

To illustrate the value and applicability of our results, we conclude with one practical application of our findings that quantifies context-based heterogeneity in earnings persistence. Earnings persistence is of interest to both academics and decision makers as it is often viewed as the primary measure of earnings quality (Francis et al. [2004], Dichev et al. [2013]). At the same time, measuring persistence is challenging. Prior research typically relies on either cross-sectional proxies or uses a rolling window to estimate persistence at the firm level. Neither of these approaches can measure earnings persistence for a specific firm-year, which is a distinctive feature of context-based persistence derived from our model. To model heterogeneity in earnings persistence as a function of context, we adopt a recently developed methodology in Farrell, Liang, and Misra [2020, 2021]. This approach allows modeling the persistence parameter as a nonparametric function of high-dimensional vectors of firm characteristics. The estimates generated from this model have a similar interpretation to regression persistence estimates, except that they vary cross-sectionally and over time.

### 6.1 METHODOLOGY

We use a standard earnings persistence regression, except that its parameters are modeled as flexible functions of textual vectors:

 $$ \begin{aligned}E_{it+1}&=\gamma_{0it}+\gamma_{1it}E_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t}\\&=\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{context}_{it})+\gamma_{1}(\mathbf{context}_{it})E_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t+1},\end{aligned} $$ 

where  $ E_{it} $ is scaled earnings of firm i in year t and context $ _{it} $ is 768-dimensional contextual vector. We use the same neural network architecture, except that the model now predicts the regression coefficients as its output. To implement this, we remove the standard activation function from the output layer and instead add a custom layer that computes forecast error  $ \epsilon_{i,t+1} = E_{i,t+1} - \gamma_0 (\text{context}_{it}) - \gamma_1 (\text{context}_{it}) E_{i,t} $. The model is trained to generate the parameters that minimize the root mean squared error (RMSE). The trained output of the model,  $ (\hat{\gamma}_{1it}, \hat{\gamma}_{2it}) $, is the context-based earnings persistence parameters estimated at the firm-year level. $ ^{29} $

### 6.3 VALIDATING CONTEXT-BASED EARNINGS PERSISTENCE

Since the firm-year measure of earnings persistence is potentially useful for different empirical applications, we perform several steps to validate it and explore its empirical properties.

6.3.1. Face Validity of the Measure. As a first step, we examine the face validity of context-based persistence. We randomly select eight text snippets from the top and bottom 5% of the distribution of context-based persistence coefficients and provide them in online appendix table OA-5. Online appendix table OA-5, panel A, presents examples that represent low context-based earnings persistence. When reading through these examples, one quickly gets a sense that the corresponding companies are experiencing economic shocks (disruptions) and are expressing uncertainties about the future. For example, companies disclose (1) potential adverse effects on future margins, (2) the potential need to respond to competitive pressures by lowering prices, (3) concerns about negative publicity, (4) a

boost to the results due to an extra week in the fiscal year, among others. These factors are intuitive indicators of the transitory nature of earnings. Online appendix table OA-5, panel B, reports eight sample texts associated with high context-based earnings persistence. These examples generally convey confidence in the operating performance. Overall, the examples are consistent with the model's ability to discriminate between transitory versus permanent earnings components based on the context. $ ^{30} $

6.3.2. Can Context-Based Earnings Persistence Be Inferred Based on Firm Characteristics? As a second step, we investigate whether variation in our context-based earnings persistence is driven by firm characteristics. We estimate the following regression:

 $$ \mathrm{P e r s i s t e n c e}_{i t}=\gamma\mathbf{X}_{i t}+\delta_{t}+\lambda_{j}+\epsilon_{i t}, $$ 

where  $ Persistence_{it} = \hat{\gamma}_1(\text{context}_{it}) $ is the estimated context-based persistence. The vector of firm characteristics  $ \mathbf{X}_{it} $ includes size (log of total assets), market-to-book ratio, return on assets, leverage, capital intensity, and loss indicator. We also control for the context-based intercept,  $ \hat{\gamma}_{0,it} $, to facilitate interpretations. The model includes time fixed effects,  $ \delta_t $, and industry fixed effects,  $ \lambda_j $. We cluster standard errors by industry and winsorize continuous variables at 1% and 99% levels where applicable. Table OA-6, panel A, provides descriptive statistics for the sample used in this analysis.

Table OA-6, panel B, presents the results. We tabulate the models with (column 1) and without (column 2) industry fixed effects because they are correlated with firm characteristics. The results are qualitatively similar in both columns. We find that context-based persistence is not statistically or economically associated with firm characteristics. The only exception is firm Size, which exhibits a significantly negative association. $ ^{31} $ These findings imply the heterogeneity in context-based persistence that goes beyond firm characteristics.

6.3.3. Is Persistence Informative Out of Sample? The ultimate test of the value added by context-based persistence is whether it can predict the actual earnings dynamics, that is, whether earnings persist or mean-revert, and whether it can do this more effectively compared to alternative firm-year-specific existing proxies. Specifically, a common approach to estimating earnings persistence by firm-year is to rely on firm-level rolling window regressions. However, the longer the rolling window is, the less descriptive

the estimates become of the current year’s persistence. We implement this approach to obtain firm i’s earnings persistence in year t by estimating it using firm i’s data from t - 10 to t. Note that such an approach assumes that persistence is constant over a 10-year window and, hence, is context-free.

To evaluate information in context-based versus context-free persistence estimates, we estimate the following out-of-sample regression:

 $$ \begin{aligned}E_{i,t+1}&=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}E_{t}+\beta_{2}Persistence_{it}+\beta_{3}E_{t}\times Persistence_{it}+\gamma_{1}\mathbf{X}_{it}\\&+\gamma_{2}Persistence_{it}\times\mathbf{X}_{it}+\delta_{t}+\lambda_{j}+\epsilon_{it},\end{aligned} $$ 

where the variables are defined as previously. Note that  $ Persistence_{it} $ is estimated based on information at or before time t so that the model does not see  $ E_{i,t+1} $. Columns 1 and 2 provide estimates based on the context-free persistence (firm-level rolling window estimates), whereas columns 3 and 4 rely on context-based persistence. We find that the interaction term,  $ Earnings \times Persistence $, is marginally significant in column 1 and it loses statistical significance in column 2 after we control for determinants of persistence. In other words, context-free persistence has a limited ability to explain earnings dynamics out of sample. In contrast, the coefficient on the interaction term is positive and statistically significant both in columns 3 and 4. The interaction  $ Earnings_{it} \times Persistence_{it} $ remains significantly positive after controlling for firm characteristics. These results indicate that context-based earnings persistence contains information useful in predicting earnings dynamics beyond that in context-free proxies.

## 7. Discussion

The deep learning methodology is particularly valuable in instances where one needs to learn a large number of features instead of constructing them manually and where deep nonlinearities (interactions) in the modeled relationships are likely to be present (Bochkay et al. [2023]). These are indeed critical aspects of our study that allow us to draw qualitatively different conclusions. Measuring the contextuality of numeric disclosures requires modeling high-dimensional semantic meaning and capturing deep nonlinearities that textual data are known for (Bengio, Ducharme, and Vincent [2000]). In contrast, prior literature largely focuses on unidimensional textual attributes such as readability (e.g., Li [2008]). Although these studies generally establish the statistical significance of these interactions, they do not show whether the interactions add economically significant explanatory power.

We comprehensively evaluate this issue in section 2 of the online appendix. For example, we replicate the regression of future earnings on current earnings along with its interaction with the Fog index (readability) examined in Li [2008]. We show that the interaction of Fog and current earnings increases the adjusted  $ R^{2} $ in this regression by only 0.0001, or 0.03% of the explained variation. This incremental explanatory power is infinitesimal compared to the contextuality values we observe in table 3. Furthermore, the interactions are economically less important than the main effects of textual data, which is the opposite of our findings. We extend this analysis to a number of other textual characteristics, such as sentiment, and examine alternative specifications. Overall, we conclude that prior empirical evidence, which relies on regression-based methodologies and shallow interactions, is inconsistent with an economically significant contextuality of textual disclosures.

## 8. Conclusion

Different modes of corporate communication often aim to complement and enhance each other. In particular, narrative disclosures often aim to contextualize the interpretation of numeric information reported in financial statements. We study the degree of contextuality, or the extent to which narrative information surrounding the reported earnings numbers helps investors interpret these numbers. We introduce a novel methodology to quantify the economic importance of context in the numeric interpretations by allowing versus restricting deep interactions between multidimensional textual and numeric information. In particular, we use a densely versus sparsely connected ANN combined with a large language model (BERT) to model the interpretation process in a way that has an analogy to information processing of multimodal data in human brain. We show that the incorporation of earnings context disclosed in MD&A enables users of financial statements to derive substantial improvement in forming accurate expectations about an entity's future prospects.

Our evidence enhances our understanding of the importance of context for the interpretation of numbers reported in financial statements. Specifically, our results suggest that MD&A disclosures are useful not only because of their direct informational content but also because they alter the meaning of numeric information. Specifically, allowing for deep nonlinear interactions between numeric disclosures and their narrative discussions substantially improves inferences about future firms' earnings, cash flows, and stock performance and hence is of first-order importance in decision making. Further, our results suggest that the informational value of textual-numeric interactions exceeds, often substantially, the direct incremental value of textual information.

We also show that earnings contextuality, that is, the importance of context in the interpretation of earnings numbers, increases in circumstances where numbers are less likely to be informative. It also exhibits a positive time trend, which suggests that MD&A reports are becoming more relevant to decision makers over time. The evidence provides a potential explanation for increased investor reactions to earnings announcements (Beaver, McNichols, and Wang [2020]) given that we do not observe systematic changes in the informativeness of accounting numbers over time.

Finally, we demonstrate a practical application of our findings by modeling context-based earnings persistence. We show rich heterogeneity in earnings persistence derived from earnings context, which cannot be easily explained by firm characteristics. Moreover, context-based persistence is useful out of sample in predicting earnings dynamics and dominates firm-year-specific context-free estimates. This finding echoes the result in Ball and Nikolaev [2022] that accommodating firm-specific heterogeneity is critical when using earnings to predict cash flows.

Our study opens a number of avenues for future research. Multimodal communications are becoming increasingly prevalent, while the use of

multimodal data is gaining momentum in ML (e.g., Mayew and Venkatachalam [2012], Hobson, Mayew, and Venkatachalam [2012], Baik et al. [2023], De la Parra and Gallemore [2024]). Although we focus on textual-numeric complementarities, a number of interesting questions can also be addressed based on the method we introduce. The examples of potential research questions are as follows.

First, similar to narrative disclosures providing context to numbers, vocal cues provide context to narrative communication (e.g., Mayew and Venkatachalam [2012], Hobson, Mayew, and Venkatachalam [2012], Baik et al. [2023], De la Parra and Gallemore [2024]). Whether and to what extent these contextual features enhance the narrative that is communicated by management is an open question that prior methodologies did not allow to effectively address. Second, interactions between verbal communications and nonverbal cues, such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language, hold promise in the detection of deception and fraudulent reporting (e.g., Peng et al. [2022], Dávila and Guasch [2022], Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu [2023]). Research in psychology suggests that detecting deceptions remains a highly challenging problem (see DePaulo et al. [2003] for review). It is reasonable to expect that individuals involved in fraud exhibit deep interactions between their nonverbal movements and vocal cues when they speak publicly. Our method readily allows tackling the importance of interactions between oral and visual communication to detect deceptive practices. Finally, the context-based persistence we derive can be used to understand the value of context when measuring accounting quality and the analysis can be extended to study the role of context for other accounting properties, such as earnings manipulations. In sum, the ability to contextualize corporate communication allows asking a number of new research questions.
