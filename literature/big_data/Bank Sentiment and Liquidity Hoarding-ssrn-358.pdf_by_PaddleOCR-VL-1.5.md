## ABSTRACT

We analyze how bank sentiment affects bank liquidity hoarding, distinguishing unexplained beliefs of bank managers from fundamental-based beliefs. We build a bank management sentiment measure from textual analysis of 10-Ks and utilize a comprehensive bank liquidity hoarding measure. We find that negative bank sentiment increases liquidity hoarding not warranted by a bank's fundamental conditions or external circumstances. Further analysis confirms that our findings reflect bank volition rather than being driven solely by borrowers or depositors. We address endogeneity concerns using exogenous weather conditions as instruments. Overall, our findings suggest that bank sentiment can influence how much liquidity banks provide to the economy and financial system.

Keywords: bank sentiment, liquidity hoarding, textual analysis, bank disclosure

JEL Classification: G21, G02, D03

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The sentiment of economic agents is a powerful force in the real economy and the financial system (Keynes, 1936). The sentiment of corporate managers influences their key decision-making. Investor sentiment leads to significant misallocations in financial markets not adjusted by rational market forces. Consumer sentiment forecasts consumer spending and other macroeconomic and financial market outcomes. $ ^{1} $ Perhaps surprisingly, there is little investigation into the sentiment of bank managers. Bank sentiment, broadly defined, is bank managers’ belief about current and future economic conditions affecting their operation and stability that is not justified by the economic fundamentals at hand. This paper explores the impact of this sentiment on banks’ liquidity provision, the main function of banks as financial intermediaries (Kashyap et al., 2002). When banks hoard liquidity due to unfounded beliefs, it can result in significant adverse impacts on the economy and financial system (Reinhart & Rogoff, 2009; Berger & Bouwman, 2017; Berger & Sedunov, 2017).

We hypothesize that more pessimistic bank managers hoard liquidity beyond what is warranted by bank fundamentals and market conditions. This hypothesis is based on evidence from psychology, behavioral finance, and banking literature. Prior studies find that negative sentiment increases subjective probabilities of negative events, influencing how individuals form expectations (Johnson & Tversky, 1983; Wright & Bower, 1992; Wegener & Petty, 1994). Sentiment can also influence the risk-aversion of decision-makers (Zuckerman, 1984; Wong & Carducci, 1991; Horvath & Zuckerman, 1993; Bassi et al., 2013). Thus, negative sentiment can

make economic agents overly pessimistic about future conditions and reduce their risk-taking. Banking research also finds that top managers have first-order impacts on the overall banking organizations' strategic decisions, such as setting target levels of credit extensions and securing funding sources (Rajan, 2004). When top managers hold unjustified beliefs about future economic conditions, they may hoard more liquidity.

We derive bank management sentiment from banks’ annual reports (10-K forms). Measurement of bank sentiment can be challenging because it is an unobservable construct that reflects the attitudes, beliefs, and emotions of bank managers. Surveying bank managers is impractical, given the administrative cost of collecting answers frequently and potential bias in reporting. In contrast, accounting disclosure documents are readily available, and bank managers spend substantial time and effort in preparing these documents. The sentiment embedded in 10-Ks is likely to reflect top corporate executives’ team-level sentiment as they are required to carefully review these documents (Sarbanes-Oxley Act, 2002; SEC, 2002). Our approach using 10-Ks builds on the accounting literature that explores the information content of disclosure documents (Feldman et al., 2010; Davis et al., 2012; Li, 2013; Frankel et al., 2016; Hanley & Hoberg, 2019).

Our main hypothesis is that negative sentiment can drive bankers to hoard more liquidity. Identifying the impact of unjustified sentiment above and beyond the fundamentals on liquidity hoarding is an important part of our analysis. We employ Hribar et al.'s (2017) approach—originally adapted from Baker and Wurgler (2006) and Lemmon and Portniaguina (2006)—to segregate the fundamental-based beliefs and the unexplained beliefs. We adapt this approach to the banking setting and regress the net negative words ratio on a more comprehensive set of fundamentals at the macro and bank location×year level. In a similar manner to Hribar et al. (2017), we employ the residuals from this regression to measure unjustified negative sentiment and the

predicted values to measure fundamental-based beliefs. We examine the effects of the unjustified negative sentiment while controlling for the fundamental-based beliefs in all our regressions.

A proper measure of bank liquidity hoarding should encompass all the opportunities available to banks to acquire and deploy liquidity. $ ^{2} $ We employ the comprehensive measure of bank liquidity hoarding developed by Berger et al. (2022), capturing comprehensive liquidity hoarding activities in all individual asset, liability, and off-balance sheet categories on the Call Reports.

We include annual sentiment data from 837 publicly traded U.S. banks and bank holding companies (BHCs) from 1993 to 2016 based on 7,770 unique annual 10-K files. We calculate liquidity hoarding for 57,841 quarterly bank observations for our analysis.

We find statistically and economically significant evidence supporting our hypothesis that banks with more negative manager sentiment hoard additional liquidity beyond the level warranted by the banks' fundamentals and market conditions. Our results hold while controlling for bank fundamentals and other sentiment measures for investors, consumers, and corporate managers of non-financial firms. The effect is more pronounced for banks with lower pre-existing liquidity and higher liquidity-related uncertainty.

Our findings are robust to controlling for alternative measures of bank management sentiment built on CEOs' options-exercising behaviors, texts in the MD&A section, and earnings conference call scripts. This robustness check implies that bank management sentiment derived from the 10-K annual reports offers distinct information about their liquidity hoarding decisions over other sentiment measures in the literature.

To further rule out the possibility that the empirical relations between negative bank sentiment and liquidity hoarding could be solely driven by borrowers and depositors, we also analyze the impact of bank sentiment on the interest rate spreads on bank term loans and credit lines, as well as spreads on deposits. We find that credit spreads increase when bank negative sentiment increases, implying that banks' reduction of liquidity supply outweighs borrowers' reduction of liquidity demand. Similarly, deposit spreads increase in response to an increase in negative bank sentiment, suggesting that banks' demand for liquidity outweighs depositors' provision of liquidity.
We also include an instrumental variable (IV) application to address potential endogeneity concerns. We employ exogenous local weather conditions in the vicinity of bank headquarters to instrument for negative bank management sentiment. A large set of weather conditions may potentially affect bank management sentiment. To avoid the problem of hand-picking or data-mining concerns, we implement the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) of Belloni et al. (2011) based on combinations of 2,090 instruments created from 144 different weather conditions in the vicinity of bank headquarters.
Our paper sheds additional light on the informativeness of banks' disclosures in the accounting literature (see Beatty & Liao, 2014 for a literature review). We show that the information content of language in bank disclosure documents can provide additional insight into banks' sentiments and their key decisions. Our empirical analyses support that banks' annual reports can provide a unique source of information regarding bank management teams' beliefs about their liquidity-related uncertainty and future economic conditions. This paper also provides a unique sentiment measure of bank managers, distinct from fundamental-based beliefs.
Our study also contributes to the bank liquidity hoarding literature. Prior studies investigate various determinants of banks' liquidity hoarding, such as Knightian uncertainty (Caballero &

Krishnamurthy, 2008), aggregate liquidity demand (Allen et al., 2009), counterparty credit risk (Heider et al., 2015), and economic policy uncertainty (Berger et al., 2022). We offer novel evidence that the sentiment of bank managers can be an important driver of their liquidity hoarding. In doing so, we integrate various components of bank portfolios in a comprehensive manner, contrasting with numerous studies that focus solely on analyzing the impacts on single or limited subsets of portfolio items, such as liquid assets (Acharya & Mora, 2015), interbank funds (Afonso et al., 2011), or deposits (Berrospide, 2021).

## 2. RELATED LITERATURE

Our paper relates to the growing literature on the informational content of financial disclosure documents in predicting or affecting key economic decisions. The textual content in the disclosure documents can provide additional insight beyond what is available from numerical information (Feldman et al., 2010; Loughran & McDonald, 2011; Davis et al., 2012; Li, 2013). Frankel et al. (2016) find that a significant portion of firm-level accruals can be explained by textual information in the MD&A section of 10-K filings. Hanley and Hoberg (2019) employ a textual topic extraction technique to parse the U.S. banks’ 10-K filings and build “emerging risks” measures in the financial sector. However, banking research in the accounting literature has mainly focused on banks’ timely loan loss provisions to investigate the role of banks’ transparency (Beatty & Liao, 2014; Bushman & Williams, 2015). In our paper, we emphasize the textual information of bank disclosure documents. We provide evidence that the bank managerial sentiment reflected in the 10-K filings explains a significant portion of their future liquidity hoarding decisions.

This paper also relates to the accounting and finance literature, deriving and understanding various economic agents' sentiments. There is extensive research on the impacts of investor-consumer- and corporate manager-sentiment, often measured at the aggregate level (Baker &

Wurgler, 2006; Lemmon & Portniaguina, 2006). Often developed in the asset pricing context, these studies focus on the impact of the aggregate-level sentiment measures on stock returns. For example, Jiang et al. (2019) construct an aggregate corporate manager sentiment based on the textual tone in non-financial firms' disclosure documents and report that the corporate sentiment predicts future stock returns. In our paper, we derive and estimate the impact of another key economic agent's sentiment—bank managers. Our bank sentiment measure is time-varying at the bank level and offers the opportunity to observe and test cross-sectional as well as intertemporal sentiment differences.

## 3. DATA AND KEY VARIABLES

### 3.1 Bank Sentiment Measure Derived from Textual Analysis of Annual Reports

We construct our bank sentiment measure based on the textual information of the most recent annual reports (Form 10-K) of publicly traded banks and BHCs from 1993:Q4 to 2016:Q4. Annual reports of public companies provide comprehensive overviews of their business and financial conditions assessed by core management officials. Almost all publicly traded banking organizations are BHCs that own commercial banks, so the sentiment is measured at the BHC level for these organizations and at the bank level for independently traded banks. We refer to it as bank management sentiment in all cases for expositional convenience.

We obtain 7,770 unique 10-K files reported by 837 publicly-traded banks. $ ^{3} $ Based on Loughran and McDonald’s (2011) dictionary of positive and negative words, we first calculate the net negative words ratio as the fraction of negative words minus positive words relative to total

words in the 10-Ks:

 $$ Net Negative Words=\frac{(Negative words-Positive words)}{Total words} $$ 

From the net negative words ratio in the bank annual reports, we segregate the fundamental-based beliefs and the unexplained beliefs by employing Hribar et al.'s (2017) approach. $ ^{4} $ This approach—originally adapted from Baker and Wurgler (2006) and Lemmon and Portniaguina (2006)—derives an aggregate unexplained belief measure from top corporate officials' survey results by regressing survey responses on a host of fundamentals at the macro level. Hribar et al. (2017) define the residuals from this regression as the unexplained belief and the predicted value of this regression as the explained belief. We adapt this approach to the banking research setting and include a more comprehensive set of fundamentals at the macro and bank location×year level. The benefit of our approach is that we can construct a sentiment measure not explained by a host of high-frequency macro fundamentals and time-varying local economic conditions. We estimate the following regression:

Net Negative Words $ _{i,t} $

 $$ \begin{aligned}i&ve Words_{i,t}\\&=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}YLD3_{t}+\beta_{2}T10Y3MM_{t}+\beta_{3}DEF_{t}+\beta_{4}MKT_{t}+\beta_{5}PROD_{t}\\&+\beta_{6}CAY_{t}+\sum LAG_{t-1}+\sum LEAD_{t+1}+Location_{i}\times Year_{t}FE+\epsilon_{i,t},\\ \end{aligned} $$ 

where control variables include monetary policy (yield on three-month T-bills, YLD3; yield spread between three-month T-bills and 10-year T-bonds, T10Y3MM), credit market condition (default spread between AAA-rated and BAA-rated bonds, DEF), stock market condition (CRSP value-weighted returns, MKT), industrial production (PROD), and consumption-to-wealth ratio (CAY).

We define the residuals from this regression as an unexplained negative belief ("Unexp_Neg_Beliefs") measure, and the predicted values as an explained negative belief ("Exp_Neg_Beliefs") measure. We examine the effects of "Unexp_Neg_Beliefs" by controlling for "Exp_Neg_Beliefs" in all our empirical regression models.

There are two features of our sentiment measure distinct from Hribar et al. (2017). First, while Hribar et al. study the corporate manager's sentiment, our measure directly focuses on the bank manager's sentiment. Second, contrary to the aggregate sentiment measure, as in Hribar et al. (2017), our measure is bank-specific. Aggregate sentiment, which refers to the overall banking industry's sentiment as a whole, may not accurately reflect the specific optimism or pessimism of individual banks. The bank-specific sentiment measure helps outline a micro-level link between bank management sentiment and its key economic decisions. In this paper's setting, it is beneficial to clearly substantiate the impact of a bank-level sentiment on its liquidity hoarding decisions.

Appendix S1 reports the regression result of the annual reports’ net negative tone on economic fundamentals and bank location×year fixed effects.⁵ We find that 80% of the variation in the annual reports’ tone is explained by these variables (adjusted R-squared is 0.805), consistent with prior findings of Lemmon and Portniaguina (2006) for consumer sentiments and Hribar et al. (2017) for the corporate manager sentiments. We derive our measure of bank management sentiment (Unexp_Neg_Beliefs) as the residuals from this regression.

Table 1 reports summary statistics for the 57,841 bank-quarter observations from 1993:Q4 through 2016:Q4. Unexp_Neg_Beliefs has substantial variations across banks over time, suggesting that sentiment revealed in banks’ annual reports has significant bank-specific determinants. $ ^{6} $

Some prior studies employ dummy variables for option-based bank CEO optimism (Bui et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2018), classifying CEOs as optimistic when they postpone exercising in-the-money stock options. Our text-based measure of bank management sentiment offers several advantages. First, the CEO's decision to exercise financial options in the company's stock is likely influenced by other factors, including the CEO's personal wealth, diversification objectives, and risk aversion. Second, our text-based measure may be more representative of bank management as a team because the 10-K is produced and vetted by the management team rather than representing only CEO thinking. Nevertheless, in a later section, we show that our result holds even after controlling for the CEO optimism dummy variable. $ ^{7} $

Other possible sources of textual information are the conference call scripts and the MD&A section of the 10-K reports. Conference calls are more spontaneous and may offer a direct link to bank managers' sentiments. MD&A section may provide more targeted discussion by management teams. However, the 10-K is a more comprehensive report that provides a detailed overview of a company's financial performance and potential risks. All sections can provide additional information about the bank manager's opinions and assessment. Importantly, conference calls are voluntary, creating sample selection biases. In Appendix S2, we discuss the pros and cons of each approach and acknowledge potentially different interpretations of the whole 10K-based bank sentiment measure from different perspectives. $ ^{8} $ We provide robustness check results controlling for the sentiment embedded in the MD&A sections and conference call scripts (see Section 4).

A valid question about the sentiment measure is, “What is the sentiment about?” There is no single answer because the sentiment may reflect managers’ subjective views on various aspects of the economic conditions affecting their banking operations. One possible subject in this paper’s context is “liquidity uncertainty,” and we provide evidence that the impact of sentiment on future liquidity hoarding is greater when topics related to “liquidity uncertainty” are discussed more often in annual reports (see Section 4).

### 3.2 Bank Liquidity Hoarding Measures

Corporate finance research often focuses on corporate cash hoarding. In the banking context, where liquidity supply in different forms is the key output, liquidity hoarding is not properly measured by retained cash because banks have unique opportunities to raise and disburse liquidity in multiple ways. For example, banks can hoard liquidity by taking in more deposits and/or disbursing fewer on-balance sheet term loans and off-balance sheet loan commitments. The bank liquidity hoarding measure developed by Berger et al. (2022) provides a comprehensive quantification of liquidity hoarding activities at the bank level. The core rationale for this liquidity hoarding measure is that it includes all the sources of liquid funds as well as their uses. Importantly, this measure incorporates all assets, liabilities, and off-balance sheet activities with weights based on their contribution to liquidity hoarding. Appendix S3—adapted from Berger et al. (2022)—presents a detailed list of these items. $ ^{9} $

Specifically, total liquidity hoarding,  $ LH(total) $, is the sum of asset-side, liability-side, and off-balance sheet-side components,  $ LH(asset) + LH(liab) + LH(off) $. Asset-side liquidity hoarding,  $ LH(asset) $ is the weighted sum of liquid assets and illiquid assets. Acquiring liquid assets, such as

cash and securities, makes the bank more liquid (i.e., positive weights to liquid assets), as securities can usually be easily sold for cash. As well, procuring fewer illiquid assets (i.e., negative weights to illiquid assets), such as commercial and industrial (C&I) loans, can also free up cash. In other words, banks can hoard more liquidity by reducing their lending. Similarly, liability-side liquidity hoarding LH(liab) is the weighted sum of liquid liabilities. The rationale is that banks can also raise liquid assets by issuing more liquid liabilities like transaction deposits. The FDIC’s liquidity regulation manual (FDIC, 2020) considers core deposits as low-cost sources of liquidity funding for banks. Off-balance sheet liquidity hoarding, LH(off) is the weighted sum of illiquid guarantees and liquid derivatives. Illiquid guarantees, such as loan commitments, can be withdrawn quickly and drain cash, and liquid derivatives, measured by their gross fair values, can be sold to raise cash. In our empirical analyses, the LH measures are normalized by gross total assets (GTA) to be comparable across banks and avoid dominance by the largest banks. $ ^{10} $

In Table 1, we report total bank liquidity hoarding normalized by GTA,  $ LH(total)/GTA $ has a mean of 0.074, suggesting that banks hoard liquidity of 7.4% of GTA on average. The liquidity hoarding measure has a wide dispersion across banks with the 25th and 75th percentile values at -0.050 and 0.193, respectively.

## 4. REGRESSION ANALYSIS OF THE IMPACT OF BANK MANAGEMENT SENTIMENT ON LIQUIDITY HOARDING

To test our hypothesis that Unexp_Neg_Beliefs is associated with more liquidity hoarding, we estimate regressions of the form:

 $$ \begin{aligned}(LH/GTA)_{i,t}&=\beta Unexp_{-}Neg_{-}Beliefs_{i,t-1}+\delta^{\prime}X_{i,t-1}+\theta^{\prime}W_{i,t-1}+\nu^{\prime}S_{t-1}+\gamma^{\prime}EPU_{t-1}+\alpha_{i}\\&+q_{t}+\epsilon_{i,t},\end{aligned} $$ 

where i and t index a bank and a calendar quarter, respectively. The dependent variable is one of the normalized liquidity hoarding measures:  $ LH(total)/GTA $,  $ LH(asset)/GTA $,  $ LH(liab)/GTA $, or  $ LH(off)/GTA $. The key independent variable is the  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $. We control for fundamental-based beliefs ( $ Exp\_Neg\_Beliefs $) and a host of variables related to banks' fundamentals and market conditions in all empirical analyses. Our bank controls (X) include the natural logarithm of bank gross total assets ( $ Ln(GTA) $), and its squared ( $ Sqr.Ln(GTA) $), capital ratio (Capital ratio), and the ratio of net income to gross total assets (Earnings) to account for bank size, leverage, and earnings. The capital ratio controls for the effects of banks' financial constraints on their liquidity hoarding decisions. We also control for the local market and corporate demand for investment (W): bank competition (HHI) based on bank deposits, local market size (Population), local firms' average value (Tobin's Q), and their cash flows (Cash flows). Bank customers' Tobin's Q and cash flows would represent their investment opportunities and funding abilities, affecting their demands for credits and subsequently influencing the banks' liquidity hoarding behavior. We include macro-level sentiment measures (S) of corporate manager sentiment (Corporate sentiment) by Jiang et al. (2019), investor sentiment (Investor sentiment) by Baker and Wurgler (2006), and the consumer sentiment index by the University of Michigan (Consumer sentiment). $ ^{[11]} $ We also control for economic policy uncertainty (EPU) measure, influencing banks' liquidity hoarding decisions (Berger et al., 2022). Controlling for the aggregate corporate, investor, and consumer sentiments is important so that our main coefficient on  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $ represents only the

effects of a bank manager’s sentiment beyond these other sentiment effects.

Additionally, we include bank fixed effects  $ (a) $ to control for omitted bank characteristics that are invariant over time, and quarter dummies  $ (q) $ to account for seasonality of bank liquidity hoarding. $ ^{12} $ We cluster standard errors at the bank holding company and year-quarter level to account for correlations of error terms.

We obtain bank-specific variables and local market conditions from Call Reports and the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, respectively. We compute the economic conditions of banks' potential customers (Tobin's Q, and Cash flows) based on information from Compustat. Potential customer firms of banks are those in the banks' states of operation. We take the weighted average of these variables for each bank based on the proportion of a bank's deposits in each area (Metropolitan Statistical Area (MSA) or counties). We obtain bank deposit amounts per branch from the Summary of Deposits by FDIC and Bouwman's website. Appendix A presents more detailed definitions of all variables.

Table 1 also shows summary statistics for the control variables. All dollar amounts are measured in 2016 dollars. The median size of banks  $ Ln(GTA) $ is 13.53, corresponding to 752 million. Capital ratio has a mean of 0.078, and most banks have capital ratios between 0.06 and 0.09. Tobin's Q of firms in the states where a bank has operations is an average value of 2.419, similar to the average value in the full CRSP/Compustat universe. Cash flows is widely dispersed across firms in different locations with the 25 $ ^{th} $ percentile at -0.014 and the 75 $ ^{th} $ percentile at 0.017.

Table 2 Panel A presents coefficient estimates from regressions of  $ LH(total)/GTA $ on  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $ and the controls. In column (1), we control for bank characteristics and bank and seasonal fixed effects. We observe that the estimated coefficient on  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $ is positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. In column (2), we additionally control for local market-level variables and continue to find a positive and statistically significant result. This result suggests that the impact of  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $ on bank liquidity hoarding is not driven by local market characteristics, such as corporate demand for cash or investment opportunities.

Column (3) also controls for other well-known sentiment measures of corporate managers (Corporate sentiment), investors (Investor sentiment), and consumers (Consumer sentiment), as well as economic policy uncertainty (EPU). The coefficient on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs is still positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. This result suggests that bank sentiment has an incremental impact on their liquidity hoarding beyond corporate-, investor-, and consumer-sentiment and EPU. $ ^{13} $ The economic significance of the estimates is also sizable. A one-standard-deviation increase in Unexp_Neg_Beliefs leads to 2.4 percentage points increase in LH(total)/GTA. Column (3) is our main specification that allows us to measure the impact of an individual bank manager’s sentiment beyond the effects of aggregate corporate, investor, or consumer sentiment or economic policy uncertainty. It also incorporates the effects of differences in bank managerial sentiment in both the cross-section of banks and over time for the same bank.

We next address a potential concern that our results could be spuriously driven in part by

unobserved aggregate factors that trend over time, driving both bank managerial sentiment and bank liquidity hoarding. In column (4), we control for a time trend that may capture unobserved aggregate factors. The estimated coefficient on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs is still statistically significant at the 1% level. In the remainder of our analysis, we use the specification in column (3) without the time trend because our hypothesis includes both effects of cross-sectional and time-series variations in bank managerial sentiment. Overall, the results in Table 2 Panel A with the total liquidity hoarding measure (LH(total)/GTA) provide clear support for our hypothesis. $ ^{14} $

Another interesting finding in Table 2 is that the estimated coefficients on the fundamental-driven Exp_Neg_Beliefs are also positive but statistically insignificant. This result suggests that the impact of Exp_Neg_Beliefs measured in this way is largely absorbed by other control variables and fixed effects in the regression.

Table 2 Panel B presents coefficients estimates from regressions of bank liquidity hoarding components, LH(asset)/GTA, LH(liab)/GTA, and LH(off)/GTA, on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs using the full preferred specification with all the controls. The estimated coefficients on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs are positive and statistically significant for the asset, liability, and off-balance sheet sides. These results suggest that an increase in the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs leads to an increase in all of the components of bank liquidity hoarding. In terms of economic significance, a one-standard-deviation increase in Unexp_Neg_Beliefs is associated with 0.4 percentage points increase in LH(asset)/GTA, 1.7 percentage points increase in LH(liab)/GTA, and 0.2 percentage points increase in LH(off)/GTA, suggesting that the strongest effects are on the liability side.

To better understand the individual items of liquidity hoarding, we regress selected bank

balance sheet and off-balance sheet items that make up much of the bank liquidity hoarding measure on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs (see Appendix S4  $ {}^{15} $). We find that banks increase cash holdings, decrease loans and loan commitments, and also hoard more liquidity through increased deposits. This item-by-item analysis reinforces our main findings and suggests that several mechanisms are at work in explaining the findings.

### 4.2 Heterogeneity Analysis: Liquidity, Uncertainty, and Forward-Looking Statements

We next investigate the implication of Basel III liquidity requirement for the impact of bank management sentiment on liquidity hoarding. We also analyze whether our findings differ by liquidity uncertainty a bank faces. We further test whether the forward-looking component of the negative sentiment mainly drives the liquidity hoarding of banks.

Basel III introduces two liquidity requirements: the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR). While these ratios have similarities to the bank liquidity hoarding measure we use, two key dissimilarities make it difficult to analyze the effects of Basel III in our context. First, LCR and NSFR focus only on what liquidity positions would be under future imagined stressful scenarios as opposed to our bank liquidity hoarding measure using Call Report data. Second, LCR and NSFR are non-public regulatory measures and not readily available for US banks from Call Reports or other public data sources, rendering direct tests impossible.

A plausible implication of Basel III is that banks with more binding liquidity constraints would hoard more liquidity in response to negative sentiment. The Basel III liquidity requirement is based on the idea that bank liquidity is an important determinant of a bank's stability. We estimate the heterogeneous impact of bank sentiment on liquidity hoarding by different levels of prior bank liquidity. We categorize the banks into high- and low-liquidity groups according to their

average LH(total)/GTA over the prior four quarters using the median. We estimate the baseline regression model (Equation 3) separately for the low- and high-liquidity banks. In Table 3 (columns (1) and (2)), we find that the sentiment effects are stronger among banks with a lower level of pre-existing liquidity. This result suggests that sentiment-driven bank managers also consider liquidity as an important foundation of bank stability, as the Basel III liquidity requirement stipulates.

A bank can hoard liquidity when it has an unjustified sentiment about uncertainty it faces. Pigou (1927) and Keynes (1936) argue that economic agents’ sentiment distorts their expectations during periods of high uncertainty. It will be informative to use the “liquidity-related uncertainty” as one concrete object of banks’ unjustified belief in the context of liquidity hoarding and test whether the “liquidity-related uncertainty” amplifies the effect of bank sentiment. We build the measure of “liquidity-related uncertainty” based on the occurrence of liquidity-related words (liquid, liquidity) and the uncertainty-related words (from Loughran and McDonald’s (2011) list of uncertainty words) contained in the 10-K documents. We estimate our baseline regression for samples of high vs. low “liquidity-related uncertainty.” $ ^{16} $ Table 3 (columns (3) and (4)) reports that the impact of bank management sentiment is more pronounced when the liquidity-related uncertainty is high in their 10-Ks documents. Overall, the analyses suggest that banks with more negative sentiment hoard more liquidity when they have liquidity concerns and expect higher uncertainties in the future.

As the 10-K forms discuss both past performance and future prospects, we further test whether the forward-looking component of the sentiment mainly drives the liquidity hoarding of banks. Relying on the dictionary-search method (Li, 2010), we first calculate the ratio of forward-

looking words in the 10-K documents. The list of forward-looking words is based on the "Cautionary Note Regarding Forward-Looking Statements" in the 10-K forms. $ ^{17} $ We run the baseline regression separately for a high vs. low forward-looking statement sample. Table 3, columns (5) and (6) report that the sentiment effect is greater when the statement is more tilted toward forward-looking. The result is consistent with the idea that the forward-looking aspect of the sentiment may serve as a primary driver for the association between bank management sentiment and liquidity hoarding in the future.

### 4.3 Robustness Checks and Additional Analyses

A potential confounding issue is top managers’ unobservable innate traits such as “prudence” or risk aversion. It is possible that more prudent or risk-averse CEOs would file more pessimistic annual reports and hoard more liquidity. To address this concern, we control for CEO fixed effects in an additional analysis. To the extent that a person’s innate prudence or risk aversion is not time-varying – as posited in the labor economics and life-cycle portfolio choice literature (see Stigler & Becker, 1977; Kim et al., 2016) – CEO fixed effects will control for the confounding effect. We extract bank CEOs’ names from the Capital IQ database. We present the estimation result in Table 4 (Panel A, column 1), showing that our baseline results hold.

Prior studies focus on a CEO’s option exercise behavior as a measure of optimism or overconfidence. Others also explore the MD&A section of annual reports or earnings conference call scripts as alternative textual sources for building sentiment measures. In our additional analyses, we control for these measures to ensure that bank management sentiment embedded in the annual reports has a unique explanatory power for banks’ liquidity hoarding decisions. We

build the option-based CEO optimism measure following Huang et al. (2018). We also build other alternative sentiment measures, such as a net negative words ratio of the MD&A section of annual reports and conference call scripts (from Capital IQ). $ ^{18} $ We reestimate the main regression Equation (3), controlling for these additional sentiment measures. Columns (2)-(4) of Table 4 report the results. Our main sentiment measure from the 10-Ks remains positive and statistically significant. $ ^{19} $

A potential econometric concern is that we may, in some cases, be using “stale” sentiment information. Our baseline result is based on quarterly liquidity hoarding measures and annual sentiment measures from the most recent annual reports. There can be a potential concern about the relevance of annual report information that may become stale as the year progresses. In column (5) of Table 4 Panel A, we reestimate the main regression equation (3), excluding the “stale” observations whose  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $ is measured more than one quarter prior to the quarter of the liquidity hoarding dependent variable. The results continue to hold.

Another potential concern is that the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs measure may be confounded by the writing quality of the financial report. In column (6) of Table 4 Panel A, we reestimate our regression by additionally controlling for the readability of the 10-K. We use the Gunning-Fog Index to measure the readability of 10-Ks (Li, 2008). The coefficient estimate is still positive and statistically significant with a similar magnitude to our baseline result.

We further test if negative bank management sentiment is a concern for banks of different sizes. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 4 Panel B report coefficient estimates on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs for small and large banks, respectively. The results suggest that our main findings hold with

A key policy issue in the U.S. since the 1930s is whether BHCs should be allowed to combine commercial and investment banking in the same organization. A crucial issue here is whether the combined institutions are more or less swayed by management sentiment as stand-alone commercial banks. In column (3) of Table 4 Panel B, we estimate the main regression for banks and BHCs with commercial banks only, while column (4) reports results for BHCs with both commercial and investment banks. The coefficient estimates on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs are positive and statistically significant for both sets, but the findings are stronger for the combined institutions.

In an additional analysis (Table 1 of Appendix S5), $ ^{20} $ we test if “liquidity hoarding” appropriately indicates that a bank holds liquidity beyond its expected needs or a benchmark level of liquidity. We employ two approaches: 1) controlling for the lagged liquidity level and 2) estimating the benchmark level of bank liquidity in a multi-stage regression. The benchmark analysis shows that the negative sentiment leads banks to hold more liquidity than their benchmark level, rationalized by their fundamentals and market conditions.

Some of the textual analysis literature focuses on negative words only, arguing that negative words are used with more heed and care than positive words (Chen et al., 2014; Loughran & McDonald, 2016). To address this issue, we derive the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs measure using only the negative words and positive words separately. We include these variables in the baseline regression model and report the estimation result in Appendix S6. $ ^{21} $ The estimated coefficients on negative words and positive words are positive and negative, respectively, and are reasonably close in magnitude, justifying our combined treatment in our main Unexp_Neg_Beliefs measure.

In untabulated analyses, we examine the non-linearity of the relations between the

Unexp_Neg_Beliefs and liquidity hoarding using piecewise spline regressions. We use the median value of the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs as a knot to perform the piecewise regressions. The coefficient estimate on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs is positive and statistically significant for the above-median category, suggesting a stronger impact of bank sentiment on liquidity hoarding when sentiment is more extreme. We also control for the TED spread (the T-bill rate minus the interbank lending rate) and the state-level regulatory leniency index (Agarwal et al., 2014). We find that the main results still hold, implying the bank sentiment effects are not driven by these alternative channels of market sentiment toward banks' credit risks or regulatory sentiment about banks' stability.

## 5. EFFECTS OF BANK SENTIMENT ON INTEREST RATE SPREADS

The empirical association between Unexp_Neg_Beliefs and liquidity hoarding of banks could be driven by both banks' supply and demand choices and customers' supplies and demands for liquidity. To ensure that our findings do not simply reflect customer choices, we examine the effects of Unexp_Neg_Beliefs on interest rate spreads for loans, credit lines, and deposits, which are important liquidity hoarding elements in the asset-side, off-balance sheet-side, and liability-side. Specifically, higher credit spreads in response to negative sentiment would suggest that the observed cutback in credit quantities reflects bank volition in withdrawing credit supply, at least to some extent, rather than being entirely driven by the reduced credit demand of borrowers. Analogously, higher deposit spreads would suggest that increases in deposit quantities reflect increases in banks' deposit demand, as opposed to being driven only by depositors' supply.

For the pricing of loans and credit lines, we employ credit spreads data from Loan Pricing Corporation’s DealScan database on commercial term loans and revolving lines of credit,

representing on- and off-balance sheet credits. $ ^{22} $ We focus on the lead banks for our analyses. We exclude LBO transactions from our analysis because such credit packages include both term loans and credit lines.

We estimate regressions of the form:

 $$ \begin{aligned}Credit spread_{i,j,t}=\rho Unexp_{-}Neg_{-}Beliefs_{i,t-1}+\pi^{\prime}V_{j,t-1}+\vartheta^{\prime}K_{i,j,t}+\omega^{\prime}X_{i,t-1}+\psi^{\prime}W_{i,t-1}\\+\chi^{\prime}S_{t-1}+\zeta EPU_{t-1}+\alpha_{i}+q_{t}+\epsilon_{i,j,t},\end{aligned} $$ 

where i, j, and t index a bank, a borrower, and a calendar quarter, respectively. The dependent variable (Credit spread) is the borrowing credit spread plus annual fee (if any) the borrower pays in percentage over LIBOR. We include bank fixed effects ( $ \alpha $) to control for omitted bank characteristics that are invariant over time; quarter dummies (q) to account for seasonality; and borrower characteristics (V) to account for credit risk, including firm size ( $ Ln(ME) $), book-to-market ratio ( $ BE\_ME $), leverage (Leverage), tangible asset ratio (Tangible), cash ratio (Cash), profitability (Profit), and credit rating (Credit rating). $ ^{23} $ Because of the multi-dimensional aspect of bank credit contracts, we control for other credit contract characteristics (K) such as credit amount (Credit size), maturity ( $ Ln(Maturity) $), collateral (Secured), and covenants (Covnt. Index). In addition, we control for bank-level characteristics (X), local market and corporate demand variables (W), other sentiment measures (S), and EPU as in equation (3). All variables are described in Appendix B.

Table 5 Panel A presents the summary statistics of these variables by term loans and revolvers. The final sample is at the loan facility-bank level, including 266 lead banks and 5,199

borrowing firms from 1993:Q4 through 2016:Q4.

Table 6 reports the estimated impact of Unexp_Neg_Beliefs on the Credit spread for term loans (columns 1-2) and revolvers (columns 3-4). In all regression specifications, the estimated coefficients on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs are positive, consistent with the direction of our hypothesized reductions in bank supply in response to negative bank management sentiment, rather than reduced demand for credit.

We next check the effects of Unexp_Neg_Beliefs on deposit spreads using the following specification:

 $$ \begin{aligned}Deposit&spread_{i,j,t}=gUnexp_{-}Neg_{-}Beliefs_{i,t-1}+l^{\prime}X_{i,t-1}+m^{\prime}W_{i,t-1}+n^{\prime}S_{t-1}\\&+qEPU_{t-1}+\alpha_{i}+q_{t}+City_{j}+\epsilon_{i,j,t}\end{aligned} $$ 

where i, j, and t indicate a bank, a branch and a calendar quarter, respectively. The dependent variable is a Deposit spread for checking accounts, savings accounts, or money market accounts relative to the three-month T-bill rate. We include the same set of controls with Equation (3). Unlike the credit-spread analysis, we include only bank and macro variables because depositor details are not available. In addition to the bank and seasonality fixed effects, we include City fixed effects where a bank branch operates to control for unobservable characteristics of local markets.

We obtain deposit spread information from the RateWatch database. Table 5 Panel B provides summary statistics for the deposit spreads. The data contain 605 unique banks and 398,428 observations at the bank-deposit product-calendar quarter level from 1998:Q1 to 2016:Q4.

Table 7 reports the findings. The coefficient estimates on Unexp_Neg_Beliefs are all positively and statistically significant for the checking accounts, savings accounts, and money market accounts. $ ^{24} $ These results are consistent with our main findings of increased liability-side

liquidity hoarding, reflecting banks’ demands for deposits rather than only depositors’ supplies.

## 6. INSTRUMENTAL VARIABLE ANALYSIS

There can be potential endogeneity concerns regarding  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $. For example, banks may observe latent indicators of future economic conditions, which may drive both  $ Unexp\_Neg\_Beliefs $ and their liquidity hoarding. Another endogeneity concern arises if our negative sentiment measure mainly captures bank distress that provokes regulatory or market participant reactions that require additional liquidity holding.

To address these concerns, we use local weather conditions in the vicinity of bank headquarters as instrumental variables for bank sentiment. Studies in psychology show that exposure to weather conditions can have a cumulative effect on one's psychological state (Cunningham, 1979; Schwarz & Clore, 1983; Lerner et al., 2015). Behavioral economics literature finds that the weather-related impact on sentiment—and subsequent economic decisions—can last as long as a season (Kamstra et al., 2003). An emerging strand of research finds that weather-induced sentiment can affect long-term corporate decisions such as hiring and investment (Chhaochharia et al., 2019) and CEOs' risk-taking (Zolotoy et al., 2019). $ ^{25} $

In line with the literature, we use the local weather conditions as potential instrumental variables for bank management sentiment. We posit that the local weather conditions near bank headquarters influence the sentiment of bank officials (reflected in the annual reports), which then affects their liquidity hoarding decisions via their operating decisions. Our identification strategy is to estimate the impact of weather-driven sentiment on bank behavior, so our empirical analysis

estimates the local average treatment effect (LATE) of the banks whose sentiment is sensitive to changes in exogenous weather conditions.

We obtain a broad set of weather information from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Climate Database. To minimize the discrepancy between the weather conditions and the time/location of top bank managers, we build a “targeted” weather measure focusing on the location and time of top managers’ presence as described in Appendix S7. $ ^{26} $

However, the large set of weather conditions raises the concern of hand-picking or data-mining. To address this concern, we implement the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) to select the best instrumental variables, following Belloni et al. (2011) and Gilchrist and Sands (2016). We include 2,090 dummy variables indicating local weather conditions of bank headquarters in our LASSO selection model. We provide more details about the LASSO implementation process in Appendix S7.

Table 8 Panel A reports the selection result. The first LASSO-chosen instrument is the seasonally-adjusted cloud coverage dummy indicating a -3 to -2 octas difference between the current and preceding three-year average from Monday to Friday working hours with one-quarter lags. For two instrumental variables, LASSO additionally chooses the cloud coverage dummy, indicating the same difference (-3 to -2 octas) between the current and previous three-years average from Monday to Friday with a two-quarter lag. The choice of cloud coverage is largely consistent with prior studies in the literature (Goetzmann et al., 2015; Chhaochharia et al., 2019).

Table 8 Panel A also reports the first-stage regressions of the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs on the various numbers of LASSO-chosen instruments and other control variables. The coefficients on the instrumental variables based on seasonally adjusted weather conditions are all statistically

significant at a 1% level individually. The partial F-statistics for up to two instrumental variables are above the conventional threshold for the weak instrumental variables (Stock & Yogo, 2005). We use two instrumental variables for implementing the two-stage least squares analysis.

In the second-stage regressions in Table 8 Panel B, we regress the liquidity hoarding measures on the instrumented Unexp_Neg_Beliefs measure and the controls. The t-statistics are based on bootstrapped standard errors to mitigate biases from errors in the estimated independent variables. The estimated coefficients on the instrumented Unexp_Neg_Beliefs all have the same positive signs as our main results, and all are statistically significant. These findings suggest that our baseline OLS results are not entirely driven by endogeneity concerns. Regarding the economic magnitude, a one-standard-deviation increase in the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs leads to a 9.7 percentage points increase in LH(total)/GTA. $ ^{27} $

In Table 8 Panel C, we report the coefficient estimates from the second-stage regressions of credit and deposit spreads on the instrumented Unexp_Neg_Beliefs measure and other controls. The estimated coefficients on the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs are all positive and statistically significant, suggesting that banks with more negative sentiment reduce credit supply and increase deposit demand. These results suggest that our main findings reflect, at least in part, banks' actions rather than borrowers' or depositors' choices.

A potential concern for the validity of the instrumental variable analysis is that the weather conditions near bank headquarters may also affect their borrowers or depositors. This concern is

already somewhat mitigated in our empirical setting because our sample of publicly traded banks and BHCs often have extensive geographic footprints beyond the headquarters where the weather is measured. $ ^{28} $ Nonetheless, to address this concern further, we restrict our sample to banks operating in multiple states. The IV analysis results presented in the Appendix S8 confirm the impact of Unexp_Neg_Beliefs on liquidity hoarding with similar statistical significance. $ ^{29} $

We additionally test whether the weather-driven sentiment’s impact on liquidity hoarding is short-lived or persistent over time. To the extent that the weather-driven sentiment reflects bank managers’ opinions and beliefs unjustified by economic fundamentals, its impact on liquidity hoarding should be short-lived. We empirically test how far in the future the weather-driven sentiment affects liquidity hoarding. In doing so, we consider dependent variables of future liquidity hoarding in the two-quarter ahead, three-quarter ahead, four-quarter ahead, and so on. The result (Appendix S9  $ {}^{30} $) shows that the weather-driven sentiment is not related to liquidity hoarding after two quarters, implying that the IV analysis result is mainly driven by the liquidity hoarding in the immediately following quarter. When we replicate the same analysis with the main sentiment measure (i.e., the baseline OLS regression), we find the association between the negative sentiment and future liquidity hoarding is reduced over time and becomes insignificant after four quarters. The relatively shorter duration of the weather-driven sentiment’s impact is attributable to the identifying assumption of the IV analysis; those managers whose sentiment is swayed by the weather condition are likely to be behaviorally biased, and their decision without much economic rationale is likely to be quickly corrected by a bank’s internal system. Overall, the analysis result

suggests that the impact of beliefs and opinions unjustified by economic fundamentals is present, but it dissipates over time.

## 7. CONCLUSION

We test our hypothesis rooted in the psychology and behavioral finance literature that negative bank management sentiment increases liquidity hoarding. We derive bank managers' sentiment from their annual reports (10-K Forms) unexplained by key macroeconomic variables and local economic conditions. We employ a comprehensive bank liquidity hoarding measure encompassing all the usages and sources of liquidity (Berger et al., 2022). We find that negative bank management sentiment increases bank liquidity hoarding, controlling for a large set of bank-and market-level characteristics. Additional analyses suggest that the findings reflect banks' supply and demand choices as opposed to their borrowers' or depositors' choices. Our findings are also highly robust to an instrumental variable analysis.

The banking literature finds that bank liquidity creation is strongly associated with real economic activity. Our paper shows that negative bank sentiment can shrink the total amount of credit extensions and increase debt financing costs, suggesting potentially detrimental impacts on the economy.

Our findings provide several implications for future research. The sentiment-based mechanism can provide alternative explanations for several puzzling behaviors of banks, such as procyclical lending and “zombie lending.” Another potentially fruitful future research path may be to explore other possible “objects” of banks’ unjustified beliefs and resulting outcomes. Extracting the topics of bank sentiment will be an interesting research agenda as natural language processing (NLP) technology is rapidly growing.

##### Appendix S5: Benchmark liquidity level and excessive liquidity hoarding

In the main regression specification, we follow the literature’s practice of using the level of liquidity held by banks as a variable of banks’ liquidity hoarding. However, a potential issue is that “liquidity hoarding” may more appropriately indicate a bank’s holding of liquidity beyond their expected needs or their benchmark level of liquidity. To address this concern, we perform several additional analyses. First, we add the lagged liquidity hoarding measure as an additional control variable, assuming that the benchmark level of liquidity is proportional to the lagged liquidity hoarding. We estimate this regression model using the system GMM approach (Arellano & Bover, 1995; Blundell & Bond, 1998) to avoid Nickell bias (Nickell, 1981). The table below, column (1) reports that the coefficient on the Unexp_Neg_Beliefs remains positive and statistically significant, consistent with our main regression specification.

#### Appendix S7: Additional information about the instrumental variable analysis

From the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Climate Database, we obtain a broad set of weather information, including cloud coverage, one-hour or six-hour precipitation, air temperature, dew point temperature, wind speed, wind direction, and sea level pressure at the day-hour-weather station level. To minimize the discrepancy between the weather conditions and the time/location of top bank managers, we build a “targeted” weather measure focusing on the location and time (working hours, season, detrended) of top managers’ presence. We select weather conditions only during local working hours 8:00AM – 5:00PM for the working days of a week. Our approach focuses on the local weather conditions near the bank headquarters, where the top managers are likely to make strategic decisions.

For LASSO, we consider 144 seasonally-adjusted weather conditions on weekdays (Tuesday only, Tuesday to Thursday, or Monday to Friday) during working hours with one- or two-quarter lags adjusted by prior one-, two-, or three-year average weather conditions in the same quarter of the year. $ ^{32} $ We choose one- or two-quarter lags of weather conditions based on the assumption that the annual report 10-K is prepared mainly in the last quarter of a fiscal year, and it takes about two or three months for the completed 10-K files to be prepared and updated to the SEC EDGAR system. $ ^{33} $ We create dummies for each of the 144 local weather conditions to account for potential nonlinear relations between sentiment and weather conditions (Gilchrist and Sands, 2016). Specifically, we create cloud coverage dummies in 1 okta bin for each of the cloud coverage variables, where an okta is a measure of cloud coverage ranging from 0 (completely clear sky) to 8 (completely overcast). Similarly, temperature dummies are in 5-degree Celsius bins, sea level pressure dummies are in 5 hectopascals bins, where hectopascals are international units of barometric pressure that are increasing in this pressure, and one-hour or six-hour precipitation are in 20 millimeters bins. The selection of bin width is based on the previous literature (Gilchrist and Sands, 2016) and the computational concern. In total, we include 2,090 dummy variables indicating local weather conditions of bank headquarters in our LASSO selection model.
