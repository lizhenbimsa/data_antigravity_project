## A B S T R A C T

As AI commercialization accelerates, firms are incentivized to engage in “AI washing”—exaggerating AI capabilities to attract capital. We develop an index to measure AI washing, the discrepancy between symbolic AI disclosure and substantive AI investment, and examine the governance effect of ESG-linked executive compensation (ESG compensation). We find that ESG compensation significantly mitigates AI washing, acting as a constraining incentive that curbs symbolic disclosure rather than stimulating substantive investment. The effect is mediated by managerial long-term orientation and employee organizational identification. Additional analyses show that the governance effect of ESG compensation is stronger among firms with higher AI technology dependency, greater media attention, and stronger managerial ability. Moreover, AI washing erodes market value, and firms facing legitimacy loss are more likely to resort to symbolic practices. This study contributes by extending governance theory to the AI context and clarifying the role of ESG compensation in aligning managerial and employee interests.

### 1. Introduction

AI is no longer just a buzzword; it is increasingly seen as a driving force in the market, a key to investor confidence, and a safeguard for a company's future. However, behind the AI revolution lies a dangerous trend: more and more companies are creating an image of technological leadership through symbolic reinforcement and the generalization of narratives in AI information dissemination, while their substantive investment in research and development, hardware and software deployment, or human resources remains relatively limited. $ ^{1} $ This leads to a crisis of trust, eroding public confidence and stifling genuine technological innovation and responsible AI practices, as emphasized by Haddi (2024): “If businesses are serious about harnessing the power of AI, it starts with an honest look in the mirror.” This issue has also caught the attention of global regulatory bodies. For example, in 2024, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) took enforcement action on misleading behavior in AI disclosures and issued risk warnings. $ ^{2} $ Similarly, Chinese regulators have begun to strengthen the regulation and accountability of AI-related information disclosure. $ ^{3} $ Currently, AI narratives are evolving from a “linguistic game” in corporate competition to a trust issue that urgently requires governance.

Delmas and Burbano (2011) were the first to define "washing" as a state of inconsistency where companies excessively promote their performance despite poor environmental outcomes. Building on this logic, this paper extends the concept to the AI context, defining AI washing as the extent of deviation between a company's symbolic disclosure of AI and its substantive investment in AI. Essentially, it is a symbolic communication strategy in which companies enhance AI narratives to

shape a positive technological image, attract public attention, and minimize the perception of technological risks, thereby creating a widely recognized but not entirely truthful “pro-AI” impression at the societal level. $ ^{4} $ In broader washing studies, existing research primarily based on institutional theory, reputation management theory, and signaling theory, has explored the causes and governance paths of various forms of washing, such as greenwashing (Delmas and Burbano 2011), CSR washing (Pope and Waeraas 2016), and diversity washing (Baker et al., 2024). These studies reveal that the formation of washing behaviors is closely related to the imbalance of external institutional constraints and social pressures (Lee and Raschke 2023; Long et al., 2025; Testa et al., 2018; Vera-Munoz 2024). However, these external governance perspectives often overlook the role of internal incentive structures in constraining symbolic behavior and enhancing the authenticity of information. As emerging technologies like AI develop rapidly, the complexity of information has weakened the effectiveness of external oversight. While traditional institutional pressures may prompt companies to create a compliant appearance, they rarely address the internal incentive logic and decision-making motives. Therefore, exploring the role of internal governance tools in addressing AI washing not only helps expand the theoretical boundaries of washing research but also responds to the new governance challenges facing corporate information disclosure in the AI context.

Executive compensation contracts are one of the most direct corporate governance mechanisms affecting management decisions (Flammer et al., 2019). In recent years, with the rise of social responsibility concepts and sustainability issues, executive compensation systems have undergone a transformation, and the inclusion of ESG indicators in executive compensation has become an important topic in both corporate governance research and practice (Cohen et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2025). Compared to traditional governance mechanisms centered on supervision and financial performance, ESG compensation reflects the evolution of corporate governance from a financial focus to a responsibility-oriented approach (Aresu et al., 2023; Flammer et al., 2019; Haque and Ntim 2020). This system embeds responsibility goals into the incentive structure, internalizing external legitimacy and sustainability expectations into the manager's incentive logic (Cohen et al., 2023; Ikram et al., 2023), thereby reshaping managerial decision-making motives and potentially influencing corporate legitimacy management (Cohen et al., 2023). In this framework, legitimacy management refers to the management actions that companies take to gain, maintain, and repair social recognition, with washing being a key manifestation of this. Thus, ESG compensation may impact AI washing at the governance level. This study aims to explore this relationship, contributing to a deeper understanding of the connection between responsibility-oriented incentives and the authenticity of corporate information disclosure.

Existing studies show that incorporating ESG indicators into executive compensation can drive improvements within established ESG goals, such as increased corporate value (Cohen et al., 2023; Flammer et al., 2019; Hou et al., 2025), improved social and environmental performance (Cohen et al., 2023; Flammer et al., 2019), reduced pollution and carbon emissions (Flammer et al., 2019; Haque and Ntim, 2020), and reduced environmental violations and social scandals (Flammer et al., 2019). Other studies have confirmed the contractual external effects of ESG compensation, in which ESG compensation may not directly set performance targets for relevant fields but still shows positive governance extensions in these areas. For example, improvements in innovation quality (Tsang et al., 2021), reductions in earnings management (Cohen et al., 2023), improved credit ratings (Peng and Iii, 2023), and reduced volatility and downside risks (Gaia Soana, 2024).

These findings suggest that ESG compensation, by emphasizing external issues, may better respond to societal responsibility and regulatory expectations, shifting corporate decision-making from symbolic compliance to more substantial responsibility fulfillment. However, this potential governance effect does not always materialize in practice. Some scholars have pointed out that defects in the design and implementation of ESG compensation may prompt companies to revert to a governance model dominated by symbolic legitimacy (Bebchuk and Tallarita, 2022; Gaia Soana, 2024; Homroy et al., 2023). Research has found that in most companies, the weight of ESG indicators in executive compensation is generally low, and disclosures are insufficient. For example, Bebchuk and Tallarita (2022) report that among S&P 100 firms, only 27.4 percent disclose specific weights, and in most cases these weights range from 1.5 to 3 percent. Such limited emphasis renders ESG incentives more akin to a governance illusion than a binding constraint. Moreover, the ambiguity and manipulability of ESG metrics further weaken their incentive effects. Gaia Soana (2024) similarly cautions that the dispersion and vagueness of ESG targets give them greater symbolic significance rather than genuine motivational power.

These observations suggest that ESG compensation has both governance potential and symbolic risk, and its effectiveness depends on whether incentive design can embed responsibility orientation into managerial decision-making and align responsibility fulfillment with performance targets. In this context, this study examines the governance effect of ESG compensation on AI washing and addresses two questions. First, does ESG compensation curb AI washing. Second, through which mechanisms does this effect operate. The findings are expected to broaden washing research and deepen understanding of internal governance mechanisms. This study conducts an empirical analysis of Chinese A-share listed companies. China is among the most active markets for AI application and commercialization worldwide. Policy advocacy and capital market attention have fostered frequent technological narratives around AI, providing a favorable setting to observe decoupling between symbolic disclosure and substantive investment. Meanwhile, evidence on ESG compensation in emerging markets remains limited. China's distinct regulatory framework, information disclosure standards, and stakeholder structure therefore offer a useful setting to examine the applicability and explanatory power of ESG compensation across institutional environments.

This study makes three main contributions. First, in terms of research perspective, it extends firm-level AI research beyond technological investment and performance outcomes to the domains of disclosure authenticity and governance mechanisms. By introducing the theoretical logic of washing research, this study examines AI washing through the consistency between symbolic disclosure and substantive investment and reveals how internal governance tools shape the alignment between firms' responsibility narratives and actions in AI-related issues. The analysis provides a new framework for understanding the formation and constraint of corporate technological narratives and extends washing theory to the AI context, thereby enriching the intersection of AI governance and corporate governance research.

Second, in terms of theoretical development, this study responds to ongoing debates regarding the governance effect of ESG compensation. From a contractual governance perspective, it examines how nonfinancial performance incentives influence symbolic compliance and substantive governance. The findings show an asymmetric recoupling effect in the AI context, whereby ESG compensation constrains AI washing primarily by reducing symbolic disclosure rather than by increasing substantive investment. Further mechanism analysis indicates that ESG compensation operates by enhancing managerial long-term orientation and strengthening employee organizational identification, thereby improving legitimacy management practices related to AI. These results provide new empirical evidence on potential divergences between symbolic compliance and substantive governance.

under ESG compensation arrangements.

Third, in terms of methodology, this study undertakes an early exploration of the quantitative identification of AI washing by constructing an AI washing index that captures the degree of deviation between symbolic AI disclosure and substantive AI investment. This approach offers a feasible path for assessing disclosure-investment consistency in the AI context and provides a replicable measurement tool for identifying decoupling risk and symbolic communication in AI-related disclosures. In addition, this study proposes a new approach to constructing ESG compensation indicators by applying the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model for semantic identification. This method helps address data availability constraints related to ESG compensation in emerging markets and enhances the feasibility of conducting research under data-limited conditions.

### 2. Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

#### 2.1. Managerial decision orientation

Managerial decision orientation is a core element of internal governance and an important antecedent of firms' information disclosure strategies (Deng et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2024). Frederick et al. (2002) show that individuals commonly exhibit hyperbolic discounting in intertemporal choice, favoring immediate gains while undervaluing long-term outcomes. In corporate settings, this tendency makes managers more prone to short-termism in resource allocation and strategic decision making (Graham et al., 2005). External performance evaluation, short-term incentives embedded in compensation structures, and short-term capital market reactions further amplify this behavioral tendency (Aghamolla and Hashimoto, 2023; Hribar et al., 2025; Martin and Kushwaha, 2024). In the AI context, where technological iteration is rapid and information verifiability is limited, managers may therefore respond to market expectations by strengthening AI narratives and engaging in symbolic disclosure (Mikalef et al., 2022; J. Yu et al., 2025), thereby providing a behavioral basis for the emergence of AI washing.

Optimal contracting theory suggests that well-designed compensation contracts can align managerial incentives with firms' long-term interests and constrain short-termism and opportunistic behavior (Flammer et al., 2019; Hou et al., 2025). In the AI context, a defining feature of AI washing lies in the deviation between disclosure and investment. Compared with increasing substantive investment, adjusting disclosure narratives is less costly and can be implemented more quickly, which makes firms more likely to prioritize communication-side responses. Traditional compensation systems, grounded in agency theory, primarily rely on financial performance indicators such as net income and stock prices to mitigate information asymmetry and goal conflicts between owners and managers. However, under traditional compensation schemes, the structural tension between short-term financial maximization and sustainable value creation often remains unresolved. This conflict creates ambiguity that managers may exploit, potentially inducing opportunistic behaviors such as symbolic disclosure (Y. Yu et al., 2024).

Flammer et al. (2019) argue that incorporating non-financial performance into compensation contracts enhances incentive effectiveness and alleviates agency problems. ESG compensation embeds responsibility-oriented metrics into compensation structures, expands the evaluative scope of traditional contracts, and reduces moral hazard and short-term orientation driven by financial indicators through medium- and long-term assessment arrangements (Alhusaini et al., 2023; Kwon et al., 2023; Su et al., 2024; X. Zhang and Li, 2024). From a governance perspective, such arrangements may not immediately increase AI investment, but they can impose constraints on disclosure behavior, encouraging managers to adopt a more cautious approach in external communication and to restrain the expansion of symbolic disclosure, thereby reducing AI washing. Consistent with prior evidence, firms with superior ESG performance exhibit lower incidences of financial fraud and earnings manipulation (Bajaj et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2012; Su et al., 2024). From an incentive compatibility perspective, ESG compensation contractualizes reputation and responsibility objectives, reshapes managers' trade-offs between short-term gains and long-term consequences, and strengthens self-discipline and responsibility awareness. As a result, it is expected to reduce short-termism and enhance disclosure transparency and decision prudence (Deng et al., 2025; J. Zhang et al., 2023). In the uncertain and information-imperfect setting of AI disclosure, tendencies such as narrow decision framing, hyperbolic discounting, and optimism bias may drive managers to pursue short-term reputational returns and intensify strategic disclosure behavior. ESG compensation, through medium- and long-term responsibility assessments and constraining incentives, is therefore likely to exert an effective governance effect on AI washing.

In sum, by incorporating sustainability and responsibility-oriented metrics into incentive systems, ESG compensation has the potential to reshape executives' time preferences and decision logic at the institutional level. Because superior ESG performance depends on sustained effort and long-term improvement, this mechanism guides managers to focus on long-term value and enhances ethical awareness and social responsibility through optimized incentive structures and strengthened responsibility constraints (Seele and Gatti 2017; Vera-Munoz 2024). As a result, firms' responses to social value and technological ethics become more substantive, and the likelihood of AI washing correspondingly declines. Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypotheses.

H1: Compared to firms without ESG compensation, firms that implement ESG compensation exhibit a lower degree of AI washing.

H2a: ESG compensation mitigates a firm’s AI washing by enhancing managerial long-term orientation.

### 2.2. Employee organizational identification

Traditional corporate governance research often treats executive incentives and individual traits as core antecedents of strategic decision-making. This executive-centric perspective may underestimate the governance role of other internal stakeholders in constraining opportunistic behavior. As the most extensive group within firms and the one closest to daily operations, employees shape the foundation of information disclosure authenticity through day-to-day behavior, peer interaction, and internal communication practices. Prior research shows that when firms provide positive feedback to employees in areas such as pay fairness, career development, and respectful management, employees' sense of belonging, moral identification, and organizational citizenship behavior increase significantly (Flammer and Luo, 2017). These changes foster informal checks and balances within organizations and may generate collective moral constraints on AI washing.

In the AI context, AI washing often manifests as reinforced external narratives without corresponding support from internal substantive practices, leading to deviations between disclosure and action. Existing studies suggest that such deviations may also stem from failures in internal communication, particularly information silos between marketing or public relations functions and research and development or operations units. In such cases, external commitments may be amplified without sufficient internal understanding, resulting in unintentional washing (Delmas and Burbano, 2011). Whether firms can establish shared norms, day-to-day constraints, and timely corrective mechanisms across departments therefore becomes central to limiting such deviations, with employee organizational identification playing a key role.

Research indicates that firms' ESG practices shape not only external images but are also internalized by organizational members as part of their social value identification (Peterson, 2004). When firms exhibit strong ESG performance, employees' sense of belonging and self-esteem increase, and these positive emotions motivate them to protect corporate reputation and long-term interests while resisting behaviors that may damage the firm's standing (Turban and Greening, 1997). Building on this foundation, ESG compensation arrangements that incorporate

responsibility indicators such as integrity and fairness into executive incentives further signal the firm's commitment to long-term value and ethical norms to employees (Flammer et al., 2019). This process strengthens responsibility images and value identification and enhances job satisfaction and organizational loyalty. At the same time, a strong ESG reputation serves screening and signaling functions that help attract value-aligned, morally oriented, and long-term oriented talent (Turban and Greening, 1997; L. Zhang and Gowan, 2012), thereby reinforcing an internal culture of integrity and peer norms. When responsibility-oriented incentives are combined with fair governance processes, high-quality psychological contracts between firms and employees are further consolidated (Flammer and Luo, 2017). In environments characterized by strong organizational identification, employees are more likely to engage proactively in voice, cooperation, and supervision as forms of organizational citizenship behavior. These behaviors impose effective soft constraints on AI washing and continuously enhance information disclosure transparency and consistency (W. Li et al., 2022; Scheidler et al., 2019). Fig. 1 illustrates the theoretical logic underlying these arguments.

In conclusion, ESG compensation, as an important tool for corporate social responsibility, not only affects the decision orientation of executives but also transmits through psychological and cultural mechanisms to organizational members, enhancing spontaneous internal supervision and corrective actions. As a result, companies are better able to uphold authenticity and consistency in their information disclosures, reducing symbolic narratives and opportunistic behaviors such as AI washing. Based on this, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2b: ESG compensation mitigates a firm’s AI washing by enhancing employee organizational identification.

### 3. Research design

### 3.1. Sample

In 2013, the Opinions of the State Council on Accelerating the Development of Energy-saving and Environmental Protection Industries first proposed "promoting intelligent and informational transformation," placing intelligent technology and sustainable development on equal footing. This policy agenda provided early institutional support for the parallel development of intelligent transformation and sustainability-oriented practices in China. Since then, the State Council and relevant ministries have successively introduced policies on energy conservation, environmental protection, intelligent manufacturing, and responsible investment, providing an institutional foundation for corporate disclosure and practice in the fields of ESG and AI. Based on this context, this study selects Chinese A-share listed companies from 2013 to 2024 as the research sample. To ensure data reliability, financial firms, ST (Special Treatment) companies, and firms with missing data are excluded, and continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% level at both tails. Financial data are obtained from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database. The identification of ESG compensation and AI disclosure indicators is based on corporate annual reports, ESG reports, and social responsibility reports, which are quantified using natural language processing (NLP) methods.

#### 3.2. Variable

##### 3.2.1. ESG compensation

At present, research on ESG compensation is generally constrained by data availability. Due to limited disclosure information, it is often difficult for researchers to quantify the specific weight of ESG performance indicators in executive compensation. Mainstream studies usually employ a binary variable to identify whether a company has established related performance constraints (Cohen et al., 2023; Gaia Soana, 2024; Z. Li and Yang, 2024). Following this approach, this study uses a dummy variable to measure whether ESG indicators are incorporated into compensation contracts. In the Chinese context, although a dedicated ESG compensation database has not yet been established, the gradual improvement of information disclosure standards provides the foundation for identifying relevant information. Since the implementation of the Guidelines for the Content and Format of Information Disclosure by Companies Offering Securities to the Public No. 2 – Content and Format of Annual Reports (Revised in 2003), the disclosure of executive compensation among listed companies has become increasingly institutionalized. In particular, the 2007 revision further required the disclosure of compensation decision-making processes, determination criteria, and payment details. Although a unified disclosure template for ESG compensation is still lacking, related expressions are typically scattered across the compensation decision and performance evaluation sections of annual reports, making text-based identification feasible. Based on this foundation, this study employs the BERT model to identify the semantic features of ESG compensation within annual reports. As a major breakthrough in the field of NLP (Bochkay et al., 2023), BERT demonstrates significant advantages in semantic understanding and contextual capture, effectively enhancing the precision and consistency of text identification. Leveraging BERT's semantic parsing capability, this study extracts ESG compensation-related information from complex annual report texts, thereby assessing the adoption of ESG compensation among firms at a large-sample level.

Operationally, the basic procedure for identifying ESG compensation using BERT in this study involves three stages: pre-training, fine-tuning, and output (Bochkay et al., 2023; D. Chen et al., 2024; de Kok, 2025). In the pre-training stage, BERT learns the basic structure and semantic features of a language. This stage has already been completed by Google, so researchers do not need to repeat it. To better adapt to the Chinese context, this study uses a Chinese-specific BERT model developed by the Harbin Institute of Technology (HIT), and all operational steps are executed in a Python 3.10 environment.

In the fine-tuning stage, the BERT model learns to map input data (text) to output data (class labels). This stage first requires randomly sampling from the text corpus and manually annotating a small portion of the text. This manually annotated data is then proportionally divided into a training set, a validation set, and a test set (Bochkay et al., 2023). We use annual reports from 2013 to 2024 and segment the entire text.

Considering that most sentences in the corpus are unrelated to compensation, a purely random sampling approach would yield a majority of irrelevant labels. To improve the efficiency of manual reading and prevent context from biasing human judgment, we first construct a compensation keyword dictionary to pre-screen sentences from the corpus. To ensure the dictionary is comprehensive, we built it by manually reading policy documents and conventional descriptions of compensation in annual reports. We then used Word2Vec and FastText algorithms to perform a comprehensive similar-word expansion across the entire corpus, ensuring the constructed dictionary covers all compensation-related keywords. The resulting dictionary contains 302 keywords, which were reduced to 169 unique words after removing compound words (available upon request). This dictionary was then used to pre-screen the text corpus, ultimately yielding a set of sentences to be predicted that are potentially relevant to compensation policy.

Next, we randomly sampled 20,000 sentences for manual annotation. To standardize the criteria for judging the semantics of ESG compensation among researchers, we adopted the framework outlined in Measuring Stakeholder Capitalism: Toward Common Metrics and Consistent Reporting of Sustainable Value Creation, a report initiated by the World Economic Forum (WEF). This report, based on internationally recognized standards, provides a common set of ESG metrics and reporting standards. We used this report to provide uniform training for our researchers, with a special focus on explaining and demonstrating easily confused labels. A strict rule was established: a sentence was marked as a positive instance only if it explicitly linked executive compensation to ESG indicators (e.g., "In accordance with the group's energy conservation and emission reduction management regulations, environmental performance is one of the appraisal indicators for company executives, and energy-saving work is incorporated into the operational performance evaluation system for heads of all units as a constraining indicator"; "Continuously improve the ecological and environmental protection management responsibility system, incorporate environmental indicators into the performance appraisal system for managers, strictly implement job responsibilities and standards in the production process, and intensify accountability and responsibility enforcement for environmental issues"; "The Group links the compensation performance of managers at all levels (from the chairman to frontline managers) with quality performance, adding product quality as a key performance indicator in the annual appraisal standards". Sentences that only mentioned ESG content without involving executive pay, or only described executive pay without referencing clear ESG indicators, were not classified as positive instances. To mitigate the arbitrariness of manual annotation, following the recommendation of D. Chen et al. (2024), three researchers independently labeled the sentences. A sentence was marked as a positive instance only if all three researchers unanimously agreed that it clearly reflected a link between the company's executive compensation policy and its ESG performance; otherwise, it was marked as a negative instance.

Subsequently, the manually annotated sentences were divided into a training set, a validation set, and a test set according to the conventional 80%, 10%, and 10% ratio. The training set was then used to train the BERT model. During training, the model uses a loss function to measure the difference between predicted and true class labels, and its parameters are updated based on the gradient from backpropagation to optimize this loss function. Next, the trained BERT model was evaluated on the test set. Unseen text data was fed into the model, which outputs a vector c that comprehensively reflects the overall semantic information of the input text. This vector c was then processed by a linear classification layer to calculate scores for different classes,  $ s_0 $ and  $ s_1 $. The softmax function was used to convert these scores into probabilities. For a binary classification problem, the probability of a text belonging to class  $ P_1 $ is:  $ P_1 = \frac{e^{s_1}}{e^{s_0} + e^{s_1}} $. By observing the model's performance on the validation set, we could assess its generalization ability and prediction accuracy on unseen data. We repeated the training and testing process 10 times, iteratively fine-tuning the steps, adjusting training parameters, and identifying and correcting misclassifications to optimize model performance. We periodically validated the model on the validation set until it demonstrated excellent performance across all metrics. Consistent with prior literature (D. Chen et al., 2024), we compute four validation metrics to evaluate the classification performance of the deep learning model, including accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score. All four metrics exceed 0.90, indicating reliable sentence-level identification performance (overall model performance and test-set results are available upon request). We then apply the trained model to the full executive-compensation text corpus, and all sentences predicted as positive instances undergo an additional manual review. The sentence-level predictions are subsequently aggregated to the firm-year level. Specifically, ESG_pay is coded as 1 if at least one sentence in a firm's annual report for a given year explicitly links executive compensation to ESG indicators, and 0 otherwise.

##### 3.2.2. AI washing

Existing research commonly uses the speech-action gap method to measure washing phenomena (Khan et al., 2024; Lee and Raschke, 2023; Long et al., 2025; Walker and Wan, 2012), with its determination logic based on the classic framework proposed by Delmas and Burbano (2011). This study follows this approach, defining AI washing as the extent of deviation between a company's symbolic disclosure of AI and its substantive investment in AI, that is, the intersection of strong symbolic communication and weak substantive action. The logic behind the determination that disclosure exceeds investment implies that the symbolic expression of AI by the company exceeds the support of actual actions. For example, companies frequently use narratives such as "big data," "large language models (LLM)," "AI-driven," and "intelligent transformation" in public disclosure reports, press releases, or capital market communications, presenting a high AI discourse density (Khan et al., 2024; Lee and Raschke, 2023), while their substantive investment in R&D, hardware and software deployment, or human resources are relatively limited.

In terms of measurement, this study constructs two indicators, AI symbolic disclosure and AI substantive investment, to represent the company's performance in the "speech" and "action" dimensions. The difference between these two values forms the AI washing index. It is worth mentioning that, while this approach conceptually aligns with the classic definition of washing, in reality, the disclosure exceeds investment situation may arise from various managerial motives, including forward-looking communication and strategic positioning, as well as opportunistic information manipulation. However, regardless of the motive, companies typically exhibit language reinforcement characteristics in their external communication, seeking external responses in terms of resources, identity, or legitimacy through symbolic expressions. When the intensity of such symbolic expressions exceeds the range that substantive actions can support, it manifests as washing. Therefore, this study follows mainstream research paradigms and measures washing from a behavioral outcome perspective, rather than identifying it from the motivation level.

In terms of disclosure, since symbolic disclosure typically manifests as reinforcement and visibility at the linguistic level (Delmas and Burbano, 2011), the frequency of AI terms in annual report texts reflects the extent to which a company emphasizes AI discourse and the density of symbolic expressions in its external communication, thereby indicating the level of symbolic disclosure on AI topics. Therefore, this study conducts keyword matching and frequency statistics on the annual reports of listed companies from 2013 to 2024 to measure the level of AI symbolic disclosure. Considering the continuous development of artificial intelligence technology, the semantic connotation of AI in corporate texts has also undergone some evolution. Early on, it was used to describe corporate intelligent or digital transformation (e.g., machine learning, deep learning, intelligent control, digital transformation, etc.),

while in recent years, it has gradually expanded to generative models and broader intelligent application scenarios (e.g., generative AI, generative models, LLM, intelligent decision-making, AI innovation, etc.). In light of this semantic change, this study constructs a comprehensive AI terminology dictionary that covers the evolution of AI technology from symbolism to connectionism. Specifically, based on several data sources, including the bilingual AI terminology database provided by the Chinese Software Developer Network (CSDN, with 2,442 terms), the China AI Industry Research Report (V) published by iResearch, the China and Global Corporate AI Application Survey by Deloitte, and the AI vocabulary list released by the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the study initially collects relevant Chinese and English professional terms related to AI and screens them according to the following principles: (1) terms unrelated to listed companies' AI application scenarios are excluded; (2) redundant or repetitive Chinese terms due to compound words or multiple translations are removed. The final AI terminology dictionary contains 585 keywords (available upon request). Word frequency statistics are then conducted using Python 3.10. Furthermore, considering the differences in importance between individual keywords, weights are assigned to them based on their frequency of occurrence, and the original word frequency is adjusted accordingly. The specific processing logic is as follows:

 $$ w_{k,i}=\left\{\begin{aligned}\frac{1+\log(fr_{k,i})}{1+\log(word_{i})}\times log\frac{N}{N_{k}},&fr_{k,i}>0\\ 0,&fr_{k,i}=0\end{aligned}\right. $$ 

where  $ w_k $ is the weight of keyword  $ k $ in the dictionary,  $ N $ is the total number of annual reports in the corpus,  $ N_k $ is the number of annual reports in the corpus containing keyword  $ k $,  $ fr_{k,i} $ is the raw frequency of keyword  $ k $ in the text of firm  $ i $, and word $ _i $ is the total length of the text for firm  $ i $. Finally, we calculate the adjusted word frequency for firm  $ i $ in year  $ t $, denoted as  $ frw_{i,t} $, and divide it by the total text length of firm  $ i $ in year  $ t $ to obtain the company’s AI disclosure intensity ( $ AI\_disclosure $). A higher value indicates richer disclosure of AI-related information.

In terms of investment measurement, this study uses artificial intelligence investment data obtained from the CSMAR database, which captures AI-related capitalized expenditures reported in firms' financial statements. Under prevailing accounting standards, expenditures are recognized as assets only when capitalization criteria are met, and such investments are recorded as intangible or fixed assets. By contrast, expenditures that are expensed are not separately disclosed by application or technological category. Consistent with this reporting framework, the AI investment measure in this study is based on capitalized expenditures, which can be systematically observed and compared across firms. This approach reflects firms' resource allocation to AI-related activities as reported under existing accounting practices. To account for differences across industries in capitalization practices and investment structures, we further apply an industry-year mean adjustment to the AI investment measure by subtracting the annual industry mean from each firm-level observation. The resulting variable is denoted as AI_invest.

Finally, based on the above measurements of AI_disclosure and AI_invest, and following the approach of Khan et al. (2024) in handling the scale effects of different variables, the AI washing index is calculated as follows:

 $$ \mathrm{AIW}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}=\frac{\mathrm{AI\_disclosure}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}-\mathrm{MeAI\_disclosure}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}}{\mathrm{SdAI\_disclosure}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}}-\frac{\mathrm{AI\_invest}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}-\mathrm{MeAI\_invest}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}}{\mathrm{SdAI\_invest}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}}} $$ 

##### 3.2.3. Controls

Following prior literature, this study controls for a series of additional factors that may influence the dependent variable. At the firm-level characteristics, R&D expenditure (Rd) and capital expenditure (Cap) are first controlled to reflect differences in firms' innovation and investment capabilities, thereby excluding the influence of overall resource endowment on corporate AI capabilities. Second, total executive compensation (Salary) is controlled to ensure that the incentive effect mainly arises from the design of the compensation structure rather than differences in total pay. Next, firm size (Size), financial leverage (Lev), profitability (Roa), and growth ability (Sgth) are included to account for the impact of basic financial conditions on AI disclosure. At the corporate governance level, several governance variables are introduced, including CEO duality (Dual), institutional ownership (Institution), board diversity (Diversity), board size (Board), proportion of independent directors (Indep), managerial ownership (Mshare), and Big Four auditing (Big4), to control for the effects of internal and external governance mechanisms. The definitions and descriptions of all variables are presented in Table A1.

#### 3.3. Baseline model

We construct the following baseline regression model:

 $$ A I W_{i,t+1}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}E S G_{-}p a y_{i,t}+\beta_{2}C o n t r o l s+\alpha_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t} $$ 

In model (3), i denotes the firm and t denotes the year. The dependent variable  $ AIW_{i,t+1} $ measures the AI washing index of firm i in year  $ t+1 $. The key explanatory variable,  $ ESG_{payi,t} $, is a dummy variable equal to 1 if firm i adopts ESG executive compensation in year t, and 0 otherwise. Controls is a vector of control variables;  $ \alpha_i $ and  $ \tau_t $ represent firm and year fixed effects, respectively;  $ \varepsilon_{i,t} $ is the idiosyncratic error term. All coefficients are denoted by  $ \beta_j $, and  $ \beta_0 $ is the constant term. To account for potential serial correlation within firms over time, we use robust standard errors clustered at the firm level.

#### 4.2. Baseline analysis

Table 2 reports the baseline results for ESG_pay and AIW. Columns (1) to (3) sequentially add year and firm fixed effects and then the full set of control variables. By comparison, the standardized effects of the proportion of independent directors and board size are about  $ -0.015 (-0.003 \times 5.296 / 1.088 \approx -0.015) $ and  $ -0.021 (-0.134 \times 0.174 / 1.088 \approx -0.021) $, respectively. The effect of  $ ESG_{pay} $ is therefore around 60 percent of that of board independence and about 40 percent of that of board size. Columns (4) and (5) show no significant effect on AI_invest but a significantly negative effect on AI_disclosure, indicating that ESG compensation primarily constrains excessive disclosure and is more consistent with a constraining contract effect.

#### 4.3. Robustness test

##### 4.3.1. Instrumental variable (IV)

To assess the robustness of the baseline results in the presence of potential endogeneity concerns, this study further adopts an instrumental variable approach for causal identification. Specifically, firms with more transparent disclosure practices and stronger internal governance are more likely to adopt ESG compensation arrangements. From a reputation capital perspective, such adoption may also serve primarily to reinforce existing reputation signals, thereby giving rise to reverse causality and potentially leading to an upward bias in the estimated governance effect of ESG compensation. Based on these considerations, we construct exogenous instrumental variables and employ two-stage least squares for identification and inference.

Our first instrument is historical openness, measured by whether a city was forcibly opened as a commercial port or concession during 1842 to 1949. The relevance of this measure follows the view that both the ESG concept and performance based compensation in China were introduced and diffused alongside Western governance practices. Regions that opened earlier were more likely to have been exposed sooner to Western institutions and governance ideas and, over time, to have developed higher levels of marketization and openness. These historical legacies may be associated with a greater propensity for local firms to incorporate ESG elements into compensation arrangements. Regarding exogeneity, historical openness is a pre-AI historical shock whose influence is more plausibly reflected in broad regional characteristics such as marketization and industrial structure, rather than in firms' contemporaneous disclosure incentives or ethical bias. Although historical openness could be correlated with regional economic vitality or talent concentration and thus with the overall level of AI development, AI washing captures the deviation between disclosure and investment and is more closely related to business ethics and organizational integrity. This suggests that any indirect effect of historical openness on AI washing is likely to be limited.

Our second instrument is regional cost of living. This choice is motivated by evidence that high living costs in China reduce the real purchasing power of executive compensation, which can encourage boards to enhance retention by increasing base pay or adopting more diversified incentives (Hao et al., 2024). In this context, firms may be more inclined to incorporate ESG metrics into compensation schemes as a complement to existing incentive structures. In terms of exogeneity, regional cost of living is less likely to have a direct link to the deviation between AI disclosure and AI investment. The core of AI washing is the inconsistency between disclosure and investment, which reflects a governance challenge related to business ethics and organizational integrity rather than local consumption levels per se. While cost of living may be correlated with broader urban attributes that affect the overall level of AI activity, such factors would typically shift AI disclosure and AI investment in similar directions, implying a stronger association with their absolute levels than with their wedge.

In terms of variable construction, historical openness is defined as a dummy variable that equals one if the city experienced such opening and zero otherwise. We interact this dummy variable with a linear time trend to construct the historical instrument (Ho). Regional cost of living, proxied by the local minimum wage standard (Lc), with data obtained from Chinese Research Data Services Platform (CNRDS). To alleviate reverse causality and ensure temporal ordering, all instrumental variables are measured using lagged values at t minus one. In addition, to reduce potential interference from omitted time-varying regional factors in the identification channel, we include a set of city-year level regional control variables in the 2SLS regressions to capture dynamic regional heterogeneity. Specifically, we control for economic prosperity measured by GDP per capita (Gdp), industrial structure measured by the share of the tertiary sector (Tertiary), urban development measured by the urbanization rate (Urban), openness measured by the number of foreign-invested firms (Fdi firms) and their industrial output (Fdi_output), human capital measured by the number of college students per ten thousand people (People), regional innovation capacity measured by research expenditure (Research_exp), number of research staff (Research_staff), and number of invention patents granted (Patent), industrial scale and technological intensity measured by industrial electricity consumption (Electricity), government innovation policy support measured by local government science expenditure (Gs), and digital infrastructure measured by the number of mobile phone users (Mu) and number of internet users (Iu).

Table 3 reports the diagnostic tests. The Anderson canonical correlation LM statistic and the Kleibergen Paap rk LM statistic are both significant at the one percent level, indicating no under-identification. The Cragg Donald Wald F statistic and the Kleibergen Paap rk Wald F statistic both exceed ten, suggesting that weak instrument concerns are limited. The Sargan and Hansen J tests yield p-values of 0.385 and 0.322, respectively, so the null hypothesis of joint exogeneity cannot be rejected. In the first stage, Ho and Lc load positively and significantly on ESG_pay. In the second stage, ESG_pay remains significantly negative, consistent with the baseline results, indicating that ESG compensation continues to reduce AI washing after accounting for potential endogeneity.

#### 4.3.2. Considering potential alternative explanations

This study further considers two types of potential alternative explanations. The first concerns identification-level alternative explanations. Although the baseline model controls total executive cash compensation, it may still omit long-term equity incentives such as restricted stock and options. If such incentives include both financial and ESG performance targets, their implicit ESG weight may affect AI disclosure decisions. When equity incentives and cash compensation share the same ESG orientation, the governance effect may be overstated. Otherwise, it may be understated. Given the difficulty of disentangling the financial and ESG weights embedded in equity incentives, we exclude firms with equity incentives during the sample period and reestimate the regressions. In addition, if ESG compensation plans coincide with top executive turnover, the observed governance effect may partly reflect personnel changes. We therefore further exclude firms that experienced chairman or CEO turnover during the sample period. As shown in columns (1) to (4) of Table 4, the core results remain unchanged.

The second type concerns mechanism-level alternative explanations. Prior research suggests that AI technologies may trigger employees' job security concerns, which in turn increases firms' reputational risk and public scrutiny (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2022). From this perspective, firms that place greater emphasis on social-dimension targets in ESG compensation may lead managers to pay closer attention to employee welfare, working conditions, and fairness. They may therefore adopt a more restrained external communication strategy on AI topics involving potential job displacement, thereby reducing disclosure intensity. If this reputational mechanism plays a more important explanatory role in the overall results, it could provide an alternative explanation for our main findings. To test whether this alternative explanation dominates, we further decompose ESG pay into three contract components, E_pay, S_pay, and G_pay, and conduct additional tests. $ ^{5} $ Since this reputational explanation mainly reflects disclosure avoidance motives related to the social dimension, it implies that the effect should be relatively more concentrated in the social dimension if it is the primary mechanism. As reported in columns (5) to (8) of Table 4, the conclusions remain consistent across specifications that include each single-dimension variable separately and the specification that includes all three simultaneously. The coefficient on E_pay is negative but not statistically significant at conventional levels. Both S_pay and G_pay are significantly negative, with a larger magnitude for G_pay. These results indicate that social-dimension motives may operate to some extent, but the overall effect is not concentrated in the social dimension. The reputational explanation is therefore unlikely to be the sole explanation for our main findings. Given the available evidence, the governance explanation is more consistent with the overall pattern of results.

##### 4.3.3. Heckman two-stage test

To examine whether the baseline results are affected by sample selection bias, this study further applies the Heckman two-stage model as a robustness test. Given that not all firms have the opportunity or willingness to adopt ESG compensation, and that the adoption of such schemes may be associated with characteristics such as larger firm size, well-developed governance structures, stronger profitability, and strategic foresight, these characteristics could themselves influence a firm's AI disclosure strategy. This may lead to a self-selection bias in the empirical results. To address this, this study employs the Heckman two-stage model to correct for the self-selection problem. In the first stage, we use a Probit model with all control variables to predict the probability of a firm adopting ESG compensation, and from this, we calculate the Inverse Mills Ratio (IMR). In the second stage, the IMR is included as a control variable in the regression equation to correct for the estimation bias arising from non-random sample selection. The regression results are shown in Table 5. The sign and significance of the key variable's coefficient do not change substantially.

#### 4.3.4. Using LLM for semantic filtering

To reduce semantic noise in the measurement of symbolic AI disclosure, this study follows the call of de Kok (2025) and introduces LLM for sentence-level semantic classification. The specific procedure is as follows: First, the annual report text is split by sentence, and all sentences without AI keywords are removed. Next, the DeepSeek-V3 model is used for batch annotation of the remaining sentences. Following the procedure proposed by de Kok (2025), prompt words are set to obtain JSON format annotation results, which are then parsed and manually reviewed to ensure no incorrect classification of sentences marked as positive examples (specific prompt words can be obtained from the author). Based on this, the proportion of sentences marked as positive examples in each company's annual report is calculated, yielding the semantically filtered disclosure indicator AI_disclosure_LLM, which is then used to reconstruct the AI washing index (AIW_LLM) and substitute the original index for regression analysis. The regression results in Column (1) of Table 6 show that after semantic filtering with LLM, the direction and significance of the coefficient for the core independent variable ESG_pay remain substantially unchanged.

#### 4.3.5. Propensity score matched sample

This study applies propensity score matching to control for systematic differences in observable characteristics between firms that adopt ESG compensation and those that do not. The basic idea is to match each firm in the treatment group (those that adopt ESG compensation) with a firm in the control group (those that do not) that has the most similar characteristics, so that the two groups achieve statistical balance on observable characteristics. After matching, the Average Treatment Effect is estimated only on this structurally similar subsample, thereby mitigating the confounding effects of covariate differences. In terms of implementation, we use 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement and use all the control variables from the baseline regression in the PSM matching process. The results of the balancing test are shown in Table A2. After matching, besides the key independent variable, the balance between the two groups on all control variables is significantly improved, and the bias is substantially eliminated. As shown in Column (2) of Table 6, the coefficient of ESG_pay remains significant.

#### 4.3.6. Entropy balancing

We further introduce Entropy Balancing for a robustness test. Unlike PSM, which requires first estimating the treatment probability, Entropy Balancing directly assigns optimized weights to the control group samples to align the distributions of all observable covariates with those of the treatment group. These weights are obtained by minimizing the entropy distance, thus avoiding the need to discard any samples. In our procedure, we set balancing constraints using all the control variables.

#### 4.3.7. Considering high-dimensional fixed effects

To examine whether the baseline results are influenced by time-varying shocks at the regional or industry level, this study further introduces high-dimensional fixed effects as a robustness test. The baseline model includes year and firm fixed effects to control for common shocks across periods and static heterogeneity in unobservable firm characteristics. However, firms may also be affected by certain local time-varying factors. For example, a province may introduce specific support policies for the AI industry in certain years, or an industry may face significant regulatory adjustments in a particular year. To control for these effects, this study further introduces regional and industry fixed effects, as well as year × region and year × industry interaction fixed effects. The regression results in Column (4) of Table 6 show that the core independent variable remains unchanged in essence.

### 5. Mechanism analysis

In the previous theoretical analysis, this study summarized the mechanisms through which ESG compensation affects AI washing into two categories: one is by constraining management's short-term behavior, guiding them to focus on long-term responsibility and reduce speculative tendencies; the other is by improving employee welfare expectations and enhancing organizational identification, thus forming soft constraints internally. To test these mechanisms, this study follows the classic approach used in prior research (Baron and Kenny, 1986; Bhatia and Kumar, 2022; Blanco et al., 2013) and constructs two sets of extended models based on the baseline model (Model 3) to examine the mediating roles of managerial long-term orientation and employee organizational identification:

 $$ MV_{i,t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}ESG_{-}pay_{i,t}+\beta_{2}Controls+\alpha_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t} $$ 

 $$ A I W_{i,t+1}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}E S G_{-}p a y_{i,t}+\beta_{2}M V_{i,t}+\beta_{3}C o n t r o l s+\alpha_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t} $$ 

where MV represents the mediating variables to be tested, including management's long-term decision orientation (Long) and employee organizational identification (Eoi). Model (4) examines the impact of ESG compensation on the mediating variables, and Model (5) tests its mediating role in the relationship between ESG compensation and AI washing, controlling for the mediating variables.

### 6. Further analysis

### 6.1. When the governance effect of ESG compensation is more effective

The preceding analysis examines the governance effect of ESG compensation on AI washing and its underlying mechanisms. Building on this discussion, this section introduces a legitimacy theory perspective and situates ESG compensation within a broader process of legitimacy management, with a focus on contextual contingencies. Suchman (1995) argues that when new organizational practices remain at the periphery of an existing institutional order, they are more likely to be regarded as secondary activities and may gain legitimacy through symbolic packaging and limited integration. Only when such practices are incorporated into a taken-for-granted order, or embedded in formal structures and procedures, are they more likely to enter the organization's technical core and become an important source of legitimacy. Legitimacy is a relational evaluation between organizations and heterogeneous audiences rather than a static organizational attribute. In contexts with highly differentiated audiences, organizations face continuous and shifting scrutiny. The strategic legitimacy perspective further suggests that managers can actively manage legitimacy by allocating limited legitimacy resources across audiences, monitoring the external cultural environment, and incorporating external signals into decision-making processes, thereby anticipating challenges and choosing between symbolic responses and substantive adjustments.

From this perspective, firms may differ in the centrality of AI to organizational legitimacy, the visibility of legitimacy evaluations surrounding AI disclosure, and the capacity to translate symbolic commitments into substantive responses. Accordingly, the governance effect of ESG compensation on AI washing is likely to vary across contexts.

#### 6.1.1. Technological dependency

AI technology dependency reflects the extent to which artificial intelligence is embedded in firms' value creation processes and thus captures the centrality of AI to organizational legitimacy. As issue centrality increases, firms can potentially obtain greater symbolic legitimacy by strengthening AI narratives. However, legitimacy research suggests that practices confined to formal structures may buffer external pressures (Bromley and Powell 2012), whereas such ceremonial arrangements become less effective once practices penetrate the technical core of the organization.

For firms with high AI technology dependency, AI is no longer a peripheral discourse but an integral component of value creation. In this context, misalignment between AI disclosure and investment is more likely to be interpreted as a deviation from core legitimacy. AI washing therefore becomes more identifiable, more closely linked to the interests of key stakeholders, and associated with higher reputational and institutional costs. Under this risk structure, ESG compensation as a responsibility oriented incentive contract internalizes AI related legitimacy requirements into compensation and performance constraints. This increases the expected cost of adopting purely symbolic disclosure strategies and strengthens the constraint on AI washing. Accordingly, we expect the governance effect of ESG compensation on AI washing to be stronger in firms with higher AI technology dependency.

##### 6.1.2. Media attention

Legitimacy is continuously evaluated by external audiences in terms of whether organizational behavior is perceived as appropriate. Media coverage plays a central role in this process by reflecting the views of investors, regulators, and the public, while also reshaping legitimacy meanings through reporting frequency, tone, and framing. Suchman (1995) notes that once organizations actively engage in legitimacy management, they tend to attract additional attention, which is often accompanied by stricter and more critical scrutiny. As visibility increases, new critics are more likely to emerge and amplify controversies.

to mobilize their own audiences.

When legitimacy has a strong cognitive component, even supportive attention can disrupt taken-for-granted practices and subject them to renewed evaluation. Media attention therefore captures the visibility of legitimacy assessments. Higher attention makes legitimacy judgments more likely to be amplified in public debate and harder to sustain in an unquestioned state (H. Chen et al., 2023). The constraining effect of ESG compensation depends in part on whether AI washing can be externally identified and generate reputational or institutional consequences. When such risks are limited, the governance effect may be weak. In contrast, under high media attention, AI narratives are more frequently exposed to public scrutiny, and deviations between disclosure and investment are more easily framed as legitimacy problems. This raises the expected institutional costs of AI washing and strengthens the governance effect of ESG compensation.

#### 6.1.3. Managerial ability

Legitimacy theory further emphasizes that legitimacy can be viewed as a cultural resource that organizations actively manage. Managers are assumed to have some control over legitimacy processes and to allocate legitimacy resources across audiences. Organizations are not passive recipients of external evaluations but actively manage legitimacy through managerial perception, interpretation, and response (Suchman, 1995). In discussing perceptual change, Suchman (1995) notes that decision makers often engage in self justification and underestimate emerging problems. Effective legitimacy management therefore requires managers to continuously monitor cultural environments, absorb external signals into decision processes, identify audience reactions, and anticipate challenges before issues escalate into crises. Otherwise, firms may be forced into reactive responses that amplify legitimacy risks.

From this perspective, differences in managerial ability to recognize legitimacy signals, understand alternative legitimacy forms, and implement substantive adjustments rather than symbolic commitments constitute an important source of heterogeneity in legitimacy management capacity. Consistent with this view, prior studies show that high ability managers are better able to identify and implement high value ESG projects, optimize resource allocation, and promote long term value creation (Welch and Yoon, 2023). They are also more effective in implementing policies, reducing moral hazard under complex regulatory environments (Curi and Lozano Vivas, 2020; Doukas and Zhang, 2020), and strengthening incentive contract constraints through improved internal control and information transmission mechanisms (Gan, 2019). Accordingly, we expect the governance effect of ESG compensation on AI washing to be stronger in firms with higher managerial ability.

Media attention is measured following He et al. (2024) as the natural logarithm of one plus the frequency of firm mentions in online news coverage and denoted as Attention. Managerial ability is calculated following Demerjian et al. (2012) using a combination of data envelopment analysis and a Tobit model and denoted as Ability. Columns (2) and (3) show similar patterns, with both ESG_pay × Attention and ESG_pay × Ability being significantly negative. These results indicate that higher media attention and stronger managerial ability also reinforce the governance effect of ESG compensation, consistent with the theoretical expectations. In the current technological context, AI narratives have gradually become a new language for firms to build legitimacy. As AI is seen as a symbol of innovation and the frontier, firms may use reinforced AI-related narratives as a key tool to repair reputation and reshape legitimacy. Based on this, the study further hypothesizes that AI washing is no longer merely an opportunistic strategy, but may become a symbolic repair mechanism for firms to rebuild external trust and reputation capital when their legitimacy is damaged. In other words, when firms face compliance risks or reputation crises, their reliance on AI washing may increase to offset the negative expectations about their legitimacy by emphasizing a cutting-edge technological image.

The study summarizes major violation announcements within the reporting period as a proxy for legitimacy damage, including inflated profits and assets, significant information omission, fraudulent listing, illegal funding, unauthorized use of funds, insider trading, price manipulation, improper accounting, and tax violations. Overall, the results support the theoretical inference that legitimacy damage strengthens firms' motivation to repair legitimacy through AI washing.

### 7. Conclusion and discussion

The rapid commercialization of artificial intelligence has intensified firms' exposure to AI washing. In an environment characterized by relatively limited external constraints, rising technological legitimacy pressure, and increasing information asymmetry, the question of how to mitigate the decoupling between disclosure and substantive investment through internal incentive mechanisms and enhance the credibility of AI disclosure has become a central issue in corporate governance. Drawing on the decoupling perspective in new institutional theory, optimal contracting theory, and stakeholder theory, this study examines how ESG compensation, as an internal governance tool, affects AI washing and through which mechanisms. The main findings are summarized as follows. First, ESG compensation mitigates firms' AI washing. This governance effect operates primarily by constraining symbolic disclosure rather than directly stimulating substantive technological investment. This finding suggests that the core role of ESG compensation lies in curbing opportunistic disclosure behavior rather than promoting technology investment per se. Mechanism analyses provide supportive evidence for several governance related pathways. Specifically, firms that link executive compensation to ESG metrics exhibit a more pronounced long-term orientation and, at the firm level, are associated with higher employee organizational identification. Correspondingly, these characteristics are further associated with lower levels of AI washing. Moderation analyses further indicate that this governance effect is stronger in firms with higher AI technology dependency, greater media attention, and stronger managerial ability. Additional analyses show that AI washing significantly damages firm market value and that firms facing legitimacy damage are more likely to rely on AI narratives to repair negative external expectations.

### 7.1. Theoretical implications

This study offers two theoretical implications. First, it reveals the contractual institutional function of ESG compensation in the realm of non-financial information governance. Traditional optimal contracting theory primarily focuses on alleviating agency conflicts driven by financial performance (Bebchuk and Tallarita, 2022; Cohen et al., 2023; Flammer et al., 2019; Gaia Soana, 2024; Homroy et al., 2023), while paying less attention to managerial biases in symbolic and legitimacy-driven behaviors. The empirical evidence in this study suggests that embedding ESG indicators into executive compensation contracts may strengthen internal incentive constraints and influence firms' long-term decision orientation, in ways that are consistent with organizational-level features related to employee organizational identification, thereby constraining symbolic disclosure and information distortion at the firm level. This mechanism not only highlights the distinctive role of non-financial performance incentives in governing symbolic decoupling, but also extends the theoretical scope of executive compensation research by offering a new analytical perspective on how internal incentives operate in responsibility governance. Building on this insight, the study further argues that ESG compensation is not merely an incentive tool, but a responsibility internalization mechanism. Its institutional design makes it more likely that external social expectations and legitimacy pressures are incorporated into firms' decision-making processes and internal constraint frameworks, and that managerial and organizational behavior is shaped through governance arrangements. By revealing the inverse relationship between ESG compensation and symbolic behavior, this study addresses existing concerns about the "symbolic" characteristics of ESG compensation (Bebchuk and Tallarita, 2022; Gaia Soana, 2024; Homroy et al., 2023), establishing the substantial role of ESG compensation in governing information disclosure authenticity and constraining managerial behavior, thereby strengthening its theoretical foundation and institutional legitimacy as a tool for responsibility governance.

Second, this study expands the scope of washing analysis from external legitimacy pressure to internal governance mechanisms and places it in the emerging context of technological legitimacy, advancing the conceptual boundaries and applicability of corporate washing theory. Existing washing research primarily focuses on symbolic communication by firms regarding environmental, social responsibility, or diversity issues (Baker et al., 2024; Delmas and Burbano, 2011; Duchin et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2025; Walker and Wan, 2012), emphasizing how external institutional pressures drive firms to maintain legitimacy through positive rhetoric (Lee and Raschke, 2023; Long et al., 2025; Testa et al., 2018; Vera-Muñoz, 2024). However, this external constraint perspective has limited focus on internal governance mechanisms and fails to fully explain the evolution of legitimacy construction in the context of emerging technologies. As AI and other cutting-edge technologies become widely applied, the logic of legitimacy construction in firms is changing. Based on this, this study explores AI washing, extending the theoretical scope of washing research and suggesting that symbolic compliance is no longer confined to the moral or social dimensions, but extends into legitimacy fields centered around innovation discourse and technological legitimacy. This study also expands washing theory from an external constraint logic to an internal governance perspective, showing that a firm's symbolic behavior arises not only from external supervision and social pressure but also from internal incentive structures, managerial cognition, and organizational responsibility mechanisms. This perspective highlights the crucial role of internal governance in shaping legitimacy strategies, filling a gap in existing research's attention to organizational endogenous governance mechanisms.

### 7.2. Practical and policy implications

This study offers several practical insights. First, the findings support

an interpretation that ESG compensation may help internalize responsibility by transforming external legitimacy pressure into internal incentive constraints, thereby enhancing the credibility and consistency of AI disclosure. This implies that governing corporate washing does not rely solely on external supervision and sanctions, but can also be achieved through responsibility-oriented incentive design that creates endogenous constraints. As a typical responsibility incentive tool, the core value of ESG indicators lies not in the indicators themselves, but in their role in internalizing responsibility constraints through incentive contracts. Based on this logic, it can be inferred that when compensation indicators embody measurable responsibility orientation and are linked to executive outcomes, their governance logic may also be applicable in other domains. In the AI field, where AI governance and information disclosure face similar information asymmetry and legitimacy pressure, this mechanism has applicability and potential for broader adoption. For firms, in addition to ESG indicators, they can consider introducing responsibility indicators closely related to AI disclosure authenticity in compensation and governance structures, such as technical disclosure transparency, AI risk assessment mechanisms, and AI technology conversion efficiency indexes, to achieve coordinated governance between internal incentives and external supervision.

Second, from a mechanism perspective, the governance effect of ESG compensation in reducing AI washing is mainly reflected in organizational-level features associated with two channels, namely strengthening managerial long-term orientation and enhancing employee organizational identification. In practice, AI projects are characterized by high investment, long cycles, and high uncertainty. Traditional performance evaluation systems often focus on annual performance or market reactions, which may encourage exaggerated AI claims to obtain short-term legitimacy. Through its responsibility-oriented institutional design, ESG compensation may help guide managers toward long-term performance, transforming short-term market pressure into incentives for sustained innovation and stable investment, and thereby weakening symbolic disclosure at the institutional level. At the same time, such responsibility-centered incentive arrangements may reinforce internal value alignment within the firm and enhance employees' identification with organizational goals. Within this framework, organizational-level features related to employee organizational identification may provide an institutional basis for employee participation in information sharing, risk warning, and internal monitoring. In contexts where AI-related information is highly specialized and external supervision is difficult to access, such internal mechanisms may constitute an important complementary monitoring mechanism. Accordingly, governing AI washing requires not only constraining executives' short-term incentives, but also fostering employees' sense of responsibility and collaborative participation, thereby building an internal governance system grounded in long-term orientation and organizational identification. In practice, firms should emphasize the alignment between incentives and the timing of performance realization, and incorporate long-term value indicators such as AI project outputs, technology commercialization performance, and organizational learning into performance assessment systems. Firms may also establish internal evaluation procedures for AI disclosure, feedback channels related to technology and ethics, and responsibility-oriented culture training mechanisms, forming a governance structure in which internal incentives and collective monitoring operate in parallel.

Third, the findings reveal the contextual differences in the contractual governance effect, offering differentiated insights for policy and practice. For firms with high AI technology dependency, AI has become a key support for competitive advantage, and the legitimacy risks associated with distorted disclosures are more pronounced. Therefore, these firms need to embed AI governance and responsibility indicators in performance assessments through contractual compensation arrangements to align incentives with technological development goals. External supervision also plays a crucial complementary role in contract governance. In contexts with high media attention, social visibility and reputation pressure amplify the constraint effects of internal incentives. Regulators can improve AI information disclosure standards, encourage media and third-party organizations to act as “reputation intermediaries” in authenticity assessments, and create a coordinated mechanism for external supervision and internal incentives. In firms with high managerial ability, the governance effect of ESG compensation is more pronounced, indicating that governance effectiveness depends not only on institutional design but also on managerial execution and cognitive levels. Firms can improve executive selection and assessment mechanisms, enhance AI-related training, and support strategic decision-making to improve contract enforcement and ensure the effective implementation of responsibility incentives at the organizational level.

To some extent, the findings of this study also provide supporting evidence and implications for global AI governance. Although the empirical analysis is based on Chinese A-share firms, regulatory practices and media reports across countries, including a series of recent securities enforcement actions and consumer protection cases targeting exaggerated AI claims, repeatedly indicate that AI washing is not an isolated phenomenon but a growing common problem accompanying the diffusion of AI commercialization (Table A3). This suggests that, rather than relying primarily on ex post enforcement, regulators and corporate governance practices may place greater emphasis on ex ante contractual responsibility incentives that closely link the credibility of AI disclosure to the payoffs of key decision-makers, thereby forming a multi-layer governance framework in which external supervision and internal incentives jointly constrain AI washing across different institutional contexts.

#### 7.3. Research limitations

Finally, this study has several limitations. First, for identifying ESG compensation, international studies often rely on commercial databases to construct a binary indicator, but such databases are not yet available in the Chinese context. To address this data gap, this study applies a BERT model to corporate annual report texts for semantic identification, offering an alternative approach for variable construction in non-database settings. However, consistent with prior work, this measure remains binary and cannot capture finer contract features such as the weight placed on ESG performance or the design of performance evaluation ranges, which limits indicator accuracy. Second, this study is based on a Chinese listed company sample. China provides a distinctive setting for examining the governance of AI washing because of its large-scale market for AI application and commercialization and its active disclosure practices. Yet ESG compensation in China is substantially promoted by administrative forces, which differs from institutional environments shaped by market self-regulation or decentralized regulation. Accordingly, the roles of policy constraints and legitimacy pressure may differ, and extrapolation to other institutional contexts should be made with caution. Future research may proceed in two directions. First, researchers may improve measurement granularity by obtaining information on the weight of ESG performance within compensation structures and related contract terms, thereby moving from whether ESG compensation is effective to how large its effects are. Second, as artificial intelligence diffuses globally and governance frameworks mature, cross-market validation across different institutional and cultural contexts can help assess generalizability and refine the theoretical framework from a broader comparative perspective.
