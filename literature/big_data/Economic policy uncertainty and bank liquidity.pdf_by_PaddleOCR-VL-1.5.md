## A B S T R A C T

We examine the impact of economic policy uncertainty (EPU) on bank liquidity hoarding. We create a comprehensive measure of bank liquidity hoarding that takes into account asset-, liability-, and off-balance sheet activities. Using over one million bank-quarter observations, we find that in response to EPU, banks hoard liquidity overall and through all three components. This behavior is more pronounced for banks with less liquidity, more peer-bank spillover effects, and more EPU exposure. Additional analyses of interest rate spreads on several bank products suggest that our findings reflect at least in part bank choices, rather than just the reactions of customers.

### 1. Introduction

Economic policy uncertainty (EPU) can have significant unfavorable effects on the economy (e.g., Bloom, 2014; Baker et al., 2016). Gulen and Ion (2016) find that U.S. corporate investment declines for an extended period following an increase in EPU. EPU also reduces venture capital investment (Tian and Ye, 2018), hinders merger and acquisition (M&A) activities (Nguyen and Phan, 2017; Bonaime et al., 2018), increases risk premiums on stocks (Pástor and Veronesi, 2013; Kelly et al., 2016), raises corporate debt financing costs (Kaviani et al., 2020), and distorts the relation between investment and the cost of capital (Drobetz et al., 2018). In terms of household behavior, Giavazzi and McMahon (2012) find that German households increased their savings significantly either by consuming less or by working more in reaction to the increase in uncertainty observed in the run-up to the close general elections in 1998. Aaberge et al., 2017 find that Chinese households reduced expenditures and increased savings in the face of abrupt political turmoil following the Tian'anmen Square event. $ ^{1} $

In this paper, we investigate another important channel through which EPU may harm the economy – bank liquidity hoarding. We build on the well-established bank liquidity hoarding literature and build a novel and more comprehensive measure for our tests of the effects of EPU. As discussed below, the literature typically uses bank liquid assets or other simple measures to study the bank liquidity hoarding behavior, and we improve on these with a much more comprehensive measure. We include not just the use of liquid funds (liquid assets), but also all the sources of these funds from elsewhere in the portfolio (other assets, off-balance sheet activities, and liabilities). We use the level of liquidity hoarding as our main measure of analysis, but we also present robustness checks with benchmark-adjusted liquidity hoarding.

Banks may hoard liquidity in response to high EPU in several ways. They may be holding more liquid assets to help protect against increased risks of liquidity shocks and/or anticipated funding difficulties that might otherwise require them to sell illiquid assets at fire-sale prices and/or miss out on profitable future loan opportunities (e.g., Diamond and Rajan, 2011). Liquid assets may also be increased to absorb higher

expected loan losses and/or potential declines in the values of their other assets in the face of greater uncertainty. These liquid assets may be on the balance sheet, such as cash and marketable securities, or off-balance sheet in the form of derivative contracts with positive market values that effectively function as liquid assets.

Banks may also hoard liquidity by supplying less credit when EPU is high because firms and projects they might otherwise fund could be harmed by increased uncertainty. The reduced credit may be on the asset side of the balance sheet, such as fewer commercial loans, or off the balance sheet, in the form of reduced loan commitments, standby letters of credit, or similar financial guarantees.

In addition, banks can hoard liquidity from each other in interbank markets through reduced federal funds sold or other interbank loans on the asset side of the balance sheet, spreading liquidity shocks to other banks (e.g., Allen and Gale, 2000; Diamond and Rajan, 2011; Heider et al., 2015).

On the liability side of their balance sheets, banks may raise more liquid deposits and other liquid liabilities in response to more uncertainty as well, hoarding liquid funds that might otherwise be intermediated through other banks or nonbank financial institutions and markets. We recognize the possibility that illquid liabilities could alternatively be raised to fund more liquid assets in response to short run increases in EPU, but increasing liquid liabilities is more consistent with standard liquidity management practices. EPU changes quarterly and changes in liquid liabilities such as deposits are much easier to reverse than changes in illiquid liabilities such as 10-year bonds. $ ^{2} $

To investigate the effects of EPU on bank liquidity hoarding, we create a new comprehensive measure of bank liquidity hoarding, LH (total). As discussed in Section 2, LH(total) focuses on the sources and uses of liquid funds, and is inclusive of hoarding on the asset-side (LH (asset)), liability-side (LH(liab)), and off-balance sheet-side (LH(off)) of banks. Thus, LH(total) is the sum of LH(asset), LH(liab), and LH(off). As explained below, our measure is much more comprehensive than can be found in the existing literature. Others generally only use part of the asset-side liquidity hoarding, either liquid assets or illiquid loans, while we combine both and also include liability-side and off-balance sheet-side hoarding.

We hypothesize that LH(total) is positively affected by EPU. Our main analysis includes data on virtually all U.S. commercial banks quarterly for over 30 years from 1985:Q2 to 2016:Q4, more than 17,000 unique banks and over one million bank-quarter observations. Regression analyses show that higher EPU results in statistically and economically significant increases in total bank liquidity hoarding, as well as increases in the asset-, liability- and off-balance sheet-side components.

Our findings are robust to the use of instrumental variable estimation and placebo tests. The main results also hold using alternative specifications of the liquidity hoarding, after controlling for market volatility and other alternative measures of uncertainty, across bank size classes, for banks with both pre- and post-Basel III capital and liquidity requirements, for banks in markets with both favorable and unfavorable local economic conditions, and for banks in different survival categories. Additional analyses suggest that the impact of EPU on liquidity hoarding is more pronounced for banks with liquidity shortages, peer-banks spillover effects, and more exposure to EPU.

We recognize that the measured effects of EPU on liquidity hoarding may reflect customers' demand and supply choices as well as those of the banks. We address this issue by examining the impact of EPU on interest rate spreads on loans, loan commitments, and deposits, which are key individual elements of our liquidity hoarding measures on the asset-, off-balance sheet- and liability-sides, respectively. The purpose of these interest rate spread analyses is to try to rule out the possibility that our main finding that EPU increases bank liquidity hoarding is driven entirely by customer demands and supplies. Our analyses of loans and loan commitments use credit spreads on over 28,000 individual term loans and revolvers from DealScan and data on the borrowing firms from Compustat. Our deposit spread analysis employs nearly 6 million observations from RateWatch. Our findings in all three cases are that the spreads increase with EPU, the opposite directions of what would occur if customer reactions were solely driving the results, increasing confidence in our findings that banks hoard liquidity in reaction to EPU.

A key contribution of our paper is that we develop the only comprehensive measure of bank liquidity hoarding (LH(total)) that takes into account all sources and uses of liquid funds both on- and off-balance sheet. As presented in Table 1 Panel A, we include the contributions to liquidity hoarding of all liquid and illiquid assets, liabilities, and off-balance sheet activities. Panel B of Table 1 shows the theoretical and empirical bank liquidity hoarding measures in the literature, each of which includes only some of the items we incorporate. Theoretical models primarily focus on holdings of liquid assets, such as cash (e.g., Diamond and Rajan, 2011; Acharya et al., 2012; Gale and Yorulmazer, 2013; Heider et al., 2015) or reserve balances (e.g., Acharya and Merrouche, 2012). One model emphasizes reduced lending (e.g., Acharya and Skeie, 2011). Others key in on liquidity hoarding in interbank markets due to uncertainty about banks' liquidity needs (e.g., Allen et al., 2009), counterparty credit risk (e.g., Heider et al., 2015) or during financial crises caused by Knightian uncertainty and liquidity shortages (e.g., Caballero and Krishnamurthy, 2008). Empirical studies of liquidity hoarding typically examine levels or changes in various categories of liquid assets (e.g., Cornett et al., 2011; Berrospide, 2012; Acharya and Mora, 2015), or prices and quantities of interbank funds (Afonso et al., 2011). Some papers also consider the effects on key components of the liquidity hoarding, such as liquid assets (e.g., Cornett et al., 2011; Berrospide, 2012; Acharya and Mora, 2015), loans (e.g., Cornett et al., 2011; Acharya and Mora, 2015), deposits (e.g., Berrospide, 2012; Acharya and Mora, 2015), and unused loan commitments (e.g., Cornett et al., 2011; Berrospide, 2012; Acharya and Mora, 2015). Some of these papers also find that banks tried to attract more deposits in response to the financial crisis, but again do not incorporate these deposits in their liquidity hoarding measures (e.g., Berrospide, 2012; Acharya and Mora, 2015).

Other studies look at the effects of uncertainty beyond the subprime financial crisis on quantities of bank loans. Gissler et al. (2016) find that banks that perceive more regulatory uncertainty reduce mortgage loans more severely. Raunig et al. (2017) similarly find reduced loan quantities after four uncertainty events based on the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) volatility index (VXO). Finally, Bordo et al. (2016) document a negative relation between EPU and bank loan quantities. An important issue in these loan quantity papers is that the lending declines can also reflect less demand for bank loans by firms and households due to uncertainty. Bordo et al. (2016) try to deal with this issue by showing that the reductions in lending are associated with bank characteristics, such as low capital ratios that are related to bank financial distress. None of these other studies conduct additional analyses of interest rate spreads as we do to rule out the possibility that the observed reactions to uncertainty reflect bank choices, rather than passive responses to customer choices.

The only other liquidity hoarding papers of which we are aware that use interest rates to examine the bank supply effects are Afonso et al. (2011) and Acharya and Mora (2015). The former paper examines spreads in the interbank federal funds market during the subprime financial crisis, and the latter uses implicit deposit rates calculated from Call Report data to examine whether banks facing more liquidity problems during this crisis increased demand for liquid funds. ## 2. Bank liquidity hoarding and economic policy uncertainty measures

In this section, we describe and provide summary statistics and correlations for our key dependent and independent variables, bank liquidity hoarding (LH) and economic policy uncertainty (EPU).

#### 2.1. Bank liquidity hoarding measures

Our key dependent variables are our comprehensive bank liquidity hoarding measure LH(total) and its components, LH(asset), LH(liab), and LH(off). These measures assign balance sheet and off-balance sheet

activities weights of +1/2 and -1/2, depending on whether these items contribute to or subtract from liquidity hoarding. As indicated above, Panel A of Table 1 shows the formula for these measures and provides details for each category. Total bank liquidity hoarding, LH(total) is defined as LH(asset) + LH(liab) + LH(off), where LH(asset) = (+1/2) × liquid assets + (-1/2) × illiquid assets, LH(liab) = (+1/2) × liquid liabilities, and LH(off) = (-1/2) × illiquid guarantees + (+1/2) × liquid derivatives.

The logic behind the measures is straightforward. Banks hoard liquidity by holding liquid assets, such as cash and securities, and off-balance sheet derivative contracts with positive market values that function similarly to liquid assets, like in-the-money interest rate swaps. Because these items contribute to banks' liquidity hoarding, they are assigned positive weights. These liquid assets and derivatives can be increased by decreasing illiquid assets, such as C&I loans, so illiquid assets are given negative weights. Similarly, liquid funds can be boosted by increasing liquid liabilities, like transactions deposits, so these items are assigned positive weights. Illiquid off-balance sheet financial guarantees, like loan commitments, are also given negative weights, since decreasing these items boosts future liquid assets by reducing future claims against them. The +1/2 and -1/2 weights assure that raising $1 of liquid assets by reducing $1 of illiquid assets increases bank liquidity hoarding by $1, and similarly for the other combinations of assets, liabilities, and off-balance sheet activities. Thus, the weighting scheme ensures that all sources and uses of liquid funds are accounted for and weighted appropriately.

The classifications of which assets, liabilities, and off-balance sheet activities are liquid and illiquid in Table 1 Panel A are adapted from Berger and Bouwman's (2009) classifications for their "cat fat" bank liquidity creation measure. All financial activities are classified as liquid, semiliquid, or illiquid based on the ease, cost, and time for banks or their customers to obtain liquid funds.

A few items that are included in and excluded from Panel A require some clarification. First, we show only items classified as liquid and illiquid, which affect the liquidity hoarding measures, and exclude items classified as semiliquid with zero weights that are not needed for the LH calculations.

Second, we include “net participations sold” as illiquid guarantees, instead of Berger and Bouwman’s (2009) classification of its opposite, “net participations acquired,” as liquid guarantees. This is a semantic difference that allows us to use fewer categories but does not affect the measurement of liquidity hoarding.

Finally, the liquid derivatives entries in Panel A are the gross fair values of liquid derivative contracts, which function similarly to liquid securities on the asset side of the balance sheet. Those with positive gross fair values contribute to liquidity hoarding and those with negative values detract from such hoarding.

It is important to distinguish liquidity hoarding (LH) from Berger and Bouwman's liquidity creation (LC). LH refers to liquidity held by the bank, whereas LC is liquidity supplied to the public. Some of the LH components are direct opposites from the components of LC and are measured using the LC data on Bouwman's website (https://sites.google.com/a/tamu.edu/bouwman/data). We measure LH(asset) as -LC (asset), since LC(asset) gives weights of -1/2 to liquid assets, +1/2 weights to illiquid assets, and 0 weights to semiliguid assets, and we need to reverse the negative and positive signs. Similarly, LH(off) is measured by -LC(off), since LC(off) assigns positive weights to illiquid guarantees and negative weights to liquid derivatives.

However, key differences occur on the liability side of the balance sheet. LH(liab) equals +1/2 times all bank liquid liabilities, the same sign as LC(liab) instead of the opposite sign, given that liquid liabilities can be used to raise funds to hoard liquidity. Thus, liquid liabilities like core deposits may be used to hoard liquidity for the bank even while creating liquidity for the public. Also, LH(liab) does not include illiquid items on the liability side of the balance sheet, such as subordinated debt and equity. These long-duration items are normally not used to raise liquid assets, as discussed in the Introduction. In contrast, they are given -1/2 weights in LC(liab). Our goal is different from Berger and Bouwman (2009) – we are interested in liquidity hoarding by the banks, rather than the liquidity they provide to the public. The liquidity hoarding data is available at Hugh Kim's website (http://www.hughhkim.com).

We normalize LH(total) and its components LH(asset), LH(liab), and LH(off) by gross total assets (GTA) in our regression analyses so that the measures are comparable across banks, and the regression results are not dominated by the largest banks. $ ^{3} $ Dollar values are adjusted to real 2016 values using the implicit GDP price deflator.

Following the literature’s practice, we refer to the level of liquidity held by banks as the amount of liquidity hoarding, rather than subtracting some amount of liquidity that would be held in a normal course of business. In the Online Appendix (Section A.4), we conduct ancillary tests that incorporate the benchmark and report analysis results that are consistent with our main findings.

#### 2.2. Economic policy uncertainty measures

Our key explanatory variables are measures of EPU, which are obtained from BBD's website (http://www.policyuncertainty.com). They are based on textual analysis of newspaper articles and compilation of policy uncertainty related to government spending, inflation risk, and tax code expiration. The newspaper element EPU(News) is based on textual analysis of ten large newspapers. The government spending measure EPU(Govt.) is the scaled interquartile range of four-quarter-ahead purchases by federal and state/local government. Inflation-related policy uncertainty EPU(CPI) is based on the interquartile range of four-quarter-ahead inflation risk, and the tax measure EPU(Tax) draws on temporary federal tax code provisions.

EPU(Composite) is the weighted sum of these measures with a weight of 1/2 for EPU(News), and weights of 1/6 for each of the other measures. The EPU measures constructed by BBD have a monthly frequency. We follow Gulen and Ion (2016) and take the natural log of the arithmetic average of the BBD indices over the three months of the quarter.

We lag the independent variables to mitigate potential reverse-causality concerns and account for lagged responses of banks to EPU. We include an extensive set of controls. For the bank control variables (X), we follow Cornett et al., (2011), who study the impact of funding sources on asset-side liquidity hoarding during the financial crisis of 2007–2009. However, a key difference is that Cornett et al. (2011) control for the liability-side and off-balance sheet side liquidity and show they are related to credit supply. Here, we examine the overall liquidity hoarding behavior of banks, by combining asset-side, liability-side, and off-balance sheet side liquidity hoarding in the dependent variable. Given the distinct set of goals, we include  $ \ln(GTA) $,  $ sqr.\ \ln(GTA) $, and Capital ratio, taken from Call Reports, to account for bank size and leverage. Large and better-capitalized banks tend to hoard less liquidity. We also control for a bank competition level (HHI), calculated as a deposit-weighted average of the Herfindahl–Hirschman index in all areas in which a bank has branch offices (Jiang et al., 2019), obtained from the Summary of Deposits by FDIC (from 1994 to 2016) and Bouwman's website (from 1985 to 1993).

For the demand controls (W), we follow Gulen and Ion (2016) because a corporate investment is an important determinant of the demand for banking services. We control for Tobin's Q, a state-level cross-sectional average of normalized Tobin's Q; and Cash flows, a state-level cross-sectional average of operating cash flows for each firm, both obtained from Compustat. We also control for Population, a bank-level population index calculated as the natural log of a weighted average of the population (in millions) in all areas in which a bank has a business, obtained from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis.

We also control for other sources of macro-level uncertainty (Z). We include Election year, a binary variable for if the calendar year is a presidential election year; SD (stock ret.), the standard deviation of daily value-weighted stock market returns; and GDP dispersion, the forecast dispersion of real GDP. Appendix Table provides a detailed definition of all variables.

Finally, following Cornett et al. (2011), we include bank fixed effects ( $ \alpha $) to control for omitted time-invariant bank characteristics, and quarter dummies (q) to account for seasonality. We cluster standard errors by bank and year-quarter to account for correlations of error terms. $ ^{6} $

#### 4.2. Instrumental variable analysis and placebo tests

Endogeneity is a potential concern. Although we saturate our regressions with extensive controls, bias may arise from omitted explanatory variables. For example, the general economic uncertainty that we are unable to control for could drive both EPU and LH. Similarly, increases in LH could create uncertainty for regulators and politicians on how to respond, creating a reverse causality problem.

To address these concerns, we follow Gulen and Ion (2016) and implement an instrumental variable approach using the U.S. Senate polarization index of McCarty et al. (1997) as an instrument for EPU (Composite). Increased polarization can bring about political gridlock, which in turn breeds uncertainty about policy choices (McCarty, 2012), indicating that our instrument satisfies the relevance condition. It is unlikely that U.S. Senate polarization would directly affect bank liquidity hoarding other than through its impact on policy uncertainty, satisfying the exclusion restriction. The first-stage regression in column 1 of Table 5 Panel A shows the expected positive and significant effect of Senate polarization on EPU(Composite). In the second-stage regressions in columns 2–5, we regress the LH measures on the instrumented EPU measure,  $ \widehat{EPU}(\text{Composite}) $, and the controls. The t-statistics are based on bootstrapped standard errors to mitigate biases from errors in the estimated independent variables. The coefficients estimate all have the same positive signs and significance with comparable magnitudes as our main results.

We perform placebo tests in Table 5 Panel B. We replace EPU(Composite) measure with  $ \widetilde{EPU}(\text{Composite}) $ randomly drawn from the sample distribution of EPU(Composite). We estimate regression coefficients with 100 random samples of  $ \widetilde{EPU}(\text{Composite}) $ and report the average coefficient estimates.  $ \widetilde{EPU}(\text{Composite}) $ is neither statistically nor economically significant, supporting our main findings.

#### 4.3. Heterogeneity analyses

We next conduct heterogeneity analyses to explore how the effects of EPU differ across subsamples. We first investigate whether a bank's prior liquidity hoarding level affects the relations between LH and EPU. We divide the banks according to average LH(total)/GTA over the prior four quarters. The banks with high (low) liquidity are those above (below) the 75th (25th) percentile. Table 6, columns 1 and 2 present the estimated coefficients for the two groups. The coefficient on EPU(Composite) is greater for banks with low prior liquidity, and the difference is statistically significant at the 5% level (z-statistic = 2.274), suggesting that less liquid banks with less liquidity are more sensitive to EPU.

We also incorporate the heterogeneous impact of peer-bank spillover effects. A bank with peers that hoarding more liquidity in response to EPU may have more incentive to hoard liquidity to avoid a liquidity shortage or to protect itself from predatory trading (e.g., Acharya, Gromb, and Yorulmazer, 2008). To test this idea, we use a peer-bank spillover effect at the industry level (e.g., Kim (2020)). We first calculate a lagged value of “aggregate liquidity hoarding” minus “a bank’s own liquidity hoarding” scaled by gross total assets as a measure of all peer banks’ liquidity hoarding (Peer LH):

 $$ Peer LH_{(-i),t}=\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}LH(total)_{j,t}}{\sum_{j\neq i}GTA_{j,t}} $$ 

We then measure the EPU-driven Spillover effect as the sensitivity of the change ratio in Peer LH to the change ratio in EPU(composite). High Spillover effect implies that a bank confronts other banks' high tendency to hoard liquidity in response to EPU. We run our baseline regression for banks facing Spillover effects above and below the median in Table 6 (columns 3 and 4). We find that the magnitude of the coefficient on EPU (Composite) is about twice as high for banks confronting high EPU-driven Spillover effects, suggesting that a bank's liquidity hoarding response to EPU is much more pronounced when peer banks also have outsized reactions.

We next analyze the impacts of EPU on liquidity hoarding of banks with different degrees of exposure to EPU. We consider a bank's stock return co-movement with changes in EPU as a proxy for the bank's exposure to EPU (Pástor and Veronesi, 2013; Kelly et al., 2016). $ ^{9} $ We estimate each bank's EPU exposure based on time-series regressions of a bank's monthly stock returns on monthly changes in EPU(Composite). Consistent with expectations that EPU hurts banks' performance, bank stock returns are strongly negatively related to EPU(Composite). We regress individual banks' monthly returns in excess of the risk-free rate on EPU(Composite) and most of the  $ \beta_{i,EPU} $ coefficients are negative (see Table 2 for summary statistics). Table 6, columns 5–6 show the regression results for the asymmetric impact of EPU on banks with high versus low EPU exposure based on the sample median. The estimated coefficient is greater for banks with high EPU exposure, and the difference is statistically significant at 5% level (z-statistic = 2.157).

The coefficients on the controls are generally consistent with expectations that risky borrowers are charged high interest spreads. $ ^{15} $ More levered firms pay higher spreads, and more profitable firms are charged lower spreads. Firms with more liquidity and higher credit ratings are charged lower interest rates. The coefficients on loan characteristics (Credit size, Maturity, Secured, Covenant index) are also consistent with riskier borrowers – i.e., those with smaller amounts, shorter maturities, more collateral and tighter covenants – being charged higher spreads. The only control with mixed signs is asset tangibility: positive for term loans and negative for revolvers. This may reflect clientele effects in which revolvers are more often granted to relatively safe relationship borrowers, while term loan borrowers are more often risky transactions credits. More tangible assets allow revolver borrowers to signal high quality by pledging collateral, while more tangible assets allow banks to require more collateral to mitigate moral hazard problems of low-quality term loan customers. $ ^{16} $

### 6. Conclusions, policy implications, and topics for future research

An exciting new research agenda explores the implications of economic policy uncertainty (EPU), and finds adverse effects on corporate and household behavior. Much of this literature employs the EPU measures of Baker et al., 2016). We extend this literature by investigating another important potential channel through which EPU may affect the real economy – by increasing bank liquidity hoarding. We build a comprehensive new measure of bank liquidity hoarding and its asset-, liability-, and off-balance sheet components. We examine the effects of EPU on bank total liquidity hoarding.

Our main empirical analysis finds that EPU increases total bank liquidity hoarding as well as all three components. Our findings are robust to the use of instrumental variables and are more pronounced for banks with less liquidity, more peer-banks spillover effects, and more exposure to EPU. Our results using credit spreads for term loans and revolvers from DealScan and deposit interest rate spreads from RateWatch all suggest that our main results reflect at least to some degree banks' efforts to hoard more liquidity in the face of EPU, rather than just the reactions of asset, off-balance sheet, and liability customers of the banks.

Turning to policy implications, government actions to reduce bank liquidity hoarding and increase bank output may be effective in improving the real economy to the extent that bank output positively affects the real economy. The literature suggests that in most circumstances, additional bank output favorably influences the real economy (see Berger et al., 2020 for a survey), so more certainty in government policies that reduce EPU may have positive social outcomes. However, an important exception may be during the height of credit and liquidity creation booms. During such booms, banks may lower their credit standards and produce excessive credit that may contribute to subsequent socially costly financial crises (e.g., Thakor, 2005; Acharya and Naqvi, 2012; Berger and Bouwman, 2017).

Our findings also raise potential ideas for future research. Additional

research on the determinants of bank liquidity hoarding, as comprehensively measured here, may be fruitful. More research on the relations among EPU, bank liquidity hoarding, and the real economy may also be are in order.
