## A B S T R A C T

We examine the use of scientific research in the development of policy documents within the context of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for diagnosing, treating, and managing diabetes. Using natural language processing, we identify "hidden citations" (i.e., textual credit without formal citations) and "token citations" (i.e., formal citations without textual credit) to scientific research within CPGs to understand how scientific evidence is selected and integrated. We find that both types of citations are pervasive, calling into question the use of formal citations alone in understanding the societal impact of scientific research. Using data on scholarly citations and expert ratings, we find that hidden citations are positively associated with the actual impact of the research on patients and caregivers while token citations associate positively with scientific impact. Qualitative insights gathered from interviews with senior guideline writers further illustrate the reasons for certain functions of scientific research, which involve balancing scientific rigor with practical demands in the guideline writing process, the need for local adaptations, political dynamics on the organizational level, and individual preferences towards certain types of studies or the use of experiential knowledge. Our work underscores the critical role of research utilization in translating scientific evidence into policy, showing that policymaker decisions shape societal impact as much as the engagement efforts of scientists, and extends institutional accounts of symbolic and substantive knowledge use.

### 1. Introduction

Scientists, universities, and research funding agencies worldwide are increasingly required to demonstrate the societal impact of academic research (Gaunand et al., 2015; Matt et al., 2017; Reed et al., 2021; Salter and Martin, 2001; Smit and Hessels, 2021). Defined as the direct or indirect contributions of research to society, societal impact can be reflected in new or revised approaches to social, health, environmental, cultural, and economic issues, influencing policymaking and public debate in these areas (Bornmann, 2013). Despite a long tradition of exploring the interplay between science and policy, and the application of scientific expertise and evidence in policymaking, there is still widespread dissatisfaction with the degree to which science, and academic research in particular, shapes policy (Almeida and Báscolo, 2006; Kenny et al., 2017). A persistent notion in policymaking is that scientific research becomes more likely to be utilized when the evidence has been produced through rigorous methods and effectively translated by the scientists (National Research Council, 2012; Sanderson, 2009). In fact, research funding bodies, such as the UK Research and Innovation Council, the US National Science Foundation, or the European Framework Programmes, require engagement strategies in the application for funding. In these performance-based research funding systems, showing an impact on policy is increasingly crucial for evaluating organizations (Hicks, 2012) and the scientists within them (Wilkinson, 2019), placing the responsibility largely on the scientists to select relevant topics, devise engagement strategies, achieve societal visibility in blogs, news media or policy documents, and track the societal impact of their research (D'Este and Robinson-García, 2023).

More recent work has called for a change in perspective in the understanding of how science can make a policy impact, focusing on the utilization of science in the policymaking process (Edler et al., 2022). This perspective suggests that the societal impact of academic research depends as much on the way how scientific knowledge is searched for and absorbed as it depends on scientists' engagement and communication efforts (Edler and James, 2015). Indeed, the uptake of scientific knowledge can be characterized as "the result of actors deliberating

about, and reflecting on issue framing, policy problems and policy options" (Edler et al., 2022: 211). Although this perspective highlights the critical role of users in the creation of societal impact by scientific knowledge, research on the concrete patterns of scientific knowledge utilization in policy documents is scarce and empirically challenging.

In the realm of science, citations in scholarly publications have traditionally been used to identify the reception and scientific impact of research (Sinatra et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2013), based on the notion that citations indicate an intellectual influence on the citing author (Teplitskiy et al., 2022). Similarly, citations to scientific research in policy documents have often been used as indicators of societal impact (Bornmann et al., 2016; Szomszor and Adie, 2022). When it comes to scholarly citations, scientists also oftentimes omit citations in their articles despite giving clear textual credit to prior research, a phenomenon coined "hidden citations" (Meng et al., 2024). Given the dynamic and complex nature of policy development processes on the one hand and the increasing information processing demands due to a dramatic expansion in scientific research output on the other hand (Chu and Evans, 2021; Jones, 2009), it is unclear how and why scientific knowledge is adopted in policy documents. Hence, we ask: what are the patterns of scientific knowledge utilization in policy documents? Answering this question is important because it helps to understand how scientific research creates societal impact beyond the efforts of scientists (Edler et al., 2022). Our research draws on institutional theory (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Suchman, 1995) and prior work suggesting that scientific knowledge may serve different functions within policymaking (Boswell, 2008). From this perspective, policymakers may justify decisions to use scientific research either through the substantive quality of a policy (e.g., the effectiveness of an intervention) or by signaling adherence to socially valued norms and procedures. This implies that scientific evidence may be used in both substantive and symbolic ways (Amara et al., 2004).

In this paper, we develop a natural language processing (NLP) approach to understand the utilization of scientific research and the underlying dynamics in creating societal impact. In a first step, we leverage recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) to identify textual credit to scientific research in policy documents. Defined as machines that simulate or mimic cognitive human functions based on algorithmic predictions, AI and its rapid evolution have been argued to herald fundamental changes to scientific research, "moving science forward with a speed and accuracy unimaginable to us today" (Wang and Barabási, 2021: 235). There is evidence that AI may assist with identifying relevant literature (Extance, 2018), processing large amounts of data (Agarwal et al., 2023), predicting outcomes (Jumper et al., 2021), or identifying hidden citations in academic research (Meng et al., 2024). We build on this research to identify textual credit to scientific research in the context of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) in diabetes. CPGs represent key policy documents in evidence-based decision-making for the diagnosis, treatment, and management of health conditions, making them critical for shaping practices in health care systems (Burgers et al., 2003). CPGs are developed by highly credentialed international and national guideline development groups, working under the auspices of organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) or the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) that employ various approaches to guideline construction and writing (Kredo et al., 2016). While guidelines contain formal citations to scientific articles, they may also contain numerous hidden citations, similar to academic papers (Meng et al., 2024). Moreover, they may reference an article but give little textual credit to the scientific research, a phenomenon that we will refer to as "token citations". In fact, Teplitskiy et al. (2022) show that most citations in academic articles serve a rhetorical function, with little-to-no intellectual influence on the authors citing the research.

Our main analysis builds on scientific articles on diabetes published in the period from 2000 to 2014 by grant holders of the Novo Nordisk Foundation in Denmark, one of the largest health-related research funding organizations in the world. Using PubMedBERT, a domain-specific NLP model, we first identify instances in which the most important international CPGs for diabetes give clear textual credit to one of those articles, indicating that the research documented in the article had been utilized in the development of the guideline. Comparing whether articles have been formally cited and/or been given textual credit, we find that both hidden and token citations are pervasive, suggesting that societal impact assessments that rely on citations in policy documents alone inaccurately represent the societal impact of scientific research.

In a second step, we explore potential explanations for the observed patterns of research utilization in CPGs. To do so, we obtain fine-grained data from senior diabetes guideline writers who rate the actual patient impact, i.e., the impact on the lives of patients and caregivers or on medical practice, of the research documented in each of the articles in our sample. Moreover, we collect data on the number of scholarly citations these articles received, indicating their scientific impact in the academic literature. To better understand the results from our analysis, we conduct three exploratory interviews with high-level guideline writers recognized as leading experts in the field of diabetes. As expected, we find that formal citations in CPGs that also give textual credit to the research in the cited article are positively associated with both patient and scientific impact. Interestingly, however, hidden citations are positively associated with patient but not scientific impact, whereas token citations are positively associated with scientific but not patient impact. A supplementary analysis based on out-of-sample data confirms the patterns in the main analysis.

The follow-up interviews with guideline writers reveal more nuanced reasons behind the observed patterns, pointing at different functions that articles serve in CPGs. Accordingly, hidden citations occur due to practical barriers and the need for local adaptations, for example, when policy recommendations need to be adapted to specific populations or resource-limited settings. Moreover, new scientific evidence may be highly relevant to a CPG but still unpublished, leading to the omission of a formal citation. In contrast, a CPG may include token citations to reflect ongoing debates or foundational knowledge in the CPG development process without directly translating scientific evidence into recommendations. Organizational commitment to credibility and political dynamics often drive these citations to reflect influential voices or ties to external stakeholders. Furthermore, high-profile studies are cited to lend legitimacy or recognize seminal work, even if the studies no longer directly inform current clinical practice.

Taken together, our study carries implications for research on the impact of scientific knowledge on policymaking as well as on science policy. First, we contribute to the growing literature on assessing societal impact (Joly et al., 2015; Matt et al., 2017; Reed et al., 2021). While citations in policy documents have traditionally been used for societal impact assessment (Szomszor and Adie, 2022), our findings show that scientific research is often given textual credit without a formal citation, leading to an underestimation of its societal impact. These hidden citations have been documented by Meng et al. (2024) for the scholarly literature as a result of scientific knowledge becoming commonplace. Our analysis reveals that the reasons for hidden citations in policy documents are much more complex, reflecting the dynamics of policymaking processes and personal judgment of policymakers. Conversely, we also find that research has been cited without being given textual credit (token citations), indicating an overestimation of how much scientific knowledge actually impacted policy. This finding complements prior work documenting a rhetorical function of many citations in the academic literature with little influence on the citing author (MacRoberts and MacRoberts, 1989; Teplitskiy et al., 2022). Similar to prior research that uses NLP techniques to identify new scientific ideas and their proliferation in academic literature (Arts et al., 2025), our study shifts the focus from mere citations to the actual use of research results in CPGs, enabling a more reliable assessment of societal impact. The ability to analyze large samples of other policy documents as well as potential impact cases by means of an AI-assisted approach showcases

another application of AI in the scientific enterprise.

Second, we contribute to literature focusing on the utilization of research as a key factor shaping the societal impact of scientific research (Amara et al., 2004; Edler et al., 2022; Jennings Jr. and Hall, 2011). We uncover the dynamics of research utilization, gaining insights on characteristics of the research articles as well as the guideline writing process, organizational factors, individual preferences, and barriers that shape the development of CPGs. These guidelines constitute important policy documents in health care, developed by guideline writers whose judgment in compiling the documents is informed by scientific evidence but also subject to the context in which they work, their opinions, and the constraints they face. Hence, our research provides evidence of the instrumental, legitimizing, and substantiating functions of research use (Boswell, 2008). Hidden citations represent instrumental use, in which research informs recommendations without being explicitly highlighted, whereas token citations align with legitimizing use, in which research is invoked to signal scientific compliance despite weak integration into the text. Moreover, instances where research is both cited and given textual credit reflect substantiating use, in which evidence is selectively mobilized to lend authority to preferred positions. Taken together, this mapping highlights how institutional pressures shape not only the uptake of scientific knowledge but also its visibility, suggesting that visibility itself may operate as an institutional mechanism through which legitimacy is constructed – an aspect that institutional theory has thus far rarely theorized explicitly (Suchman, 1995). Hence, we respond to calls in the literature for a change in perspective in the understanding of how science can make a policy impact, taking the utilization of science in the policymaking process into account with respect to how scientific knowledge is searched for and absorbed (Edler et al., 2022).

### 2. Conceptual background

### 2.1. Assessing the impact of science

Traditionally, assessing the impact of scientific research has focused on the influence that research has on other research, referred to as scientific impact (cf. Bornmann, 2013; Niederkrotenthaler et al., 2011). Metrics for scientific impact are usually citation-based and exist for different levels of the publication pyramid (Balayla, 2017). At the article level, impact is gauged by the number of citations a paper accumulates in the academic literature (Neylon and Wu, 2009). At the researcher level, metrics such as the h-index and its variations are utilized to quantify an individual's scholarly performance (Bornmann and Daniel, 2005; Bornmann et al., 2011). Tools like the Journal Impact Factor (Garfield, 2006) and the Eigenfactor (Bergstrom et al., 2008) seek to determine a journal's relative influence within its discipline. A major drawback of these citation-based metrics is their limited resonance outside the academic realm, often providing a narrow viewpoint on the broader impact of scientific research on policy (Bornmann and Daniel, 2008). This limitation is further accentuated when considering the selective nature of citations in documents that shape new policies. $ ^{1} $

Over the past two decades, increased criticism of citations to predominantly capture impact within academia has led to the development of alternative ways of assessing the impact of science (see Smit and Hessels, 2021, for a review). These include the assessment of usage-based metrics such as the number of downloads of a specific article from the publishers' platforms or the increasingly popular "altmetrics". The latter, sourced from online platforms like news outlets and social media, offer an expansive perspective on the reception of research beyond the scholarly community (Bornmann, 2014). At the same time, altmetrics have been criticized for rather measuring attention than capturing how research results are actually adopted by policymakers and in society (Fenner, 2014). A third way of assessing societal impact has been the use of impact case studies that deeply explore the nexus between scientific research and its societal application (Bozeman and Youtie, 2017). However, impact case studies, while valuable, are often labor-intensive, may lack objectivity, and tend to offer anecdotal evidence, limiting their generalizability (Bornmann, 2013).

All these methods to gauge the societal impact of academic research have in common that they adopt a "supply-side" perspective. Edler et al. (2022) argue that the current literature on the impact of science on policymaking is biased towards the efforts of scientists to generate that impact, and it neglects to address the conditions present in policymaking and organizations that use scientific research. For instance, the salience, credibility, and legitimacy of scientific knowledge are important for its uptake in policy. This supply-side bias has resulted in an overemphasis on monitoring, assessing, and reporting the impact of research, placing the burden of demonstrating research impacts on scientists even though the impact of science is often a long-term process that is not always easily identifiable. Instead of predominantly focusing on how scientists can improve the format and delivery of research to increase societal impact, Edler et al. (2022) suggest that more attention should be paid to understanding how scientific knowledge is searched for and absorbed, envisioning a "demand-side assessment" that analyzes the functions of scientific research in policymaking.

#### 2.2. Clinical practice guidelines as policy documents informed by scientific evidence

Given the diabetes context of our study, we conceptualize societal impact following Alla et al. (2017: 9) as “a direct or indirect contribution of research processes or outputs that have informed (or resulted in) the development of new [...] health policy/practices, or revisions of existing [...] health policy/practices, at various levels of governance (international, national, state, local, organizational, health unit).” In line with this definition, we are interested in how findings from diabetes research shape and refine both new and existing policies in diabetes prevention, diagnosis, and treatment by influencing relevant clinical practice guidelines. Generally speaking, clinical practice guidelines (CPGs, also referred to as medical guidelines or protocols) have the purpose of informing and assisting clinicians and patients in clinical decision-making (Tzeel, 2002). CPGs are systematically developed statements and the primary policy documents in clinical decision-making and patient care, aiming to minimize unnecessary variations in practice and to encourage the provision of high-quality, evidence-based care (Thomas, 1999).

CPGs share many characteristics with policy documents in other domains, including their development, implementation, and review processes. Both CPGs and other policy documents aim to standardize practices and improve outcomes, providing evidence-based recommendations (Burgers et al., 2003). The creation of CPGs involves a systematic review of evidence and input from various stakeholders, similar to the development of other policy documents. Moreover, these documents are periodically reviewed and updated to reflect new evidence and changing practices (Kredo et al., 2016). In that sense, by providing practically implementable recommendations and decision criteria, guidelines are meant to inform actual actions and not just scientific debates. Guidelines are widely diffused and highly accepted among practitioners. Institutions, such as the Council of Europe, see it as an obligation for healthcare professionals to be aware of the guidelines in their specific medical area (Council of Europe, 2002). Moreover, survey results indicate that clinicians and practitioners use guidelines as the most important source to update their knowledge on new treatments (Novo Nordisk Foundation, 2017).

As such, CPGs represent a case in which policy documents are linked to scientific research. Like academic articles, they contain citations to published research. But they are also different from articles:

recommendations for or against the use of specific clinical practices involve subjective value judgments influenced by the opinions and experiences of the guideline development group. In addition, they may emphasize non-medical aspects such as cost control (Woolf et al., 1999). Therefore, not all of the scientific research on which a guideline is based may have been cited (White and Wang, 1997), and not all of the cited research may actually have been used (Bornmann and Daniel, 2008).

As a consequence, societal impact assessments that rely on explicit citations to scientific research in CPGs may be biased, similar to the issues with citation analysis in the academic literature (MacRoberts and MacRoberts, 1989). Meng et al. (2024) describe the phenomenon of "hidden citations" of prior research in scientific articles, also referred to as indirect or implicit citations. Hidden citations in scientific articles may be due to a particular scientific discovery having become deeply embedded in the scientific literature, so that it becomes common knowledge and "obliterated by incorporation" (Garfield, 1975; Merton, 1973). They may also be due to restrictions imposed by publishing venues and authors' decisions to be selective or reference a review article instead (Wilhite and Fong, 2012). Consequently, they present considerable challenges for traditional bibliometric measures of impact. Meng et al. (2024) develop a machine-learning approach to detect hidden citations in the corpus of text of an academic article by identifying instances of textual credit to prior research. They find hidden citations to be a pervasive phenomenon, indicating that an article's ability to credit all sources of inspiration is limited. Moreover, they document that those papers that accumulate hidden citations tend to be highly cited otherwise. At the same time, they show that a substantial fraction of papers with low citation counts still accumulate hidden citations. Moreover, Teplitskiy et al. (2022) show, based on an extensive author survey, that most citations in academic articles serve a rhetorical function, with little-to-no intellectual influence on the authors citing the research. Still, higher citation counts of an article lead authors to more meaningfully engage with that research, since its perceived quality is higher. However, how these dynamics play out in the context of policy documents like CPGs is unclear.

#### 2.3. An institutional view on the functions of scientific research in policymaking

The development of policy is a complex process, influenced as much by values and interests as by evidence and research (Hall, 1993). Policymakers need to navigate the constraints of policy development cycles, while at the same time being influenced by the dominant discourse within their organizational environment and their own beliefs about what constitutes valuable knowledge (Amara et al., 2004). We conceptualize these dynamics through an institutional theory perspective, which emphasizes how rules, norms, logics, and shared beliefs shape organizational behavior and lead actors to conform to socially expected standards (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). From this perspective, policymaking organizations are fundamentally concerned with securing legitimacy by meeting societal expectations regarding appropriate structures, practices, and outputs (Scott, 1991; Boswell, 2008). Legitimacy may stem either from the substantive quality of their outputs, such as the effectiveness of their interventions, or from signaling their adherence to socially valued norms and procedures, such as demonstrating compliance with scientific conventions (Boswell, 2008). These institutional pressures influence how different policy domains consider and mobilize scientific research, with some areas (e.g., health) more strongly oriented towards evidence-based practice than others (Hanney and González-Block, 2009).

Building on Boswell's (2008) institutional account of expert knowledge, scientific evidence in policymaking can fulfill three main functions (see also Amara et al., 2004; Christensen, 2021): an instrumental function when research directly informs recommendations, problem-solving, and decision-making; a legitimizing function when research is invoked to signal scientific compliance; and a substantiating function when research is selectively mobilized to support preferred positions, particularly when alternative courses of action are under consideration. While the instrumental function reflects substantive use of evidence, the legitimizing and substantiating functions represent symbolic use – either solely (legitimizing) or in combination with substantive elements (substantiating). These functions differ in how use becomes visible. Instrumental use tends to be embedded in problem-solving processes and does not necessarily require external visibility. By contrast, legitimizing use emphasizes visible citation practices, while substantiating use renders research visible only when it reinforces preferred positions (Boswell, 2008).

Collectively, these three functions provide a conceptual foundation for understanding how CPGs engage with research. Historically, CPGs were largely based on expert opinion, which included selective references to scientific evidence (Shaneyfelt et al., 1999). Rather small teams of dedicated volunteers, often working with minimal funding and varying levels of understanding of CPG development methods, sought to produce practice recommendations for local settings (Grilli et al., 2000). More recently, transparently constructed, evidence-informed approaches that integrate expert opinion and patient values have gained acceptance as best practice for CPG development. Although well-credentialed international and national guideline development groups, such as the World Health Organization (WHO), have developed comprehensive development manuals, guideline writing efforts often reflect diverse interpretations of the best way to construct and write CPGs (Kredo et al., 2016). Adding to the complexity, CPG development worldwide employs various tailored approaches to identify, appraise, synthesize, and describe the evidence base underpinning best-practice statements. Recommendations can draw on a range of evidence sources, including peer-reviewed literature, grey literature, other CPGs, and expert opinion. Consequently, there is no standardized approach to any aspect of CPG activity (Kredo et al., 2016; Shaneyfelt et al., 1999), even though approaches have emerged that assist CPG development in not only summarizing the body of evidence for a recommendation and assessing its local relevance but also providing guidance on how to translate evidence into recommendations in a standardized and transparent way (Grilli et al., 2000).

Prior research on the use of scientific knowledge, primarily focused on policy in healthcare, has identified three groups of factors influencing the utilization of research: organizational factors, individual preferences, and barriers (Edler et al., 2022). Organizational factors, such as the presence of knowledge brokers, adequate resources, and a supportive organizational culture, are positively associated with the uptake of scientific knowledge (Belkhodja, 2014). Equally important are individual traits of the actors involved, including their background and social capital. For example, policymakers with advanced degrees or academic work experience are more likely to utilize research (Jennings Jr. and Hall, 2011). Policymakers also vary in their openness to research from different sources and employ different types of research in substantive or symbolic ways (Amara et al., 2004). Moreover, research use may encounter barriers such as ideological differences, variations in risk perception, lack of contact, and differing time frames for knowledge demand and supply (Almeida and Báscolo, 2006).

Obviously, studying the dynamics that lead guideline writers to utilize scientific evidence is a complex and empirically challenging task. Instead of focusing on the individuals involved in the CPG development process, we therefore take a different approach and seek to identify how scientific evidence is utilized in CPGs to fulfill certain functions. In the following, our analysis first seeks to identify when CPGs give textual credit to the research documented in an article. Then, by crossing formal citations with textual credit and in follow-up conversations with guideline writers, we seek to uncover the correlates and dynamics of research utilization, focusing on characteristics of the research articles as well as characteristics of the guideline development process, organizational factors, individual preferences of guideline writers, and practical barriers that shape guideline development.

### 3. Methods

### 3.1. Data

We use textual data taken from scientific articles in journals publishing diabetes research as well as from CPGs informing physicians, healthcare providers, and patients about the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The starting point for this dataset is a set of the most important international CPGs for diabetes published in English language between 2004 and 2016. This list was identified by a panel of diabetes experts as part of a joint effort with the Novo Nordisk Foundation. The Novo Nordisk Foundation is one of the largest private research funding organizations in the world that awards research grants mainly in the health and life sciences with a focus on diabetes research (Novo Nordisk Foundation, 2017). The list contains diabetes guidelines issued by international (e.g., World Health Organization), European (e.g., European Association for the Study of Diabetes), US based (e.g., American Diabetes Association), and UK based (e.g., National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) healthcare and diabetes associations. It includes updated versions of CPGs when the update occurred during our period of analysis. Our final sample includes 21 CPG documents. The full list of CPGs used is available in Appendix 1.

We examine these CPGs for citations to all published journal articles that resulted from research projects funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation between 2000 and 2014 and identify 81 journal articles that were cited at least once in one of the 21 CPGs. We restrict our analysis to these cited articles – and do not use all articles that resulted from research projects sponsored by the foundation – for two reasons. First, this approach ensures a minimum level of relevance of the underlying research to clinical practice. Second, the calibration and validation of our NLP approach require a manageable set of articles. Furthermore, we only consider original studies and reviews/meta-analyses and therefore excluded opinion papers or editorials that were still related to some sort of funding provided by the Novo Nordisk Foundation (the full list of journal articles used is available in Appendix 2). The articles in our sample were published in general medical journals such as the New England Journal of Medicine or Lancet as well as in diabetes field-specific journals such as Diabetes Care or Diabetologia. The resulting dataset then consists of pairs of articles and guidelines, i.e., each observation indicates a combination of one of the 81 articles and one of the 21 CPGs. Obviously, we only consider article-guideline pairs for which articles can logically have an impact on a guideline, i.e., they were published prior to or in the publication year of the guideline. This leads to a sample of 1302 article-guideline pairs or dyads.

We supplement the textual data from articles and guidelines with three additional data sources. First, we invited 12 highly experienced guideline writers from the US, UK, Europe, and Africa to evaluate the 81 journal articles in our sample with respect to their actual patient impact, i.e., the real-world impact of the research on the lives of patients and caregivers or on medical practice. Second, we gather data on the number of citations each article receives in the eight years following its publication, using Google Scholar to track citations in subsequent scholarly literature. This time period is selected to match the maximum number of years we can observe for the most recent articles in our sample published in 2014. Third, we conduct in-depth follow-up interviews with three high-level experts who also participated in the patient impact rating. Based on a semi-structured interview guide, we collect insights on the guideline development process and on potential explanations for the different research utilization patterns in CPGs.

Because our main analysis is based on a limited set of articles related to projects funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation for which we can collect a host of additional information, we seek to replicate our analysis with a large out-of-sample dataset to address potential selection biases and to provide consistency checks for the observed patterns. This dataset includes all original research articles, review papers, and meta-analyses published in Diabetes Care, the leading diabetes field journal, from January 2017 to March 2025. This period is fully independent from our main sample, and the selection is not restricted by any funding source or citation status, resulting in 2313 articles. We then construct all possible article-guideline pairs using the most recent CPGs from four major diabetes-related organizations: the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology Clinical Practice Guideline (published in October 2022), the ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines (December 2022), the ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes (October 2023), and the IDF Global Clinical Practice Recommendations for Managing Type 2 Diabetes (April 2025). This yields a sample of 7864 article-guideline dyads (again excluding those dyads where the publication year of the guideline precedes the publication year of the article).

### 3.2. Measures

In the analysis, we seek to explain when CPGs give textual credit to research documented in an article. To identify textual credit, we develop and validate an AI-based approach.

##### 3.2.1. AI model and textual data processing

Following Meng et al. (2024), who use unsupervised interpretable machine learning (Latent Dirichlet Allocation, LDA) to detect topic-specific catchphrases as allusions to particular discoveries, we seek to uncover textual credit to scientific research in CPGs by means of a complementary approach that is based on large neural language models. Unlike LDA, which clusters topics based on probabilistic associations in the text, language model embeddings can capture fine-grained semantic similarity between articles and CPGs (Zhao et al., 2023). This allows us to detect nuanced textual credit to specific articles based on contextual meaning rather than explicit wording or phrasing, providing an adaptable alternative to LDA. Other recent embedding-based approaches, such as the one proposed by Arts et al. (2025), who identify novel scientific ideas through unique linguistic markers, are promising for tracing scientific breakthroughs but are less applicable to our focus on assessing how existing research is used in policy documents.

To explore the feasibility of identifying textual credit in CPGs, we conduct a pilot study using Semantha, $ ^{2} $ a proprietary AI-based text analysis algorithm based on a generic language model trained on everyday language. In this pilot, we design and test a multi-round validation process with various subsamples of article-guideline dyads and different sets of human experts. We conclude that textual credit can be identified by means of measuring the semantic similarity between article and guideline text pairs which requires the definition of a threshold. Setting a threshold provides an operational boundary for interpreting semantic similarity scores as markers of textual credit in a clear and meaningful way. Given the medical context and to increase transparency and reproducibility of our analysis, our main study builds on an open-source language model pretrained on biomedical text data.

Specifically, we use PubMedBERT, a domain-specific variant of the BERT $ ^{3} $ large language model, to process the textual data (Gu et al., 2021). Pretraining large neural language models on unlabeled text has proven to be highly effective for understanding, comparing, and generating text content (Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2020). While the original BERT was trained on general domain corpora such as Wikipedia articles and books, PubMedBERT is pretrained on 14 million article abstracts from PubMed, $ ^{4} $ a comprehensive database of

The first is MPNet, $ ^{5} $ a general-purpose language model that addresses BERT's limitation of neglecting dependencies among predicted tokens (Song et al., 2020). The second is BioBERT, $ ^{6} $ a mixed-domain model combining medical with general and other scientific datasets (Deka et al., 2022).

For our analysis, we determine whether CPGs give textual credit to diabetes research articles by assessing semantic similarity between each

article's abstract $ ^{7} $ as a representation of its core content and the CPG's content at the paragraph level. In cases where the abstract includes general background or methods, we limit the text to the findings and conclusions to ensure the focus remains on the unique insights from each article. Each abstract is treated as a paragraph and then compared against all paragraphs within a CPG. Since CPGs often span multiple topics or subtopics in a single document, focusing on paragraph-level comparisons helps capture meaning at a smaller, more specific level than for an entire document, allowing us to pinpoint relevant content within a guideline without the analysis being diluted by unrelated sections. $ ^{8} $

Given the properties of our paragraph-level analysis, we employ PubMedBERT embeddings, a PubMedBERT-base model fine-tuned using sentence-transformers. $ ^{9} $ To capture context and meaning from the text, these transformers encode each sentence into embeddings, i.e., high-dimensional vectors of real numbers (Mikolov et al., 2015), derived from the language model. This model is constructed so that semantically related words and phrases are close to one another in the vector space, capturing the collective meaning of word combinations and order rather than individual words in isolation. The advantage of this approach is that it is independent of specific wording, as synonyms and alternative phrases yield very similar embeddings.

To detect textual credit to the articles' abstracts in the guidelines' paragraphs, we compute semantic similarity scores for each of the corresponding text pairs. To do so, we first calculate the weighted average embedding for each paragraph (i.e., each abstract and guideline paragraph). Specifically, for a given paragraph consisting of multiple sentences (S1, S2, ..., SN), we assign weights to each sentence proportional to the logarithm of its length and then generate a weighted average of the embeddings for these N sentences. We use these weighted average embeddings to calculate cosine similarity for all possible text pairs. Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors, providing a score that indicates how similar two paragraphs are in terms of their semantic content. The scores, which naturally range from -1 to +1, are scaled by a factor of 100 for interpretability. In this range, +100 indicates perfect similarity, 0 indicates orthogonality or unrelated content, and -100 indicates opposite meaning. Thus, the closer a score is to +100, the higher the semantic similarity between the paragraphs; scores approaching 0 indicate decreasing similarity, while scores approaching -100 reflect an increasing opposition in meaning.

##### 3.2.2. AI model validation and threshold calibration

Building on insights from the pilot study, we follow a three-step validation process to identify a threshold in the level of semantic similarity between a particular CPG and article above which we can assume that guidelines have given textual credit to a particular article. Although the degree of similarity may vary, we ultimately seek to find evidence for whether a CPG actually gives textual credit to an article. Hence, since PubMedBERT produces continuous similarity scores, we need to define a cut-off value below which similarity is not considered meaningful enough to conclude textual credit. To determine this threshold, we compare PubMedBERT's similarity assessments with those provided by different sets of human diabetes experts in the pilot study. Fig. 1 provides an overview of the entire process, including the pilot study phase.

In the first step, we evaluate how PubMedBERT's similarity scores align with the expert ratings to derive an initial threshold. These ratings stem from four experts representing both producers and users of diabetes research and CPGs. From our base sample of 81 journal articles and 21 guidelines, we randomly select 50 text pairs, each consisting of an article abstract and a corresponding guideline paragraph that cites the article. In the pilot study, the experts manually rated the similarity of these pairs on a four-point Likert scale (0 = no similarity to 3 = high similarity). $ ^{10} $ We then use the semantic similarity scores obtained from the PubMedBERT model in a robust linear regression to understand the predictive power of these scores against the expert ratings and compare the Mean Square Error (MSE) with those of the other language models. Post-assessment interviews with the experts provided further insights into what they considered appropriate semantic similarity to reflect textual credit. These discussions confirmed that, for most practical and policy-relevant purposes, determining whether a CPG builds on a certain article is best approached as a binary, yes-or-no decision that reflects the judgments experts themselves make about textual credit. Defining a threshold also enables us to validate and calibrate PubMedBERT's output by applying established machine learning validation tools such as the confusion matrix $ ^{11} $ and then to use that calibration consistently across a larger dataset.

Building on this approach, we apply the confusion matrix to identify the optimal threshold at which PubMedBERT and the other models classify the pairs as semantically similar or not, according to the expert assessments. For the binary classification evaluation, we analyze each model's confusion matrix components (true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives) and calculate a composite metric, the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), to summarize model performance (Chicco and Jurman, 2020; Sokolova and Lapalme, 2009). $ ^{12} $ This metric is used to both compare the models and determine optimal thresholds for classification. Additionally, we compute agreement rates and intercoder reliability between expert assessments and model classifications using Krippendorff's  $ \alpha $ (Krippendorff, 2004).

We find that PubMedBERT demonstrates good alignment with the similarity ratings of the four experts, which we averaged after calculating a Krippendorff's  $ \alpha $ of 0.7044, indicating acceptable consistency for aggregation (Neuendorf, 2002). The robust linear regression yields an MSE of 0.524, and, compared to the alternative models, it outperforms MPNet (MSE = 0.627) while performing comparably to BioBERT (MSE = 0.511). Based on PubMedBERT's confusion matrix, the model's MCC reaches 0.752, with an optimal semantic similarity threshold set at 66.5 (MCC-optimal range: 65.764 to 67.236). This threshold yields a satisfactory Krippendorff's  $ \alpha $ for expert-AI intercoder reliability (0.759), with an 88% expert-AI agreement rate, indicating solid consistency between PubMedBERT's classifications and expert ratings. In comparison, MPNet and BioBERT achieve lower MCCs, with MPNet showing particularly limited intercoder reliability. Table 1 provides full model comparisons.

#### 3.2.3. Textual credit

We measure textual credit in two ways. We construct a dummy variable indicating whether at least one paragraph in a guideline shows high semantic similarity with the article abstract, based on the similarity threshold validated before. Moreover, we calculate the proportion of paragraphs in a guideline that show high semantic similarity with the article's abstract, based on the similarity threshold. It is calculated by dividing the number of matching paragraphs by the total number of paragraphs in the guideline, indicating the extent to which the article's content is integrated into a guideline.

##### 3.2.4. Utilization patterns

Besides textual credit, we construct three mutually exclusive dummy variables indicating whether a guideline has formally cited an article and/or given textual credit to it (citation and textual credit; token citation; hidden citation), with no formal citation and no textual credit as reference category. Consequently, each article-guideline dyad is classified into one of these four categories. Appendix 3 shows examples for the three utilization patterns.

##### 3.2.5. Article patient impact

We measure the patient impact of the research documented in an article based on an expert assessment by 12 highly experienced

guideline writers in the field of diabetes from the US, UK, Europe, and Africa. The expert raters are selected based on their role as co-authors and/or committee chairs of one or more of the 21 guidelines in our sample, to ensure familiarity with the particular diabetes areas covered in both the guidelines and the articles. Each expert brings over 30 years of experience in clinical practice and research, providing valuable and comprehensive insight into the practical implications of diabetes research across diverse healthcare systems and clinical settings. Using an online survey tool, experts were presented with a selection of articles (full bibliographic information and DOI link) from our 81-article sample, chosen based on whether the articles were cited in one or more guidelines they co-authored and making sure that each article receives at least one expert rating. In the few cases in which this process led to an article being rated by more than one expert, we chose the rating by the most senior expert, based on their self-reported years of involvement in guideline writing. The experts were asked to rate how much impact an article had on the lives of patients and caregivers or on medical practice on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 ("no impact") to 5 ("very high impact"). As such, our measure of patient impact reflects the extent to which research affects the two ultimate stages of societal impact in medical research: improvements in actual medical practice and in patient outcomes (Hanney et al., 2000).

##### 3.2.6. Article scientific impact

We measure the scientific impact of an article as the number of citations it receives in subsequent scholarly literature (cf. Bornmann, 2013; Niederkrotenthaler et al., 2011). Citations are tracked via Google Scholar over an eight-year period following the article's publication date. For robustness, we also consider a five-year window to account for the possibility that certain articles may accumulate citations more quickly after publication, and we use the logarithm of this variable because of its skewed distribution.

#### 3.2.7. Covariates

We control for a number of factors at both the article and guideline level. Review/meta-analysis article is a dummy variable that takes the value of 1 if the article is a review and/or meta-analysis, and 0 otherwise. This distinction is important because review articles and meta-analyses summarize scientific insights and may be used differently in CPGs compared to original studies. We control for the type of diabetes addressed in the article using dummy variables for type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and type 1 and type 2 diabetes. This accounts for the higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes and its potential overrepresentation in our data. We also include time between article and CPG publication (in years) to account for the time that has passed between the article's publication and the publication of the CPG.

At the guideline level, we control for the size of the committee developing a CPG by considering the number of guideline writers, and for the length of the CPG by the total number of CPG paragraphs, as both factors may influence the likelihood that a CPG uses an article. We consider the semantic diversity in a CPG to capture the breadth of content covered. For each paragraph, we calculate how similar each sentence is to the paragraph's weighted average embedding. The deviance is then determined by averaging how much each sentence deviates from that embedding, while giving more weight to longer sentences. The mean deviance across all paragraphs serves as our measure of the guideline's overall semantic diversity. We include year dummies for the year of guideline publication to control for potential time-specific effects that might influence the use of research articles in the guidelines.

In additional analyses, we seek to further explore the role of research orientation and timing effects. Following Chattopadhyay et al. (2025), we design a ChatGPT-4o-based classification prompt that instructs the model to distinguish between basic and clinically applied research based on whether it advances fundamental understanding without immediate applicability or describes work that contributes directly to clinical practice. $ ^{16} $ This yields a binary variable, article basic research, coded 1 for basic and 0 for applied research. As a consistency check, we use the CHI journal-level classification (Hamilton, 2003), which rates journals on a four-point scale from 1 (applied) to 4 (basic research), generating the variable journal basicness. Of the 28 journals in our dataset, 20 have a CHI classification, allowing us to generate a subsample of 1157 article–guideline dyads to assess the influence of research orientation at the journal level. We also include four dummy variables for a journal-level classification by Tijssen (2010). The classification is based on the institutional affiliations of authors publishing in a journal, identifying the share of contributions from clinical, civic, and industrial sectors: (a) clinical-relevant journals that feature some contributions by authors with a general (not university) hospital affiliation, (b) clinical practice journals that have many contributions from authors with a general hospital affiliation, (c) industry-relevant journals where authors often have an industry affiliation, and (d) industry-clinical journals with contributions from both industry and general hospital affiliated authors. Five of the 28 journals represented in our sample were not included in the original classification. Thus, we manually classified them based on a random sample of 50 articles from each journal. The assumption behind this classification is that author affiliations of articles indicate the type and orientation of the research typically published in a journal. Finally, we define long time lag as a binary variable coded 1 if more than eight years passed between article and CPG publication, and 0 otherwise; eight years marks the midpoint of the observed lag distribution (maximum = 16 years).

### 3.3. Analysis

We conduct a series of both logit and OLS regression analyses on the full sample of n = 1302 article-guideline dyads to explore the use of scientific research in CPGs. In a first step, we use the two measures of textual credit (dummy and proportion) in CPGs as dependent variables to understand the factors that influence the actual use of research articles in guidelines. These regressions seek to validate our measure of

semantic similarity between an article's abstract and a guideline to ascertain textual credit as a reflection of an article's societal impact. As further validation, we rerun the regressions on a subsample of n = 1150 dyads where no citation link exists between the article and the guideline to exclude the possibility that textual credit is only driven by an underlying citation link. Furthermore, we use a multinomial logit regression on the full sample of dyads with the three utilization patterns (i.e., citation and textual credit, token citation, hidden citation) as dependent variables to better understand the factors that drive different patterns of research utilization in CPGs.

Next, we complement our quantitative analyses with qualitative insights. While the regressions provide empirical evidence on the correlates of formal citations and textual credit, we aim to develop a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics of research utilization in developing CPGs. To achieve this, we conduct in-depth follow-up interviews with three of the 12 guideline writers who also evaluated a subset of articles on their patient impact. All interviewees are high-level experts who are recognized as leading in the field of diabetes. The first interviewee, based in Sweden, has over 40 years of experience in cardiovascular medicine and diabetes care and has held key leadership roles in developing international CPGs. The second interviewee, based in Tanzania, has more than 30 years of experience in public health policymaking and has worked within global health networks and diabetes management in resource-limited contexts. The third interviewee, based in the United States, brings over 35 years of experience in primary care research and chronic disease management, with extensive expertise in developing CPGs for primary care in diabetes. The interviews were followed a semi-structured approach, inviting interviewees to first describe the process of developing guidelines and then reflect upon potential reasons for the different utilization types of academic articles in CPGs. The interviews lasted between 45 and 60 minutes and were audio-recorded and transcribed, resulting in 80 pages of interview transcripts. These transcripts were first individually coded by two of the authors on potential explanations for different types of research utilization by guideline writers. In several meetings, the two authors then discussed and compared their individual results and categorized them along the lines of potential reasons for citing and using, citing without using, and using without citing research results in CPGs that emerge because of characteristics of the guideline writing process, organizational factors, individual preferences and practical barriers.

In addition to shedding light on patterns of research utilization by guideline writers (as presented in the Results section), the interviews also offered deeper insights into the development of CPGs, highlighting a collaborative and complex process aimed at achieving consensus-driven decisions. ### 4. Results

### 4.1. Descriptive results

Table 2 presents descriptive statistics. We find that 44.9% of the article-guideline dyads include at least one instance in which the CPG has given textual credit to an article. However, content from individual articles is used in only a small portion of the guideline's text, as indicated by the low textual credit ratio of 0.3%. This is expected, as CPGs typically draw on a wide range of articles to develop recommendations across multiple medical areas. Looking at the patterns of utilization in CPGs, in 8.9% of the dyads an article has been formally cited and been given textual credit, while in 2.8% of the dyads an article has been cited but not been given textual credit (token citation). Notably, in 36% of the dyads an article has not been cited but given textual credit, suggesting that hidden citations are a pervasive phenomenon also in policy documents such as CPGs. In 52.3% of the dyads, we find no formal citation and no textual credit to an article.

Interviews with guideline writers suggest potential explanations for this prevalence of hidden citations (Appendix 4). Committee members often integrate insights from ongoing or smaller regional studies without citing them formally, due to time constraints and delays in updating CPGs, space limitations, or the perception that findings are already well known in the field. One interviewee (CPG committee chair), for example, explicitly described how unpublished research was used informally in recommendations: “I knew about important studies ongoing, and I knew that they would be presented very close to when we were releasing [the guideline]. So, I contacted the authors and got a preview of the almost finished manuscript and dug out things which were not changed [and] couldn’t be changed anymore.”

Furthermore, the average score of an article's patient impact is 3.326. The minimum value of 2 suggests that every article in the sample has at least some impact on the lives of patients and caregivers, even if it is low, which is consistent with the fact that all articles in our sample are cited at least once in a CPG and thus carry some practical relevance. An article has received on average 616 scholarly citations, however with considerable variation in our sample. Interviewees confirmed that while highly cited articles often appear in CPGs, patient impact is what determines whether their content is meaningfully incorporated. They emphasized prioritizing studies with strong clinical outcomes, particularly randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses, which facilitate consensus and provide actionable evidence for practice (Appendix 4).

Table 3 presents the pairwise correlations between the variables. None of the correlations among the explanatory variables used in the regressions are particularly high (for the diabetes types, type 2 diabetes will be excluded and serves as the reference category) and the mean variance inflation factor (VIF) is 1.97, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a concern (Belsley et al., 1980). When examining the correlations between the textual credit in CPG and the two types of article impact (scientific and patient impact), we observe that the indicator of textual credit is positively correlated with both an article's scientific impact and patient impact while the textual credit ratio shows a positive correlation with patient impact only. The positive relationship between patient impact and both measures of textual credit provides evidence that semantic similarity between an article's content and CPG paragraphs (which defines textual credit) may indeed reflect societal impact. ### 4.2. Regression results

We first regress our measures of textual credit in CPGs on the key explanatory and control variables, as reported in Table 4. In Model 1, we find a significant positive association between an article's patient impact and the likelihood of receiving textual credit in a CPG (p < 0.001). The average marginal effect indicates that a one-unit increase in patient impact is associated with a 10.9 percentage point increase in the probability that a CPG gives textual credit to the article. This suggests that articles with a greater impact on patients and caregivers are more likely to have their content used in CPGs. Similarly, Model 2 shows a positive relationship between patient impact and the textual credit ratio in CPGs (p < 0.01), indicating that the greater the patient impact of an article, the more extensively its content is integrated into the guideline. This finding resonates with interviewees' descriptions of the consensus-driven guideline process, which tends to prioritize high-evidence studies with clear and direct benefits for patients. As summarized in Appendix 4, committees especially value evidence that demonstrates clinical applicability across diverse settings (on top of other factors such as rigor or transparency), which helps explain why the content of high patient-impact studies is extensively reflected in CPGs (beyond being cited).

When we focus on the subsample analysis in Models 3 and 4, we again find significant and positive associations between patient impact and textual credit, even when no explicit citation link exists between the article and the CPG. Specifically, the subsample results suggest that the effect of patient impact on textual credit is not driven by an underlying citation link but can also occur without formal citations. These findings demonstrate that the impact of an article's research on the lives of patients and caregivers – who are the societal end users and beneficiaries of the research (Smit and Hessels, 2021) – substantially explains the semantic reflection of that research in at least one CPG paragraph, even in the absence of a citation link and after controlling for other relevant factors. This confirms that the semantic matches identified using PubMedBERT are valid indicators of societal impact. Our interviewees further support this interpretation: research is sometimes used without citation due to practical and organizational constraints, such as the need to streamline references or adapt guidelines to local resource settings, but not because of a lack of patient impact. In fact, smaller, context-specific studies with high (local) patient impact often inform recommendations without being cited, reflecting the committee's reliance on local evidence and expert consensus (Appendix 6). This illustrates how semantically detected matches indeed capture the societal relevance of such research, even when not visible in formal citations.

Interestingly, we find that the relationship between an article's scientific impact and textual credit in CPGs is not significant, except in Model 4, where we even find a slightly negative association. Apparently, scholarly citations – beyond the actual patient impact – do not explain when guideline writers give textual credit to an article. Qualitative insights from the interviews suggest that one reason for this may be that highly cited, historically significant studies are often only cited to build legitimacy rather than to inform recommendations (Appendix 5). One interviewee reflected on this practice: "[A study from 1998] showed for the first time that glucose control is good for patients with type 2, and it suggested a benefit to [the drug] Metformin... [Such studies] are going to continue to be cited even though science has moved on."

Considering the covariates, we find that review or meta-analysis articles as well as a guideline's semantic diversity are positively associated with both the incidence and the extent of textual credit, whereas the time elapsed between the publication of the article and the guideline as well as the length of the guideline are positively correlated with the incidence of textual credit only. Conversely, we find that type 1 diabetes is negatively correlated with both the incidence and extent of textual credit while the number of guideline writers is negatively correlated with the extent of textual credit only. These results are intuitively plausible and some of them also align with the interview insights. Review or meta-analysis articles summarize a larger body of research and are therefore more likely to be used by guideline writers (Appendix 6). Guidelines that address a greater variety of topics are more likely to reflect research more broadly. This semantic diversity may arise from the inclusion of multiple medical disciplines, the need to tailor recommendations to various clinical settings, evolving medical terminology, differing expressions of recommendation strength, and ongoing debates in certain areas. Such factors likely enhance the range of content that is semantically aligned with research articles, resulting in greater textual credit in CPGs. Articles that were published longer time ago have a higher chance of being noticed by guideline writers while longer guidelines simply offer greater opportunities for articles to be featured. Type 1 diabetes research features less prominently in guidelines because of its lower prevalence in the population compared to type 2 diabetes. Finally, the more guideline writers are involved in the process, the more "competition" and negotiation there is for research to be featured in a guideline. This reflects how collaborative, consensus-driven processes within committees often require balancing organizational and political dynamics, which can sometimes lead to evidence not being textually credited but only cited to satisfy these dynamics (Appendix 5).

We now turn to the multinomial regression results which provide insights into the factors that shape incorporating scientific research into CPGs, across the three utilization patterns cited/textual credit, cited only (token citation), and textual credit only (hidden citation) (Table 5). Interestingly, we find that the three utilization patterns associate with patient and scientific impact in different ways. As expected, we find a positive and significant relationship between both the patient and scientific impact of an article and the first utilization pattern, indicating both a citation and textual credit. Then again, research is cited but not given textual credit (token citation) when its scientific impact, but not its patient impact is high. Conversely, textual credit is given but without a citation (hidden citation) when an article's patient impact, but not its scientific impact is high. These results indicate that the three utilization patterns reflect different functions of articles in CPGs regarding an article's patient and scientific impact. $ ^{17} $ Interviewees described this differentiation in organizational terms: token citations often serve to maintain credibility, broaden the citational scope, or satisfy stakeholders, whereas hidden citations reflect pragmatic adaptations to local contexts or time and space constraints (Appendix 5 and 6). They also noted that external reviewers sometimes recommend including specific studies to demonstrate that the committee considered the full breadth of evidence, even when these studies do not ultimately shape the recommendations. Interviewees mentioned that token citations often reflect inconclusive or controversial evidence, being included to acknowledge ongoing debates and provide readers with a broader view of the literature. One interviewee explained the logic behind token citations as follows: "You basically have to cite a lot of literature because the issue isn't resolved. [...] You have to say, look, this is a controversial area. There's been lots of stuff. There's been all these meta-analyses, there's a Cochrane review, there's policy statements from different organizations, and you'd put those in there so that somebody who's interested in the topic can sort of go into the literature."

Focusing on the covariates, we find that review and meta-analysis

articles are significantly more likely to receive textual credit only, but they show no significant relationship with the other two utilization patterns, suggesting that formal citations are reserved for original studies. For diabetes types, we can observe that articles addressing type 1 diabetes are significantly less likely to receive textual credit only, and they show no significant association with the other two utilization patterns. This indicates that type 1 diabetes articles are less likely to be used without citation compared to research on type 2 diabetes. The time elapsed between article and CPG publication shows a significant and positive association with hidden citations but not with the other utilization patterns, suggesting that older articles are more likely to be used without formal citation. This aligns with interview accounts of the gradual absorption into common knowledge (Appendix 6): as evidence matures into widely accepted practice, it continues to inform guidelines without explicit reference. At the same time, external reviewer suggestions sometimes drive token citations of newer but inconclusive studies simply to show that the guideline considered the full debate (Appendix 5).

This is also in line with earlier research on hidden citations that suffer from “obliteration by incorporation” (Meng et al., 2024), i.e., the research documented in an article has become an integral part of broader clinical knowledge over time. While we find no significant association between the number of guideline writers and the three utilization patterns, the total number of CPG paragraphs turns out to be positively associated with the patterns “cited and textual credit” as well as “textual credit only”, indicating that longer guidelines are more likely to integrate article content. Finally, semantic diversity in CPGs has a significant positive association with the same two patterns, suggesting that CPGs covering a broader range of topics are more likely to incorporate research article content, either with or without an explicit citation.

### 4.3. Supplementary analyses on research orientation and proliferation

To add nuance to our analysis, we examine how different research orientations (i.e., basic or applied) relate to utilization patterns in CPGs. The table in Appendix 7 provides full results. Focusing on the ChatGPT-based classification of articles into basic or applied research (Model 1), we find that basic research is negatively associated with hidden citations but unrelated to the other utilization patterns. This finding is consistent with the previous observation that the patient impact of an article and hence more applied research positively relates to hidden citations. Accordingly, guidelines seem to rely on applied research directly relevant to clinical practice without formally citing it. The consistency check using the journal-level measure supports this pattern, with journal-basedness negatively associated with hidden citations (Model 2). Interviewees confirmed that committees rely heavily on applied research for its direct relevance to clinical decision-making. They described how smaller, practice-oriented studies, even if not widely recognized academically, often inform recommendations in ways that are not formally credited (Appendix 6). While there is no direct link to basic research in the interviews, interviewees noted that landmark or historic studies are often explicitly cited to acknowledge their foundational role, even when newer evidence has overtaken their clinical relevance (Appendix 5).

Moving beyond the distinction between basic and applied research, we examine the journal application domain classification (Tijssen, 2010) to capture more fine-grained orientations of scientific research (Model 3). We include three dummy variables in the multinomial regression indicating whether an article was published in a clinically relevant journal, a clinical practice journal, or an industry-relevant journal, with industry-clinical journals being the reference category. While none of the journal categories significantly associates with the citation with textual credit pattern, articles in industry-relevant journals are positively associated with token citations and hidden citations, suggesting that guideline writers may either cite industry contributions without textual credit or give textual credit without citation. Similarly, articles in clinical-relevant journals are positively associated with hidden citations. By contrast, articles in clinical practice journals, with a high share of contributions from general (non-university) hospitals, show no significant association with any utilization pattern, suggesting that strong non-academic clinical involvement alone does not increase the likelihood of citation and/or textual credit. These findings align with interview insights on organizational and political considerations. Committee members sometimes cite studies linked to influential stakeholders or interest groups to enhance legitimacy and credibility, even when these studies do not directly inform recommendations (token citations, Appendix 5). At the same time, insights from local or practice-oriented studies may "quietly" shape recommendations without being cited, as they are often treated as either "common knowledge" or valued for their practical impact rather than academic recognition (Appendix 6).

Moving to the role of the time dimension, the results of the main analysis show a positive association between the time elapsed between an article's publication and the corresponding CPG and both textual credit (Table 4) and hidden citations (Table 5). This suggests that the actual use and uptake of new scientific knowledge in CPGs takes time. We expand this discussion of knowledge proliferation in two ways, adding a new variable that indicates a long time lag and introducing interaction terms between article basic research and the two time-related variables to test whether the research orientation of an article changes the pattern of knowledge proliferation. Appendix 8 shows the results. We find no significant interaction effect when the continuous time measure is used (Model 1). Introducing the variable indicating a long time lag in Model 2, we find qualitatively consistent results with the continuous measure of time. Model 3 then shows the corresponding interaction effects. We find that basic research articles with a long time lag are significantly more likely to be cited and textually credited, suggesting that basic research may take longer to develop into practical contributions and to justify recommendations adopted in CPGs. In contrast, basic research articles with a long time lag are very unlikely to serve as a token citation. As the large negative interaction coefficient in Table R9 likely reflects data sparsity for this combination rather than a substantive effect, we do not interpret this coefficient on its own but additionally compute predictive margins to estimate the average predicted probability of token citations across all combinations of the two variables. These margins confirm a close-to-zero predicted probability for the combination of basic research and long time lag ( $ 9.11 \times 10^{-9} $, p = 0.058). Conversely, basic research with a short time lag is marginally more likely to receive a token citation (4.2% predicted probability, p = 0.071), indicating that recent basic research may only be cited without being fully integrated into recommendations yet. For hidden citations, the interaction effect is not significant, suggesting that basic research, regardless of how old it is, is generally not used in CPGs without a formal citation.

In another supplementary analysis, we examine how an article's scientific impact, measured in terms of scholarly citations, interacts with the article's research orientation. While scientific impact often builds over time, it is highly skewed, with some articles garnering many citations while many others receiving few. Appendix 9 presents the results. For basic research articles, higher scientific impact is associated with lower odds of being cited and given textual credit and higher odds of receiving token citations. There is no significant interaction effect for hidden citations. Accordingly, it is those basic research articles that are highly cited in the academic literature that are used as token citations. Consistent with the main results, however, it is applied research with high patient (and not scientific) impact that associates with the actual use in a CPG.

#### 4.4. Robustness and sensitivity checks

To assess the robustness and sensitivity of our results, we perform several additional analyses. $ ^{18} $ First, we examine the consistency of the findings across alternative specifications for all regression models. The results remain consistent when we (a) rerun the analyses by restricting the PubMedBERT model to only consider paragraphs with more than one sentence to prevent single bullet points or long headings from being misinterpreted as paragraphs; (b) use the logarithm of the number of scholarly citations as a measure of scientific impact or a five-year window for that measure; and (c) apply two different semantic similarity thresholds to distinguish textual credit from no textual credit (mid-range = 67 and lower end = 66.5), both derived from the MCC-optimal threshold range established during our validation rounds.

Second, we explore the sensitivity of varying thresholds beyond the MCC-optimal range. Regarding the distribution of cases across utilization patterns, we find that lowering (increasing) the threshold increases (decreases) the proportion of cases with citation and textual credit as well as hidden citations, while reducing (increasing) the proportion of token citations. Choosing a threshold equal to or lower (higher) than 61.5 (77.5) renders the observed relationships between scientific and patient impact and the utilization patterns partly insignificant. Accordingly, these sensitivity checks indicate that the conclusions drawn from our main analysis hold even in the case of substantial variation in the chosen threshold.

Third, in order to abstract away from the specifics of our research context, we conduct an out-of-sample analysis using 7864 article-guideline dyads to address potential selection biases in our main sample. Since the main dataset includes only articles cited in at least one CPG, it may overrepresent instances of citations with textual credit or token citations, while underrepresenting uncited yet potentially influential articles. Moreover, all articles in the main sample are linked to projects funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation, whose emphasis on societal impact and stakeholder engagement may increase both the likelihood of formal citation and the chance of hidden citation through early sharing with guideline writers. Finally, the relatively small number of articles included in the main sample raises the risk of missed citation-textual credit matches, potentially leading to misclassified hidden citations. The out-of-sample analysis mitigates these concerns by drawing on a larger and more representative corpus of articles that is independent of citation status or funding source, thereby allowing for a broader identification of research utilization patterns in CPGs. It includes all original research articles, review papers, and meta-analyses published in Diabetes Care, the leading diabetes field journal, from January 2017 to March 2025.

Appendix 10 shows that utilization patterns in the out-of-sample data closely mirror those in the main sample: hidden citations remain the most frequent type, followed by citations with textual credit, and token citations. The lower proportions of the latter two in the out-of-sample data reflect the broader inclusion criteria, which did not require an article to be cited in a CPG. Hidden citations still account for roughly one third of cases, suggesting that their prevalence is not substantially affected by relaxing the citation-based sampling criterion or by removing potential influence from the specific funding source. More broadly, the results indicate that hidden citations are a relatively common and stable phenomenon across different samples. The same consistency holds when narrowing the comparison to cases involving only textual credit (with and without citation) and those involving only citations (with and without textual credit).

### 5. Discussion

In this paper, we provide novel insights into the patterns of scientific research utilization in policy documents, particularly in clinical practice guidelines for diabetes. We highlight the complexities of the guideline writing process, investigating how scientific evidence is utilized by guideline committees composed of experts to shape its societal impact. Developing and validating an NLP approach, we identify when CPGs give textual credit to scientific research to ascertain when guideline writers have actually used and translated scientific research in policy recommendations. Crossing instances of textual credit with the occurrence of formal citations, we identify three patterns of research utilization: citation and textual credit (a research article is formally cited and its insights inform the guideline recommendations), token citation (a research article is cited but does not directly inform the guideline recommendations), and hidden citation (a research article informs the guideline recommendations but is not cited). We find that these utilization patterns correlate with article and guideline characteristics in different ways, suggesting different functions that these articles assume in guidelines. In particular, we find that giving textual credit to scientific research strongly associates with its impact on patients' lives and on health care practices, while formal citations are often related to an article's scholarly impact. This implies that hidden citations indicate evidence with high relevance to practice, whereas token citations appear to provide guidelines with scientific credibility rather than substantive input. We also find that basic research rarely shows up in hidden citations and may take longer to justify recommendations in CPGs. Moreover, only basic research that is highly cited in the academic literature turns out to be used as token citations.

In three interviews with high-level guideline writers, we probe deeper into the underlying dynamics and explore the role of process characteristics, organizational factors, individual preferences, and barriers that shape the development of clinical practice guidelines. The qualitative insights offer possible explanations for the utilization patterns identified quantitatively. For example, citing and using research may be linked to both the perceived evidence hierarchy and the need to build consensus in the guideline writing process. Token citations might reflect external pressures to enhance legitimacy or to show that a broad range of studies was considered. Hidden citations, in turn, could stem from practical constraints, local adaptations, or reliance on the committee's collective expertise. The interviews further suggest that the strong association between giving textual credit to scientific research and patient impact reflects a deliberate prioritization of actionable, patient-centered evidence over academically prestigious studies with limited clinical relevance. Interviewees noted that high-impact journals can lend credibility but may not necessarily inform practical recommendations unless they directly improve patient outcomes.

Our findings contribute to ongoing debates about the measurement of societal impact by challenging the reliance on formal citations alone as indicators of influence in policy settings. While citations have long been used as proxies for scientific impact in scholarly contexts (MacRoberts and MacRoberts, 1989; Sinatra et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2013), our results indicate that using citations to scientific research in policy documents may either under- or overestimate its true societal impact. Guideline committees sometimes include token citations to satisfy expectations of comprehensiveness or to acknowledge influential stakeholders, even if the cited research does not directly shape recommendations. This finding complements prior research on rhetorical citations in the scholarly literature that exhibit little influence on the citing author (Teplitskiy et al., 2022). However, while these types of citations are dominant in scientific literature, our results indicate that token citations in CPGs are relatively infrequent.

Conversely, some research is used but not cited, either because it is seen as common knowledge or because reference lists are intentionally streamlined for brevity. The identification of hidden citations in policy documents aligns with observations of similar phenomena in scholarly contexts (Meng et al., 2024), but our findings suggest that the underlying dynamics differ in policy settings. While hidden citations in journal articles often arise because findings have been fully absorbed into

common knowledge (“obliteration by incorporation”), our results indicate that in CPGs, hidden citations can also result from process-related and organizational factors. For example, hidden citations sometimes arise when committees rely on unpublished or locally specific studies that are relevant for practice but not widely disseminated. Time pressures and bureaucratic delays in updating guidelines can additionally lead to the informal use of evidence awaiting publication.

As such, the reliance on formal citations alone may misrepresent the actual role of scientific research in shaping policy, a limitation that our NLP approach helps to address by recognizing textual credit even in the absence of a citation. Our study sheds light on the more nuanced dynamics involved in the utilization of scientific research within policy documents, as guidelines adopt scientific knowledge based on a variety of factors. The interviews with senior guideline writers furthermore reveal that hidden citations are often a function of practical constraints in the guideline writing process, such as limitations in reference lists, or in the specific healthcare context the guidelines are to be applied, reflecting adaptations necessary for specific regional contexts or resource endowments. In addition, individual preferences for relying on experiential knowledge and the related expert consensus within the guideline committee may result in the omission of formal citations, even if the recommendations are supported by existing research. Moreover, the inclusion of token citations points to additional considerations, such as organizational demands for credibility or alignment with influential stakeholders. These patterns extend findings from studies suggesting that policymakers' interpretation and use of scientific evidence involve complex sensemaking processes (Edler and James, 2015; Edler et al., 2022).

From the perspective of institutional theory, our research suggests that institutional pressures shape not only whether scientific knowledge is taken up in policy, but also how this use becomes visible. While institutional accounts often imply that legitimacy may be secured by signaling adherence to appropriate norms and procedures (Suchman, 1995; Boswell, 2008), our results suggest that substantive use can remain largely invisible (hidden citations), whereas symbolic use may be highly visible (token citations), i.e., visibility does not reliably indicate influence. Recognizing visibility as part of how legitimacy is constructed (Scott, 1991) helps explain why citation counts may misrepresent the actual contribution of scientific research to policy.

Our results also indicate that different institutional influences are associated with distinct patterns of research use, suggesting that institutional conditions may shape not only whether research is used, but also how this use becomes visible in our context. Scientific domains and audiences that emphasize scholarly credibility appear linked to token citations, while settings that prioritize clinical relevance and patient outcomes are linked to hidden citation and substantive integration (Amara et al., 2004; Boswell, 2008; Christensen, 2021). While token citations and hidden citations reflect these contrasting institutional logics, citations with textual credit can arise in both types of settings, i.e., serving scholarly credibility when rigor is central and substantive integration when clinical relevance and patient outcomes are prioritized. This suggests that institutional conditions do not simply determine whether research is used, but they shape the form and visibility of that use. Taken together, these findings point to heterogeneous legitimacy pressures and strategies, opening space for further work on how different policy settings use scientific knowledge.

Our research underscores the importance of knowledge utilization determining the societal impact of scientific research, particularly against the backdrop of the collaborative and iterative nature of CPG development, in which individual preferences, organizational dynamics, and external pressures all shape how evidence is selected and presented. This perspective contrasts with traditional models that center on scientists' engagement and communication efforts (D'Este and Robinson-García, 2023), shifting the focus to how policymaking adopts scientific knowledge (Edler et al., 2022). Considering the role of knowledge utilization has implications for performance-based funding and evaluation systems, which typically assess researchers based on explicit engagement activities and citation counts in policy documents (Wilkinson, 2019). The nuanced patterns observed here suggest that these metrics need to evolve to account for hidden forms of knowledge absorption and informal integration of research into policymaking (hidden citations) and to address potential overestimations of societal impact for certain studies, such as those published in high-impact journals, used in an "encyclopedic" manner to provide foundational context, or written by influential authors (token citations).

Our findings point to practical implications for policymaking in a broader sense, including guideline development within healthcare as well as policy processes in governmental settings. National and supranational (e.g., EU) policy processes could, for example, improve transparency by requiring brief evidence explanations, indicating how research informed each recommendation, rather than relying on citation lists alone. Advisory committees could also adopt procedures that explicitly record both formally cited and substantively used evidence. For instance, EU Expert Groups or the Expert Panel on effective ways of investing in health (DG SANTE – Directorate-General for Health & Food Safety) could introduce standard “evidence rationale” fields in their guidance templates to ensure substantive use is captured. Similar documentation principles could also be considered in the context of ERA policy initiatives that seek to strengthen evidence-informed policymaking and improve comparability across policy domains and EU Member States.

From a methodological perspective, the application of an AI-assisted approach in this study demonstrates the potential of NLP models like PubMedBERT in broadening the scope of impact assessments by detecting hidden and token citations, complementing recent work on the promise of AI in science (e.g., Extance, 2018; Jumper et al., 2021). The capacity of AI to handle large document corpora can improve the assessment of societal impact by identifying and analyzing implicit forms of research (non-)use that are otherwise difficult to capture. Interviewees indicated that even within committees, it is not always clear which studies ultimately shaped recommendations, suggesting that NLP techniques could improve transparency by highlighting implicit links between research and policy documents.

### 6. Limitations and future research

Our work contributes to the literature on the utilization of scientific research in policymaking and on societal impact assessment by highlighting the prevalence and significance of hidden and token citations and the contextual factors that shape research utilization in policy. In addressing our research question, we show that research use follows distinct patterns – citation and textual credit, token citation, and hidden citation – that differ in both their visibility and substantive influence. Our findings underscore that assessing societal impact via policy documents requires moving beyond mere citation counts, capturing the diverse ways in which policymakers search for and absorb scientific research to derive policy decisions and formulate the respective policy recommendations. Nevertheless, our work is not without limitations that in turn offer opportunities for future research.

First, while our NLP approach provides an innovative way to identify hidden and token citations, the model's accuracy in identifying textual credit may vary depending on the language used in policy documents and the specificity of the research references. Future work could enhance the model's adaptability to diverse policy contexts and document types. Second, even though we demonstrate robustness of our findings for a wide variety of thresholds, our approach requires threshold calibration using an expert validation process. Future research could explore how threshold-free methods, such as that used by Arts et al. (2025) who identify novel scientific contributions by detecting linguistic markers appearing for the first time in the literature, could complement our approach. This could offer valuable insights into when and how radically new findings are adopted in policy documents, using

our utilization patterns as a framework. Third, our main analysis is based on a sample of scientific articles resulting from projects funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation that are reflected in clinical practice guidelines in diabetes. While this allows us to obtain fine-grained data on the individual articles and to carefully assess the relationship between articles and guidelines in a policy context that heavily relies on scientific evidence, our approach arguably limits the generalizability of our results. On the one hand, we face selection effects since we only consider articles as outcomes of projects funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation. We counter this concern using an analysis of out-of-sample data that yields consistent results. Moreover, the vast majority of published academic research is the result of externally funded research and hence selected according to scientific merit of the idea and capabilities of the investigators. On the other hand, the assessment of policy impact in CPGs and, more specifically, CPGs in diabetes, may complicate transferring our results to a different medical context or a different type of policy document than clinical guidelines. However, we believe that our results are highly informative of the general processes how policy documents use scientific research and engage with the academic literature.

Future research could build on our insights to explore hidden and token citation dynamics across various policy domains – such as environmental policy, social policy, or economic regulation – to provide broader insights into how scientific research is integrated across diverse fields. More broadly, our findings invite future work to examine how institutional pressures and logics shape both the visibility and the substantive influence of scientific evidence across diverse policy domains and forms of evidence beyond clinical guidelines. The interviews we conducted, while limited in number, provide some indications for how policymaking absorbs scientific research. But more research is needed to explore the mechanisms in contexts in which experts and expert committees work under the auspices of a governing institution that itself may follow a certain political agenda (Edler and James, 2015).

Finally, we acknowledge that our research does not consider patents as possible outcomes of scientific research that could lead to effective treatment options, nor altmetrics that would indicate the extent to which research results have been communicated (D'Este and Robinson-García, 2023). Future research could investigate whether and how these aspects influence the uptake of scientific research in policy documents.
