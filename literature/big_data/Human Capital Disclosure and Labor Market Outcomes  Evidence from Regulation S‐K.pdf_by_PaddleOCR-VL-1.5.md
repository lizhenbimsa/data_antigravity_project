# Human Capital Disclosure and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from Regulation S-K

## ABSTRACT

We examine the labor market consequences of the 2020 Regulation S-K requiring human capital disclosure in 10K filings. Using large-sample job-level data and a Generative Large Language Model (GLLM), we observe that public firms subject to the regulation increase their disclosure of diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) information in job postings relative to a matched sample of large private firms. The increase in job-posting disclosure is more pronounced among firms facing greater external pressure to increase their workforce diversity. These findings suggest a shift in demand for diverse candidates by public firms following the regulation. Yet, consistent with

short-term inelastic labor supply, this demand shift lengthens the recruitment period, with noticeable increases in workplace gender diversity emerging one year after the regulation, particularly among firms that demonstrate a credible commitment to DEI. Our study documents how securities regulations can impact labor market practices and underscores the challenges involved in shaping workforce diversity.

## 1. Introduction

Securities regulations, such as those established by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), are developed to protect investors and maintain the fair and efficient functioning of financial markets. Since the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, the SEC has begun imposing environmental and social disclosure requirements, with notable consequences for firm behavior (e.g., Christensen et al. [2017], Baik et al. [2024]). One salient example is the 2020 Regulation S-K, which requires public companies to provide human capital disclosure (HCD) in 10K filings. Regulation S-K confirms a significant move by the SEC towards matters that could influence not only public firms and their investors (Arif, Yoon, and Zhang [2024]) but also other stakeholders (Christensen, Hail, and Leuz [2021]). We contribute to the research on the effects of such disclosure requirements by studying the labor market consequences of human capital disclosure in 10Ks.

Prior to Regulation S-K, despite some investors considering HCD important for their investment decisions (Heath et al. [2023]), public companies were only required to disclose the number of employees and the CEO-to-employee pay ratio in their 10K filings and proxy statements. $ ^{1} $ To fill this disclosure gap, some investors petitioned the SEC to require firms to disclose information about their human capital management policies (e.g., HCMC [2017], Sheehan [2017]. In 2020, the SEC amended Regulation S-K, providing a clear signal that it considers HCD important for market participants. Including HCD in 10Ks would make such information more credible, given auditor oversight and the risk of SEC enforcement actions, and would reduce information acquisition costs for investors (Blankespoor, deHaan, and Marinovic [2020]).

The regulation was conceived as a principles-based disclosure mandate that gives firms discretion over which material human capital metrics to disclose. Bourveau et al. [2024] document a dramatic increase in HCD in the first 10Ks after Regulation S-K. While less than 1% of firms had a section dedicated to HCD before the regulation, this rate jumped to 85% after the regulation. The increase was particularly evident in workforce Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) disclosure, which rose from almost zero to 57%.

for S&P 500 firms. $ ^{2} $ In light of these results—and given that labor market activities (e.g., job postings) and outcomes (e.g., hiring) related to DEI are measurable—we focus our examination on DEI.

The labor market consequences of including DEI information in 10Ks are not obvious. On the one hand, firms with lower levels of workforce diversity may anticipate that they will be exposed to greater external pressure to increase their diversity levels after Regulation S-K. This would happen whether such firms chose to be forthcoming and include DEI information in their 10Ks, thereby revealing their status, or chose to omit such information, implicitly signaling their type (e.g., Milgrom [1981]). It follows that Regulation S-K would provide an incentive for these firms to try to increase their actual workforce diversity. To do so, they would include more DEI information in their job postings to signal their commitment to a diverse and inclusive workplace culture, and thereby attract and recruit diverse candidates (Choi et al. [2023a]).

On the other hand, Regulation S-K could also have no impact on DEI information in job postings or workforce diversity, for several reasons. Certain firms might have a weak commitment to increasing workforce diversity despite their claims about DEI (Bailey et al. [2025], Baker et al. [2024]). Furthermore, DEI is a politically contentious matter as it addresses issues related to race, ethnicity, gender, and other social identities historically subject to discrimination. Some individuals may view efforts to promote DEI as a sign that the company gives less weight to meritocratic promotion or rewards (Petersen, Saporta, and Seidel [2000]). Hence, firms might avoid including DEI information in job postings. Even if firms did choose to provide information about their DEI policies in job postings, this might not necessarily lead to increased workforce diversity if job seekers do not perceive such disclosures as credible. More fundamentally, structural labor market conditions, such as limited or inelastic labor supply, may hinder changes in workforce diversity.

We begin our analysis by examining the short-term effects of Regulation S-K on the extent and length of DEI disclosure in a sample of 5,169,408 job postings. We implement a difference-in-differences research design where the treatment group comprises U.S. public firms, which are subject to the regulation, whereas the control group comprises U.S. private firms of similar size, which are not subject to the regulation. We obtain job-posting data from LinkUp, which enables us to analyze a large sample of job vacancies and descriptions, providing valuable insights into the dynamics of the U.S. labor market. To measure the extent and length of DEI disclosure in job postings, we use a Natural Language Processing (NLP) model provided by

RavenPack, specifically Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Classification. This method allows us to identify word phrases that can be categorized as DEI disclosure. We then aggregate job-posting data at the firm-job-type-month level and create a panel data set spanning 11 months and centered on the amendment of Regulation S-K. Our sample includes job postings from 1,891 public and 347 private firms.

We find that public firms increase the extent and length of DEI disclosure in their job postings following the regulation. The dynamic analysis reveals no pre-trends, but after the amendment of Regulation S-K, we observe that public firms increase the length of DEI information in their job postings and increase the number of job postings with DEI information by approximately 1.4 percentage points, which corresponds to a 2.3% increase above the unconditional mean.

We then conduct three cross-sectional tests to shed light on the mechanisms through which Regulation S-K affects DEI disclosure in job postings. Consistent with certain firms anticipating greater external pressure to increase their diversity levels after the regulation, we find that the average effect of Regulation S-K is significantly larger for firms that prior to the regulation have (1) low levels of gender diversity, (2) high levels of ownership by environmental, social, and governance (ESG) funds, and (3) greater analyst attention to DEI issues during conference calls. The substantial variation in the intensity of the treatment effect we observe indicates heterogeneous incentives to respond to Regulation S-K.

We also systematically evaluate our sample of 5.2 million job postings for credible DEI disclosure communication by conducting two distinct sets of analyses. First, we employ a Generative Large Language Model (GLLM) (de Kok [2025]) and find that job postings that include DEI information generally describe employers' credible commitments and inclusive culture. Notably, the GLLM's evaluation of job postings content allows us to distinguish more credibly communicated DEI information from 'boilerplate' DEI statements. We observe that while the use of job postings with credibly communicated DEI commitments increases for treatment firms relative to control firms following the regulation, the use of job postings with boilerplate DEI statements remains unchanged. Second, we find that job postings that include DEI information are associated with higher DEI satisfaction ratings on Glassdoor and stronger DEI performance based on the S&P Global ESG Score database, suggesting that credible DEI communication in job postings aligns with perceptions held by employees and other stakeholders.

While the increased DEI disclosure in job postings indicates a growing demand for diverse candidates, job postings are only the first step in the hiring process. The actual hiring of diverse candidates might not increase immediately. One possible obstacle is that the labor supply is generally inelastic in the short term. When we examine the short-term effect of Regulation S-K on a firm's workforce composition, we indeed do not observe, on average, any statistically or economically significant change in workforce

gender or ethnic/racial diversity for treated firms immediately following the regulation. $ ^{3} $

We conduct two sets of tests to confirm that the absence of an average effect is consistent with limited or inelastic labor supply in the short term (e.g., Welch [1997], Chetty et al. [2011]). First, we explore variation in labor supply by looking at geographic and industry heterogeneity in workforce diversity. We find that gender diversity marginally increases in U.S. states and industries with a larger presence of women candidates, indicating that supply-side structural differences play a salient role. Second, we find that public firms conduct more interviews following the regulation, resulting in longer recruitment times, and extend more job offers, suggesting a commitment to increase diversity. However, we also observe more declined offers, consistent with labor market tightness due to a limited supply of diverse candidates (Muehlemann and Leiser [2018]).

When we extend our examination to two years post-regulation, allowing for adjustments in the labor supply, we observe that treatment firms successfully increase their workforce diversity. The dynamic analysis reveals no preexisting trends, but following Regulation S-K, a slight increase in gender diversity becomes evident four quarters later and persists throughout the remaining quarters examined. We also explore demand-side variation and observe that workforce diversity increases more among firms that exhibit a credible commitment to DEI through the presence of a DEI officer and through a change in their DEI disclosures in job postings. For example, we find that firms with a DEI officer improve gender diversity in senior positions by an average of 2 pp compared with control firms and enhance ethnic/racial diversity in senior positions by an average of 1 pp. These effects are not trivial, as they represent approximately a 5% increase in diversity for treated firms relative to the pre-period.

A possible threat to the validity of our identification strategy is that concurrent events could change hiring incentives for public firms (but not for private firms) in a way that is consistent with our findings but inconsistent with Regulation S-K playing a role. We address this concern in several ways. First, we do not observe any differential trends between public and private firms in the pre-regulation period, so any confounding events have to line up precisely with the regulation amendment and affect only public firms. One potential concern is that the death of George Floyd in May 2020 might influence the labor market outcomes for public firms, given their greater visibility relative to private firms. If that were the case, social movements rather than Regulation S-K would drive our results. We conduct a falsification test by studying a state of the world characterized by strong social movements but no changes in securities regulations: the emergence of the

#MeToo movement in October 2017. We find that public firms' job-posting DEI disclosures do not change relative to those of private firms following the #MeToo movement, which is inconsistent with the concern that social movements, rather than Regulation S-K, are driving our results.

Taken together, our findings show that certain firms responded to Regulation S-K by changing their labor market disclosure strategies in order to attract diverse candidates. However, increasing workforce diversity in the short term is challenging. A limited labor supply of diverse candidates for certain jobs and the time required to recruit and hire candidates for different occupations make it difficult to significantly increase diversity in the short-term. Indeed, we observe that meaningful changes begin to emerge only four quarters after the regulation.

From a big-picture perspective, these findings contribute to the literature by documenting how securities regulations can reach beyond capital markets, potentially shaping labor market outcomes through disclosure. $ ^{4} $ While the capital market effects of securities regulations have been widely studied (Leuz and Wysocki [2016]), research on non-capital market outcomes has only recently emerged. Two recent examples are Christensen et al. [2017] and Baik et al. [2024], who study the real effects of securities regulation related to mine safety and conflict minerals disclosure. Christensen et al. [2017] estimate the incremental effect of including in financial reports information that is already publicly available, whereas Baik et al. [2024] study the effect of disclosing information not previously released elsewhere. We document how Regulation S-K alters firms' incentives in the labor market. Put differently, we examine the real effects of securities regulation on organizational practices, which is increasingly important given the recent global trend towards more ESG reporting (Christensen, Hail, and Leuz [2021]).

Our paper also contributes to recent studies on labor market disclosure such as Choi et al. [2023a], who show that job seekers value workforce diversity information and are more inclined to apply to employers with diverse teams once they are aware of their diversity efforts; Pacelli, Shi, and Zou [2022], who show that including information on workplace culture in job postings helps firms attract and retain employees; and Sran (2021), who examines how firms balance disclosure in job postings, weighing the benefits in the labor market against the costs in product market competition. Our study documents that Regulation S-K has real effects in terms of labor market disclosure and emphasizes the challenges associated with enhancing workforce diversity, an issue also underscored in Sutherland, Uckert, and Vetter [2024].

Finally, our paper relates to recent studies about firms' responses to racial and gender diversity issues (e.g., Billings, Klein, and Shi [2022],

Balakrishnan et al. [2023], Lins et al. [2024], Law and Tan [2025]). These studies focus on specific events (George Floyd's death and the #MeToo movement), whereas we focus on the role of securities regulations in shaping firms' social behavior and examine the broader labor market consequences.

## 2. Institutional Setting and Conceptual Framework

The emergence of a human capital-driven economy in the 20th century has launched an ongoing discussion among academics, practitioners, and policymakers regarding the importance of human capital disclosure (e.g., Zingales [2000]). However, human capital disclosure in the United States has remained relatively scarce until recently (Honigsberg and Rajgopal [2022]). Public companies were mandated to disclose only the number of employees in their 10K filings, and starting in 2018, they were also required to report the ratio of CEO-to-employee pay. Recent studies have highlighted the impact of these disclosures, finding that some investors responded negatively to high within-firm pay dispersion, underscoring broader concerns about pay inequality (Pan et al. [2022]). Furthermore, the disclosure of median employee pay levels has had unintended consequences on employees (Boone et al. 2024; Dambra et al. [2025], Carter et al. [2025a]). This trend highlights both the complexity of human capital disclosure and the evolving expectations of stakeholders regarding corporate disclosure practices.

Apart from these two reporting requirements, there was still considerable information asymmetry between investors, who demanded more human capital information (e.g., HCMC [2017], Sheehan 2017), and companies, which did not voluntarily disclose it. $ ^{6} $ Market forces did not lead to voluntary unraveling (e.g., Grossman and Hart [1980]), possibly because firms were uncertain about investors' responses to HCD (Nagar [1999], Suijs [2007]) or did not gauge whether HCD was value relevant (Arif, Yoon, and Zhang [2024]).

In 2020, the SEC updated its guidelines for business descriptions in Regulation S-K Item 101. This regulation now requires a description of the firm's human capital resources, including disclosure of the number of employees and any human capital measures or objectives that the firm focuses on in managing its business, such as those that address the attraction, development, and retention of employees (SEC [2020]).

On the one hand, this provides a clear signal to firms that the SEC considers human capital disclosure important for market participants. Moreover, including HCD in 10Ks would make such information more credible, given auditor oversight and the risk of SEC enforcement actions, and it would reduce information acquisition costs for investors (Blankespoor, deHaan, and Marinovic [2020]). $ ^{7} $ On the other hand, Regulation S-K does not provide a clear definition of human capital, nor does it mandate specific human capital management policies and metrics to be disclosed. This allows companies the flexibility to select the topics and metrics they consider materially relevant to their business.

Despite this lack of specific guidance by the SEC, Bourveau et al. [2024] document that firms responded unequivocally to Regulation S-K. Before the regulation, less than 1% of firms had a section dedicated to the discussion of human capital issues. In their first 10Ks after the regulation, more than 85% of firms included such a section. Perhaps related to the SEC's reference in the final rule to measures or objectives that address the attraction, development, and retention of personnel (SEC [2020]), the effect is particularly evident in disclosures related to workforce diversity metrics, which increased by 30% on average and up to 57% for S&P500 firms (see the online appendix section V for examples of DEI disclosure in 10Ks).

In light of these findings, we study the labor market consequences of more DEI information in 10Ks. On the one hand, following Regulation S-K, we expect that firms with lower levels of workforce diversity may anticipate greater external pressure from investors, analysts, and other stakeholders to increase their diversity levels. This would happen whether such firms chose to be forthcoming and include DEI information in 10Ks, thereby revealing their status, or chose to omit such information, implicitly signaling their type (e.g., Milgrom [1981]). It follows that Regulation S-K would provide an incentive for these firms to try to increase their workforce diversity. Enhancing workforce diversity, even marginally, would enable them to highlight these changes in the 10K and potentially reduce some pressure. To increase their workforce diversity, firms would begin by including more DEI information in their job postings to signal their commitment.

to promoting a diverse and inclusive workplace culture, and thereby attract and recruit diverse candidates (Choi et al. [2023a], Cai et al. [2024]). Consistent with these arguments, we expect Regulation S-K to result in more DEI disclosure in job postings and, possibly, more workforce diversity. $ ^{8} $

On the other hand, Regulation S-K could also have no impact on DEI information in job postings or workforce diversity, for several reasons. First, certain firms might have a weak commitment to increasing workforce diversity despite their claims about DEI in the 10K (Baker et al. [2024]). Second, firms might avoid including DEI information in job postings and focus on other, less controversial human capital issues. This could occur if, for example, some individuals and groups view efforts to promote diversity as a signal that the company places less weight on meritocratic promotion or rewards, although the relationship remains contentious (Petersen, Saporta, and Seidel [2000], Castilla and Benard [2010], Castilla and Rho [2023]).

Finally, even if firms do provide information about their DEI policies in job postings, it remains unclear whether this would lead to increased workforce diversity. Job seekers might not apply to firms if they do not find the DEI disclosures credible. Even when firms credibly communicate DEI commitments in job postings, translating these commitments into actual hires can be hindered by unconscious biases among individual recruiters or by structural barriers within the firms that impede diverse hiring. More fundamentally, structural labor market conditions, such as limited or inelastic labor supply, may hinder increases in diversity. Recruiting diverse talent can be challenging because the pool of diverse candidates in the economy remains fixed in the short term, and hiring candidates for different occupations takes time. $ ^{9} $

In sum, the interactions between capital market pressures and labor market dynamics make the ex-ante prediction about the labor market effects of Regulation S-K less clear. Limited workforce diversity information and the fact that job search is costly for both job seekers and firms further suggest that the labor market outcomes are far from obvious and are likely to be heterogeneous, depending on labor market conditions and companies' recruiting policies. We leverage our extensive data on public and private firms to empirically test whether Regulation S-K influences labor market disclosure and workforce diversity outcomes.

## 3. Data and Sample Selection

We measure labor market outcomes using novel micro-level data from two large-sample data sets, LinkUp and Revelio Labs. First, we obtain job-posting data from LinkUp, a platform that specializes in aggregating job listings directly from corporate websites. Campello, Kankanhalli, and Muthukrishnan [2023] show that LinkUp data reliably predict job gains from the U.S. Census Bureau's Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI). Furthermore, they highlight a strong correlation between the volume of job postings on LinkUp and the number of total private-sector hires as reported in the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS). The data set has several additional advantages. First, it is unique in the absence of duplicate entries, as it excludes third-party job listings often found on platforms like Indeed or LinkedIn. Second, LinkUp matches each job posting to a specific O*NET code—a standardized classification system for job roles. This allows us to conduct a within-job-type analysis, comparing labor market outcomes for similar occupations. Third, LinkUp provides information about how quickly job positions are filled, which we use when studying the length of the recruitment period. Finally, the data are available for both public and private firms, which allows us to use private firms as a control group in our analyses.

Next, we gather resume data from Revelio Labs, which offers coverage of nearly all LinkedIn profiles starting from 2007 (with some individual profiles containing job histories dating back to the 1970s). As a professional networking platform with over 1 billion profiles, LinkedIn tends to have up-to-date user information, as users maintain their profiles to serve their own professional interests (Jeffers 2024). This data set allows us to measure workforce diversity in terms of employees' race, ethnicity, and gender as Revelio Labs infers those demographics using a machine learning technique (Baker et al. 2024). $ ^{10} $

We match job-posting data from LinkUp and individual resume data from Revelio Labs with public firms in COMPUSTAT based on their GVKEYs and with private firms in FactSet using FactSet ID. For private firms, we start from the sample of firms with sales data available in FactSet. We keep only private firms with more than 100 million in sales (in U.S. dollars), because firm size is a major determinant of disclosure (both financial and ESG disclosures; see Dhaliwal et al. To measure DEI disclosure in job postings, we use RavenPack's NLP algorithm (i.e., NER and Classification), which identifies and classifies entities (i.e., words or word phrases) related to DEI and equal employment opportunities (EEO). Specifically, the RavenPack algorithm detects all meaningful entities in a job description and provides an entity taxonomy covering four different categories: Benefit and Compensation, Duties and Responsibilities, Qualifications, and Employment Policies. We focus on the two subcategories of Employment Policies that are relevant to our research question: Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion and Equal Employment Opportunity.

We calculate the total number of entities belonging to the two subcategories to measure the extent and length of DEI-related disclosure for each job posting. Our DEI measure represents a conservative estimate for the length of DEI disclosure, as firms may add several sentences to discuss DEI, while RavenPack only targets the terms that are most relevant to DEI. $ ^{11} $ To systematically evaluate our sample of 5.2 million job postings for credible DEI disclosure communication, we also employ a GLLM, as discussed in section 4.3 and in section I of the online appendix.

In our cross-sectional analyses, we examine the role of socially responsible investors and analysts. First, to measure firm ownership by socially responsible investors, we follow Choi, Ferri, and Macciocchi [2024b] and obtain mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) portfolio holdings data from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) U.S. Mutual Fund Holdings Database. Within the CRSP universe of funds, we identify socially responsible investors using data on ESG funds from Morningstar's annual Sustainable Funds U.S. Landscape Report. We scale the institutions' reported shares owned by the total number of shares outstanding (from CRSP). To prevent our ownership measures from being confounded by the effect of Regulation S-K on institutional investors' portfolio strategies, we measure holdings for socially responsible funds at the end of June 2020, which is the last quarter before the amendment of Regulation S-K. Second, to determine whether analysts posed DEI-related questions during conference calls, we extract the text of the Q&A sections from the Capital IQ database for calls held during the five-year period preceding the regulation (with an additional analysis of a one-year period for robustness). We then apply RavenPack's NLP algorithm, as described above, to analyze the content and identify whether DEI-related questions were present. Based on this analysis, we categorize firms into two groups: those that received DEI-related questions from analysts and those that did not.

Finally, we obtain the review and interview data from Glassdoor, where users share their interview experiences and outcomes. Glassdoor's "Give-to-Get" policy makes it a relatively credible source of employee information, especially in the presence of disclosure protections (Böke et al. [2024]). We use the review data on employers' review pages to measure employees' DEI sentiment, which ranges from 1 to 5 stars. The interview data are used to measure the monthly total of the inverse hyperbolic sine of the number of interviews for each firm job type, as well as the number of job

offers tendered, accepted, and rejected. $ ^{12} $ Finally, we obtain firms’ DEI performance ratings and equal pay ratings from the S&P Global ESG Score database.

## 4. Regulation S-K and Labor Market Disclosure

### 4.1 DEI DISCLOSURE IN JOB POSTINGS

Our first empirical analysis focuses on examining DEI disclosure in job postings following Regulation S-K. We use a difference-in-differences research design centered on the amendment of Regulation S-K and estimate the following ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model:

 $$ DEI_{ijt}=Treatment_{i}\times POST_{t}+Month FE_{t}+Firm\times Job Type FE_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ijt}. $$ 

We examine two dependent variables— $ d(DEI) $ and  $ IHS(DEI\ Length) $—that measure job-posting disclosure at the firm i, job type j, and month t level to mitigate the impact of monthly fluctuations in certain types of jobs. In particular,  $ d(DEI) $ measures the monthly proportion of job postings with DEI information for each firm-job-type;  $ IHS(DEI\ Length) $ measures the monthly average of the inverse hyperbolic sine of the number of DEI entities used in job postings for each firm-job-type. $ ^{13} $ We report descriptive statistics for our dependent variables in table 2.

### 4.2 EXTERNAL PRESSURE AND THE HETEROGENEITY IN THE TREATMENT EFFECT

As we argue in section 2, the human capital disclosure mandate in Regulation S-K, in expectation, has the potential to increase incentives to enhance workforce diversity, particularly for firms already under intense external scrutiny regarding DEI issues. Thus, certain firms are likely to incorporate DEI information into their job postings with the intent to attract and recruit candidates from diverse backgrounds. In what follows, we examine the cross-sectional variation in pre-regulation levels of external pressure to clarify the mechanisms underlying our results.

We examine three proxies for external pressure: preexisting levels of workforce diversity, ownership by socially responsible investors, and attention from analysts on DEI issues. First, ceteris paribus, firms with low pre-regulation levels of workforce diversity could anticipate greater pressure to increase their workforce diversity (see section 2). Second, firms with high pre-regulation levels of ownership by socially responsible investors could anticipate greater pressure from these investors to increase their workforce diversity. Those firms could also have greater incentives to increase workforce diversity in order to keep (or cater to) those investors. This is consistent with Heath et al. [2023], who show that socially responsible investors prefer firms with greater workforce diversity, greater equity (e.g., career opportunities, work–life balance), and better corporate culture. $ ^{19} $ Third, firms might feel more compelled to increase workforce diversity when DEI issues are brought up by analysts during conference calls.

### 4.3 ANALYSIS OF THE DEI CREDIBILITY OF JOB POSTINGS

Job postings represent the first step in the hiring process (figure 1). To attract and recruit diverse candidates effectively, the DEI information conveyed in job postings must be credible. Diverse candidates are more likely to apply for positions at companies that demonstrate a clear commitment to DEI, do not discriminate based on factors beyond job-related skills, and foster an inclusive culture. They may also prefer companies that offer specific benefits supporting minorities, such as flexible hours, parental leave, and childcare (e.g., Mas and Pallais [2017]). To address this issue, we leverage our algorithm's ability to identify the communication of credible DEI commitments and distinguish between credible DEI disclosures and boilerplate statements. Specifically, if a job posting is identified as including DEI disclosure ( $ d(DEI)=1 $) and the algorithm determines that all four DEI-related criteria are present (so the job posting includes at least a brief discussion—score 0.5, or a more complete discussion—score 1 of all DEI-related criteria), we flag that job posting as containing credible firm-specific DEI statements. Conversely, if any of the four DEI-related criteria is not present (i.e., equal to 0), we flag the job posting as including boilerplate statements. Arguably, this is a conservative approach, as it might overclassify job postings as including boilerplate statements since it requires just one of the four DEI-related criteria to be missing to flag a job posting as boilerplate. We then create two variables—Firm-specific DEI Statements (all factors) and Boilerplate DEI Statements (all factors)—by aggregating these indicators to the firm-job-type-month level in the same way as our main  $ d(DEI) $ variable; that is, by computing the monthly proportion of job postings with credible (or boilerplate) statements for each firm-job-type.

To refine our measure, we repeat this process but focus only on two DEI-related criteria—benefits and culture—since they identify clear references to corporate culture or specific benefits related to DEI. Thus, a job posting with DEI disclosure  $ (d(DEI) = 1) $ but no reference to either of these two important aspects may simply be including boilerplate statements. We label these two variables as Firm-specific DEI Statements (culture and benefits) and Boilerplate DEI Statements (culture and benefits).

Next, we examine the length of the recruitment period by looking at how long a job remains vacant and the number of interviews a firm conducts. Considering an additional element (e.g., DEI) when determining whether a candidate is a good match for the position can require interviewing more people and, at the margin, lengthen the recruitment period. In other words, in an effort to improve workforce diversity, a firm adds DEI disclosures to its job postings (table 3), thereby attracting its traditional applicants while potentially also receiving applications from new individuals drawn by the DEI information. With an expanded applicant pool, it takes longer to assess, interview, and hire for a given position. Observing prolonged recruitment periods after the regulation and more interviews would suggest that firms are facing more challenges in finding their ideal candidates. If the labor supply is limited or inelastic in the short term, the number of offers tendered by firms looking to increase diversity might rise, but the number of declined offers would also increase, as diverse candidates can only accept one position.

## 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we study the real effects of the 2020 Regulation S-K. The regulation mandates human capital disclosure in 10K filings, reflecting a broader trend by the SEC toward expanding disclosure requirements beyond traditional financial metrics. By examining the labor market consequences of this regulation, we shed light on the interconnections between securities regulations and organizational practices, expanding the understanding of the impact of securities regulation beyond capital markets. Our findings suggest that Regulation S-K prompts firms to increase the extent and length of DEI disclosure in job postings. Furthermore, consistent with inelastic labor supply in the short-term, the impact of the regulation on the workforce becomes visible 4 quarters after the regulation, particularly when paired with credible internal commitment to DEI. This suggests that addressing diversity issues in the labor market entails substantial challenges.

Our study also highlights the need for further research to explore additional mechanisms through which Regulation S-K influences labor market dynamics and human resources (HR) management. We focus on DEI because it is the most common topic resulting from Regulation S-K, and because it directly relates to labor market outcomes (i.e., workforce diversity) that we can precisely measure. However, Arif, Yoon, and Zhang [2024] show that Safety & Health and Employee Development are other value-relevant HCD topics discussed in 10Ks following the regulation.
