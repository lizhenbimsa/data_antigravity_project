## A B S T R A C T

Keywords:

Inflation expectations

Twitter

Text mining

Big data

Forecasting

Drawing on Italian tweets, we employ textual data and machine learning techniques to build new real-time measures of consumers' inflation expectations. First, we select keywords to identify tweets related to prices and expectations thereof. Second, we build a set of daily measures of inflation expectations around the selected tweets, combining the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) with a dictionary-based approach, using manually labeled bi-grams and tri-grams. Finally, we show that Twitter-based indicators are highly correlated with both monthly survey-based and daily market-based inflation expectations. Our new indicators anticipate consumers' expectations, proving to be a good real-time proxy, and provide additional information beyond market-based expectations, professional forecasts, and realized inflation. The results suggest that Twitter can be a new timely source for eliciting beliefs.

### 1. Introduction

In recent years, Twitter has become one of the most famous social networking sites, with 200 million monthly active users worldwide and about 10 million active users in Italy in 2019 (AGCOM, 2020). The microblogging service is widely used – both by journalists and consumers – to spread and obtain news in real-time and is a primary source of information for many users around the world. Discussions on this platform reflect trending topics among a variety of agents and reveal collective opinions on several issues, such as politics, technology, and economy.

Given the consolidation of Twitter as a public forum for personal opinions and experiences, in this study, we investigate whether tweets convey people's beliefs about short-term price dynamics and whether they can be used to elicit inflation expectations.

Inflation expectations are at the heart of any consumption and investment decisions of households and firms in the economy. Moreover, timely and accurate knowledge of inflation expectations is paramount for monetary policy, as inflation expectations in longer horizons are a measure of the credibility of the central bank, while in shorter horizons they reflect a measure of the effectiveness of monetary policy. For this reason, inflation expectations dynamics are carefully studied by both academics and policymakers.

There are two commonly used sources of inflation expectations: surveys and prices of financial assets linked to inflation. Both measures have their advantages and disadvantages. Survey data reflect true expectations of a (small) selected sample of agents, such as professional forecasters, households, or firms, but they are available only at a low frequency, usually monthly, or quarterly. While market-based measures, such as those derived from swap contracts linked to inflation or inflation-protected securities, are readily available at high-frequencies, they are imperfect measures of consumers' inflation expectations. Indeed, they reflect investors' inflation expectations and time-varying risk premia (Haubrich et al., 2012; Gürkaynak et al., 2010).

In this paper, we suggest Twitter as a source for eliciting consumers' inflation expectations that can be both timely (as in market-based expectations) and accurate (as in survey-based ones). The source is timely as Twitter messages are continuously updated; it is accurate, as it provides information on the expected inflation rate of a large sample of consumers, given its broad and diverse user base, and it is not affected by the risk premia caveat. If successful, this approach may complement the existing inflation expectation data sources and provide daily indicators of consumers' beliefs. Hence, we pose the following research questions: Do tweets say something about inflation? Can we use tweets to get a daily proxy of inflation expectations? Would this proxy convey timely and correct information, in addition to the existing sources of inflation expectations?

To address these questions, we first select keywords to identify the tweets related to the (current and expected) prices of goods and services in Italy. We collect all tweets posted in the Italian language between 1 June 2013 and 31 December 2019 containing at least one of the selected keywords. We obtain a large number of tweets (11.1 million) related to inflation and expected price dynamics, but also concerning advertisements, e-commerce websites, and sales.

To reduce the noise and build a set of Twitter-based daily indicators, we take a three-step approach. First, we filter out the noisy content and isolate valuable signals by implementing a topic analysis on the text of the messages using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), an unsupervised machine learning algorithm which statistically estimates the topics (probabilistic collections of words) of a set of documents (Blei et al., 2003), enabling us to select tweets related to inflation developments. Second, using the filtered data, we apply a dictionary of manually labeled bi-grams and tri-grams to assign tweets to bins, each denoting expectations of increasing or decreasing inflation. Within each bin, we compute an index that considers the raw daily count of tweets. Then, we aggregate the raw daily counts of tweets representing increasing or decreasing inflation expectations in directional indicators, which increase (decrease) with expectations of increasing (decreasing) inflation.

To validate the signals extracted from the Twitter messages, we investigate the extent to which they correlate with available sources of inflation expectations. As survey-based inflation expectations, we use the monthly survey on consumer and business confidence provided by the Italian national statistical institute, ISTAT. The survey tracks respondents' qualitative expectations on price trends over the next 12 months. We also compare the Twitter-based indicators with market-based inflation expectations to exploit the high frequency of the data (bearing in mind the caveat of time-varying risk premia). For market-based inflation expectations, we use swap contracts linked to Italian inflation with a one-year horizon. The analysis shows that the signal extracted from Twitter is significantly related to both survey-based and market-based expectations and is a consistent proxy of the dynamics of inflation expectations.

Finally, we analyze the extent to which the Twitter-based indicators can be used to predict survey-based inflation expectations and “artificially increase” their frequency. The Twitter-based indicators predict the survey-based inflation expectations, both in-sample and out-of-sample, and convey additional information content beyond the existing data sources (i.e. survey- and market-based measures, realized inflation, and predictions by professional forecasters).

Remarkably, Twitter-based indicators derived from a small sub-sample of users interested in economics or in news, in their self-published biographies, have similar predictive properties both in-sample and out-of-sample. Besides, to isolate the forward-looking component of the signal extracted from Twitter, we build a set of indicators obtained using future words (e.g. "long-run" or "expect") or verbs, which have similar but weaker properties due to lower volumes of tweets. These robustness checks confirm that our Twitter-based indicators capture the correct signal, as they reflect the beliefs of well-informed individuals and forward-looking expectations.

The present paper contributes to the economic literature in several ways. Our main contribution is to propose a new source of data to elicit expectations with certain advantages compared to the standard expectations. First, it involves a wide variety and large number of individuals, relative to market-based data reflecting traders' opinions and survey measures that consider small samples of agents. Second, the high frequency of the data allows us to build daily indicators, whereas polls are available at a monthly or quarterly frequency. Lastly, this data source is not linked specifically to any country, so it can be used and replicated in several instances. These advantages make Twitter a potentially powerful source from which to extract agents' expectations. Our work is also a methodological contribution to how machine learning techniques of text analysis, together with a semantic approach, can be used to extract meaningful information on macroeconomic variables and expectations from noisy, textual, and very large data. The LDA textual analysis has been recently used in the economics literature (Hansen et al., 2018); relative to the existing studies, we combine the LDA with a semantic approach, to extract a directional signal of inflation expectations. Lastly, our work contributes to the literature that investigates the usefulness of social media data to predict real-world outcomes (Antenucci et al., 2014; Mao et al., 2015; O'Connor et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2014; Jiao et al., 2020; Da et al., 2014) by focusing on inflation expectations.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the data and the keywords used to select the tweets. Section 3 reports the three-step procedure adopted to compute the Twitter-based indicators of inflation expectations.

Section 4 compares these indicators with the survey- and market-based measures. Section 5 shows robustness checks using sub-samples of users interested in economics or in the business news or based on future words and verbs. Section 6 shows the additional information content of the Twitter-based indicators in-sample, above and beyond the lagged survey- and market-based expectations, realized inflation, and professionals' forecasts. Section 7 shows the predictive superiority of Twitter-based indicators out-of-sample, relative to the existing sources. Section 8 gives the conclusions.

### 2. Twitter data: keywords and initial dataset

We use messages (tweets) from the social networking site Twitter. Tweets are short messages of at most 140 characters. $ ^{1} $ Once a sender (twitterer) writes a message and sends it out, the tweet reaches the users/people with whom the twitterer is linked (followers), who, in turn, can forward the message to their followers (re-tweet). Tweets typically contain news, links, opinions, advertisements, or personal information that the twitterer wants to share with the public. They are publicly available on the social network and can also be searched within the search engine provided by the platform.

To build our database of tweets, we select several keywords in Italian related to inflation, prices, and price dynamics and collect all the tweets that contains at least one of them. $ ^{2} $ The dictionary of selected keywords in Italian [English] can be categorized as follows:

• prezzo [price], prezzi [prices], and costo della vita [cost of living] capture tweets about prices in general that do not provide information on price dynamics unless further analyzed;

• caro bollette [expensive bills], inflazione [inflation], caro [expensive], caro prezzi [high prices], caroprezzi [high-prices], benzina alle stelle [high gasoline prices], bolletta salata [high bill], caro affitti [high rents], caro benzina [high gasoline price], caro carburante [high petrol prices], and caro gas [high gas prices] reflect certain price dynamics and capture tweets about increasing price(s);

• deflazione [deflation], disinflazione [disinflation], ribassi [sales], ribasso [sale], meno caro [less expensive], and bollette più leggere [less expensive bills] select tweets about decreasing prices. $ ^{3} $

Using this set of keywords, we collect tweets that explicitly communicate expected price dynamics and those that report some recently observed price dynamics. The latter reflect inflation perceptions rather than expectations, but they are relevant inputs in the expectation formation process. To exemplify our rationale, consider a simple Bayesian learning framework, where individuals form expectations on future inflation, by observing noisy signals. Imagine that an individual has a prior distribution over annual inflation next year,  $ \pi $, which is normally distributed with mean  $ \tilde{\pi} $ and variance  $ 1/\tau $. She observes on Twitter a noisy signal on  $ \pi $,  $ \hat{\pi} $, which is, conditional on  $ \pi $, normally and independently distributed with mean  $ \pi $ and variance  $ 1/\chi $. This tweet could say something about the realized inflation (which, if the data generating process of inflation is autocorrelated, will also say something about future inflation) or about future inflation directly. She will use this signal to generate a posterior distribution of future inflation, thus updating her expectation. Assuming independence of signals, the posterior distribution of  $ \pi $ will be normally distributed with mean  $ \tilde{\pi}(\hat{\pi}) = \frac{\tau}{\tau + \chi} \tilde{\pi} + \frac{\chi}{\tau + \chi} \hat{\pi} $ and variance  $ 1/\tau' $, where  $ \tau' = \tau + \chi $. This person might then send a tweet with her mean expectation  $ \tilde{\pi}(\hat{\pi}) $. With our data collection we observe either  $ \hat{\pi} $ or  $ \tilde{\pi}(\hat{\pi}) $ (or both). If we observe the latter, we have a direct revelation of expectations. If we observe the former,  $ \hat{\pi} $, we have an input in the expectation update process.

Our initial dataset consists of 11.1 million tweets sent between 1 June 2013 and 31 December 2019 and about 950,000 users. The sample contains the full text of the tweet and the available meta-data, including the public biography of the tweet sender, the number of followers, etc.

The initial dataset includes tweets related to different themes: it includes messages related to inflation developments (coming from personal experiences or news and official media outlets) or expectations thereof, but it also contains "noise", such as advertisements, e-commerce tweets, or tweets that use the word "inflation" in a different context from price developments. Table 1 provides a selection of tweets from our sample. The first three tweets refer to news about past inflation developments (real or perceived). The following three tweets convey information about expected inflation developments. The seventh tweet refers to advertisements, and the last one shows the use of the word "inflation" with a different meaning from the economic sense.

### 3.1. Step 1: Topic analysis

To filter out the noise in our initial dataset, and isolate valuable signals, we implement a probabilistic topic analysis provided by the LDA. Here we provide a brief intuitive description of the LDA and how it is implemented. $ ^{4} $ The LDA is a way to reduce the dimensionality of large amounts of textual data and provide a “summary” description of what a document (a collection of words and their relative frequencies) is about. To do so, the LDA posits the way the document has been generated. It assumes that all documents have been generated by a document-specific mixture of topics and that each topic is defined by a distribution over words. $ ^{5} $ Both the mixture of topics and the distribution of words defining the topics are hidden. Thus the objective is to use the observed documents to infer, through Bayesian methods, the distribution of words that defines the topic, and the mixture of topics (the relative importance of each topic) that describes a document. After fitting the LDA, it is possible to assign a meaning to each topic by examining which words have the highest importance (the highest probability mass in the distribution), as explained by Steyvers and Griffiths (2007), and describe the content of each document by considering its most relevant topics (the highest weights in the mixture).

The analysis proceeds as follows. First, we process the textual data transforming the tweets into a suitable form for text mining (cleaning, splitting, rare words and stopword removal, and featurization) and we implement the LDA on this clean dataset. $ ^{6} $

Several authors consider the brevity of tweets to be a disadvantage for applying topic extraction algorithms like LDA. We address this issue by applying a tweet-pooling strategy based on users, as suggested by Alvarez-Melis and Saveski (2016). We thus generate a set of documents, where each “document” is created by grouping all the  $ n_{a} $ tweets written by a single author a in the dataset. We then apply the LDA to this corpus made of A documents, where A is the total number of users in our sample.

In the estimation procedure, the LDA requires the ex-ante specification of the number of topics, and we consider 50 topics. We estimate this number automatically by running the LDA for several topic numbers between 20 and 75 and choosing the first minimum value for the log perplexity of a held-out sample of 30% of the data, as explained in the online Appendix.

After the LDA estimation, we use the estimated topic-word distribution to predict the most dominant topics of each single tweet. In practice, we re-run the online variational inference algorithm on a new document-term matrix, where now each tweet is a document, obtaining a new estimate of the document-topic proportions for each tweet. We then consider the most likely (dominant) topic of each tweet and select those tweets whose dominant topic is either of the two of interest related to inflation/deflation (i.e. Topic 20 or 37). To avoid ambiguous cases, we discard tweets whose second most likely topic has less than a 5 percentage point difference with the first most likely topic, $ ^{9} $ unless the first and second most likely topics are both related to inflation/deflation. $ ^{10} $

In recent text mining literature, some authors have questioned the stability of the topics discovered by the LDA across different executions of the algorithm. $ ^{[11]} $ To deal with this issue, we train three topic models, with three independent runs of the LDA procedure with 50 topics with no fixed seed for the random number generator. To confirm the robustness of the results across the three runs, we check how often the different LDA executions agree in selecting a tweet. Overall, for about 83% of the cases, at least two LDA runs agree to assign a tweet to the two selected topics. We sample the remaining 17% and after a careful inspection, we decided to keep it in our sample, as it contains relevant content for the scope of our analysis. $ ^{[12]} $

This first step of the procedure leads to a final dataset of 1,534,743 tweets (about 14% of the initial dataset) with 165,551 distinct users. The volume of tweets varies substantially over time and across keywords. For instance, tweets on “inflation” record a daily average of 131 and a maximum of 1,671, while those on “deflation” record a daily average of 67 and a maximum of 5,744, on 29 August, 2014. $ ^{13} $ The tweets with the word “price(s)” are around 170 per day on average, with a maximum of 1,089 and a standard deviation of 117.

The topic analysis allows us to isolate valuable signals, filtering out many messages unrelated to inflation expectations, and primarily related to advertisements or e-commerce websites. In the original dataset, around 4.6 million tweets are selected because they mention the word prezzo/prezzi [price(s)], but only 406,430 (about 8%) survive to the second phase. The topic analysis is also useful for discerning tweets that mention the words “inflation”, “disinflation”, even if in this case the discarded tweets are much fewer (only 9%), both in absolute and relative terms. Among these tweets, about 462,000 (out of almost 506,000) survive to the topic analysis, suggesting that most of the tweets with these keywords contain useful information about price dynamics.

### 3.2. Step 2: dictionary-based approach

As a second step, we implement a dictionary-based approach to the filtered data. We refine the rough initial dictionary used to select the tweets, creating a novel dictionary made of manually labeled bi-grams and tri-grams. In particular, we extract all the bi-grams and tri-grams containing the words “price”, “prices”, “expensive”, “inflation”, and “deflation” and we manually label them as Up or Down, depending on the authors’ subjective interpretation of whether they indicate increasing or decreasing price dynamics, respectively. After sorting the n-grams in descending order, with respect to the average yearly number of tweets in which they are contained, we select all the n-grams that appear in at least 100 tweets every year.

Fig. 1 depicts the first 15 most frequent bi-grams and tri-grams classified as Up (green horizontal bars) and Down (red horizontal bars). The most common Up bi/tri-grams are più caro [more expensive], caro prezzo [expensive price], or un prezzo altissimo [a very high price] with an average yearly volume of tweets equal around 10,000. The most common Down bi/tri-grams are prezzo speciale [special price], prezzi bassi [low prices], metà prezzo [half price], or prezzi più bassi [lower prices]. $ ^{15} $

Further, for robustness, we create a set of alternative dictionaries using (1) only bi-grams, (2) only tri-grams, or (3) both. In building our dictionaries, we use a different set of bi-grams and/or tri-grams, depending on the average yearly volume of tweets in which they are contained over the full sample, and we select (A) the top 5%; (B) the top 10%; (C) the n-grams that appear in at least 100 tweets every year; and (D) all the labeled bi-grams and/or tri-grams. Overall, the results do not change significantly by using these alternative dictionaries, thus confirming that our results do not depend on the specific choices made when building the dictionary. $ ^{16} $

We then build two Twitter-based indexes (Index Up and Index Down) by measuring the daily volume of tweets containing at least one of the bi-grams or tri-grams included in the dictionary. $ ^{17} $ The rationale for focusing on the pure raw tweets count is the intuitive notion that the more people talk about something, the greater the probability that it reflects their opinion and that their views can influence other people's expectations. That is because the more people talk about a topic, the more attention they pay to it, and the stronger the echo of the news that they provide. At the same time, the fact that they associate a possible direction of price variations in their message should influence (or reflect) expectations accordingly. For instance, the fact that agents talk about expensive bills should reflect expectations of higher inflation. In contrast, people discussing declining oil prices should correspond to expectations of lower inflation.

Fig. 2 depicts how the Up and Down indexes vary over time. Both Index Up and Index Down appear to reflect news about current and future inflation. They tend to spike after the Italian Consumer Price Index (CPI) flash estimate releases by ISTAT or after European Central Bank (ECB) Governing Council press conferences, or speeches by one of the ECB's Board

### 3.3. Step 3: computation of aggregate directional indicators

We then combine the indexes shown in Fig. 2 to generate directional indicators, which point towards increasing or decreasing inflation expectations. As there is no straightforward way to do this, we propose here several indicators and check how each of these performs:

1. Indicator #1: We compute the difference between the two indexes,  $ \pi_0^e \equiv (\text{Index Up-Index Down}) $. We winsorize  $ \pi_0^e $, by cutting the extreme values, those greater than three standard deviations, and setting them to 100. Next we standardize the series dividing it by three times the standard deviation. The resulting index is smoothed using a (backward-looking) moving average (MA) of 10, 30 and 60 days. This smoothing aims to capture the idea that most likely it is not just the single piece of information received in a day that is important for inflation expectations, but also information obtained in the recent past.

2. Indicator #2: We compute  $ \pi_0^e $ as defined above and regress it on a set of dummies for the releases of the preliminary CPI in Italy and Germany, the ECB press conferences and the speeches by any member of the ECB Board and a single dummy for 29 August, 2014. With this procedure we eliminate additional noise emerging solely because our indexes tend to spike when news about inflation is released. We then take the residuals from such a regression, winsorize the extreme values, so that those values greater than three standard deviations are set to 100 and standardize them with respect to three times their standard deviation. The resulting index is smoothed using a (backward-looking) MA of 10, 30 and 60 days.

### 4. Twitter, survey and market-based inflation expectations

To ascertain whether the suggested Twitter-based inflation expectation indicators are capturing inflation expectations, we compare them with both survey-based and market-based measures. Survey-based measures are more accurate, but they are available only at low-frequency (i.e. monthly). On the other hand, market-based inflation expectations are available at higher frequency, as securities are traded continuously, but they might not truly represent expectations due to the time-varying risk premia and liquidity characteristics of the contracts on which they are based. Our ideal analysis would be to compare the Twitter-based indicators with survey-based data only. Still, given the relatively short time sample and the monthly frequency of surveys, it is also instructive to compare our indicators with market-based indicators, exploiting their higher frequency.

### 4.2. Twitter expectations vs market expectations

Among market-based measures of inflation expectations, we focus on inflation swap contracts linked to Italian inflation, due to these securities being relatively more liquid than inflation-linked Italian sovereigns. A swap is an agreement whereby one party pays the other a variable amount, in this case, the realization of the inflation rate in Italy, $ ^{21} $ and the other pays a fixed amount, the swap rate, over the maturity of the contract. If investors are risk-neutral, these rates represent the expectations for average inflation over the maturity of the contract. In contrast, with risk-averse investors there can be a wedge, the so-called inflation risk premium, between the swap rate and the expected average inflation rate. $ ^{22} $ Indeed, Haubrich et al. (2012) and Casiraghi and Miccoli (2019) document the significant presence of time-varying risk premia in inflation swap rates, suggesting that these measures do not accurately reflect inflation expectations.

We rely on swap contracts with a maturity of 1 year (1Y) consistent with the horizon of the survey-based measures. Fig. 5 shows the time series plots of each Twitter-based inflation expectations index with the Italian inflation swap 1Y. All indicators are highly and significantly correlated with inflation swap rates. Again, there are a few short-time intervals in which our Twitter-based inflation expectation indicators do not co-move. For example, at the beginning of March 2017, there is a spike in all of the four Twitter-based indicators, while the inflation swap stays constant. In this case, the increase in our indexes is due to a high volume of the n-grams inflazione [inflation rises] and rialzo dei prezzi [rise in prices]. These n-grams were due to a few newspaper articles commenting on the rise, in February 2017, of Italian inflation due to an increase in oil and food prices, which were highly retweeted and discussed during the first few days of March.

### 5. Robustness

### 5.1. Sub-sample of users interested in economics (Econ) or news (News)

One key issue in analyzing social network data is to understand whether the information conveyed is actually capable of shaping expectations or whether it is discarded by the receiver because the source is not considered authoritative. With our data collection, we can condition the selection of tweets to some characteristics that the users have published in their biographies on Twitter and focus on senders whom the general reader could consider to be authorities on inflation. To this end, first, we re-compute the Twitter-based indicators using only tweets from users interested in economics (Econ) in their self-published biographies. $ ^{24} $ These are all the users who mention in their biographies the words economista [economist], finanza/e [finance/finances], economia [economics/economy]. There are a total of 3,980 such users in our sample. We then produce similar indicators considering only users who are interested in the News according to their biographies; these are all the users who have the words giornale/i [newspaper/newspapers], news, notizia/e [news], stampa [press], in their description. There are in total 7,231 individual users with such characteristics.

The new series cover a smaller number of tweets but follow the same pattern as the indicators computed considering the entire pool of users (Fig. 6). For instance, Index Down is on average equal to 14 when it is computed on the Econ sub-sample, while it is 118 when computed on the fully-filtered dataset. Index Up is 37 over the full set of tweets and 3 for the Econ sub-sample. $ ^{25} $

The results are robust when the indicators are computed on these sub-samples (Tables 6 and 7). $ ^{26} $ The coefficients are all positive and significant. The  $ R^{2} $ is slightly bigger compared to those of Tables 4 and 5, suggesting that by further reducing the sample we are not missing useful informational content, as we capture the tweets associated with a specific audience that is more knowledgeable about economics and inflation. This result suggests that our Twitter-based indicators are actually capturing the correct signal on inflation expectations and are not strongly contaminated by noise. ### 6. Informativeness exercise

The analysis so far suggests that Twitter-based indicators are meaningful proxies of inflation expectations. Now we investigate an additional question: does observing the Twitter-based indicators give an informative advantage to consumers' expectations beyond the existing measures? We assume that people form inflation expectations by exploiting signals from several sources, including Twitter, and test whether Twitter indicators anticipate consumers' inflation expectations conditional on other signals. As we cannot observe all the signals that individuals receive, we assume that those conveyed by market-based inflation expectations, consensus forecasts, the CPI estimates, and Twitter are sufficient statistics for the information set observed by individuals. Then, we estimate the following regression model:

 $$ E_{t}^{ISTAT}\pi_{t,t+12}=\alpha+\rho E_{t-1}^{ISTAT}\pi_{t-1,t+11}+\beta IS_{t}^{1y}+\delta CF_{t-1}^{y+1}+\gamma Infl.exp._{t}+\eta CPI_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t} $$ 

 $ E_{t}^{ISTAT} \pi_{t,t+12} $ is the month  $ t $ survey-based inflation expectations provided by ISTAT, as in Section 4.1, and  $ E_{t-1}^{ISTAT} \pi_{t-1,t+11} $ is the previous month value.  $ IS_{t}^{1y} $ is the one-year inflation swap rate.  $ Infl.exp._{t} $ is one of our Twitter-based indicators.  $ CF_{t-1}^{y+1} $ is the monthly average of Consensus Forecast of Italian inflation for one-year ahead, which accounts for the inflation expectations of a survey of professional forecasters. $ ^{30} $  $ CPI_{t-1} $ is the lagged value of the realized inflation, which is included to account for the effects of news on inflation expectations.

All the regressors are in the information set available when expectations are formed. As the responses to the survey are collected in the first half of the month t, without specifying any single day, we assume that the information set of the individuals – when answering the survey – consists of signals received up to, and including, the 15th day of the month. Therefore, for both  $ IS_{t}^{1y} $ and  $ Infl.exp._{t} $ we use the value of the measure on the 15th day of the reference month  $ t $. $ ^{31} $

### 7. Predictive power out-of-sample

Finally, we study the predictive power of our Twitter-based indicators of inflation expectations and run a simple out-of-sample forecasting exercise. As in the in-sample informativeness exercise, the target variable to forecast is the monthly survey-based measure of the consumers' inflation expectation produced by ISTAT. We use a recursive scheme to forecast from 1 up to 6 months ahead. Our first in-sample consists of 36 monthly observations from June 2013 through May 2016. To gauge the performance of competing models we can use the Cumulative Sum of Squared forecasting Errors Differences (CSSED), which is defined as

 $$ CSSED_{m,\tau}=\sum_{\tau=R}^{T}(\hat{e}_{bm,\tau}^{2}-\hat{e}_{m,\tau}^{2}) $$ 

where  $ \hat{e}_{bm,\tau}^{2} $ are the squared forecast errors from the benchmark model in the out-of-sample, and  $ \hat{e}_{m,\tau}^{2} $ are the squared forecast errors from the competing model. A value of the CSSED below 1 at a certain point of the out-of-sample means that if we run the forecasting exercise by splitting the in-sample and the evaluation sample at that point, the benchmark model outperforms, showing a lower RMSE than the competing model. However, if the CSSED is above 1, then the competing model outperforms the benchmark.

Fig. 7 shows the CSSED for the forecast horizons from one-month ahead (top left) to six-months ahead (bottom right), when a 30-day backward-looking MA is used to smooth the Twitter-based indicators. $ ^{34} $ The market-based index rarely helps in predicting the survey-based index out-of-sample; in fact, the corresponding green line almost always lies below the others for the whole out-of-sample, across all forecast horizons, except for a very short interval at two- and three-month horizons. All our Twitter-based indicators are outperforming the benchmark, also at 1-month ahead, which is the nowcasting exercise. At longer horizons, Twitter-based indicators tend to outperform extremely well at the end of the out-of-sample.

We obtain similar results using the alternative dictionaries to build the directional indexes, discussed in Section 3.2 (i.e. dictionaries based on the bi-grams and/or tri-grams with different thresholds for the yearly average volume of tweets) and using dictionaries with verbs in the future tense and future words. In addition, we obtain similar results using an AR(p) model augmented with the inflation swap index as the benchmark.

All these results corroborate our view that Twitter-based indicators of inflation expectations do convey meaningful information that can be used to more accurately forecast, or even fill-in, survey-based inflation expectations.

### 8. Concluding remarks

In this paper, we suggest a new way to measure consumers' inflation expectations using Big data techniques and Twitter feeds. We compute a set of Twitter-based inflation expectation indicators by combining a dictionary-based approach and Machine learning techniques and show that they are a good proxy for consumers' inflation expectations. As with any Big data applications, the new measures are characterized by the fact that they are timely, and they are not subject to a publication lag. Besides, they anticipate consumers' expectations and provide additional information content beyond the alternative existing measures (i.e. professional forecasters and market-based expectations).

The analysis underscores the relevance and importance of information transmitted over social networks for policy purposes. This literature is quite new, and still beset by some uncertainty, especially with respect to the extent to which information on social networks can be transformed into an efficient and understandable signal. While more research on this topic is warranted, it needs cross-fertilization from computer scientists, statisticians, and economists.
