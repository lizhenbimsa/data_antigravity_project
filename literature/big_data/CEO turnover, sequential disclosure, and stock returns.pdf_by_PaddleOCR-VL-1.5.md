## Abstract

We document that firms experience large negative stock returns during, and positive returns following, the first informational events after forced CEO turnovers. This V-shaped return pattern is driven by the strategic sequential disclosure of bad news and good news, aligned with incoming CEOs' incentives to manage expectations. The pattern is more pronounced when these incentives are stronger, such as when firms earn higher stock returns and have higher valuation uncertainty leading up to the informational events. Evidence from firms' earnings surprises, analysts' forecast revisions, and large language model-based measures of disclosure behavior indicates that incoming CEOs often initially release bad news about realized and short-term earnings, projecting a broadly pessimistic outlook for the firm's future performance, and subsequently disclose favorable news about longer-term earnings prospects. Our findings suggest that investors make the costly mistake of failing to discern the incentives behind managers' disclosure.

Keywords: CEO turnover; stock returns; expectation management.

JEL classifications G12, G14, M12, D84.

## 1. Introduction

Expectation management plays a crucial role in shaping firms' interactions with financial markets. The existing literature extensively documents that managers have incentives to guide equity analysts' earnings forecasts to ensure these forecasts are beatable (e.g., Barth, Elliott, and Finn 1999; Bartov, Givoly, and Hayn 2002), and that such expectation management is often effective (e.g., Matsumoto 2002; Richardson, Hong Teoh, and Wysocki 2004; Bernhardt and Campello 2007). A common view for this phenomenon, as suggested by Matsumoto (2002) and Richardson, Hong Teoh, and Wysocki (2004), is that analysts want to maintain access to firms by appearing optimistic, which leads them to initially issue overly optimistic forecasts. Firms, in turn, aim to beat these forecasts during earnings announcements and thus walk down the forecasts over time. Under this view, both managers and analysts understand the implicit "game" and cooperate to produce mutually desirable outcomes. However, this perspective does not easily extend from analysts' expectations to market expectations, as investors lack the same incentives to gain access to

firms. This raises important questions: can managers successfully manage investors’ expectations, and if so, how?

Empirically studying how firms’ expectation management behaviors impact financial markets is challenging due to the difficulty in measuring managers’ ex-ante incentives for expectation management, which are not directly observable and can vary over time. $ ^{1} $ In this article, we investigate this question by focusing on forced CEO turnovers—a salient and high-stakes setting in which new CEOs are widely recognized as having strong incentives to disclose information strategically and manage expectations (e.g., Moore 1973; DeAngelo 1988; Elliott and Shaw 1988; Weisbach 1995; Godfrey, Mather, and Ramsay 2003; Guan, Wright, and Leikam 2005; Noh and Zhou 2022; Daniele et al. 2024).

One expectation management strategy that new CEOs may adopt is to disclose bad news and good news sequentially:

1) Bad news to lower benchmarks. Incoming CEOs first disclose inherited bad news to lower benchmarks for evaluating their future performance. This “big bath” strategy allows them to reset expectations without bearing personal responsibility for past issues.

2) Good news to signal competence. To meet board and market expectations, incoming CEOs subsequently signal their ability to enhance shareholder value through forward-looking announcements, such as value-maximizing (dis)investment plans.

The following example illustrates this predictable sequencing of disclosures:

Plug Power Inc., a publicly traded company listed on Nasdaq, replaced its management team on January 25, 2015. The market responded favorably by bidding up its stock price by 5.4 percent. However, the new management lowered the sales forecast only a few days later:

Fuel cell maker Plug Power Inc. tempered its sales forecast for 2015, saying it had decided to issue numbers “people could rely” on after receiving “a lot of criticism for under-delivering.” (Reuters, January 29, 2015) $ ^{2} $

When announcing this downward revision in sales forecast, the new management implicitly “blamed” their predecessors for setting an overly optimistic forecast. This revision caught investors off-guard, and the firm’s stock price fell by 10 percent upon the news. On January 30, however, the CEO revealed the following in an interview:

We want to under promise and over deliver, [...] So that's why we put those [forecast] numbers out there. (Albany Business Review, January 30, 2015; emphasis added by authors) $ ^{3} $

The stock price subsequently rebounded. This case exemplifies how new management can strategically sequence bad news and good news to manage expectations.

In this article, we show that the example above is not an isolated case but rather a systematic phenomenon across US firms. We study a sample of approximately 3,800 CEO turnovers in firms listed on the US stock market from 1993 to 2019. Our research design exploits variations in new CEOs' incentives to strategically disclose information across forced and voluntary turnovers. We use Peters and Wagner (2014) dataset to distinguish between forced and voluntary turnovers. Our empirical analysis conditions on both the

We find a distinct V-shaped stock return pattern around the informational events following recent forced CEO turnovers. Specifically, stock prices decline by an average of 3 percent (t-value = 4.12) during these events, coinciding with negative earnings surprises, downward revisions in analysts’ forecasts, and pessimistic tones in corporate filings. Over the following year, however, these stocks significantly outperform, generating abnormal returns of 7–14 percent, accompanied by upward revisions in analysts’ long-term earnings growth forecasts. The magnitude of these gains exceeds the initial losses, suggesting a net positive effect on shareholder value. $ ^{4} $

Figure 1 visualizes this V-shaped stock return dynamics. The solid line shows that the stock prices of firms with recent forced CEO turnovers experience a significant decline during the first informational event. In subsequent quarters, however, these stocks deliver substantial positive abnormal returns, more than fully recovering their earlier losses. This V-shaped pattern is absent among firms with voluntary turnovers (dashed line), as new CEOs in these cases have fewer incentives for strategic disclosure.

To shed light on the specifics of how the strategic disclosure process works, we examine two additional predictions. First, a new CEO's likelihood of disclosing bad news should increase if the stock price has risen prior to the informational event. This is because high stock returns indicate that investors may hold elevated expectations about the new CEO and/or may be unaware of the predecessor's undisclosed bad news. As a result, high returns reduce the CEO's need to signal competence while increasing the need to manage expectations. Consistent with this prediction, we find that stock prices decline more sharply during the informational events if the stocks experienced higher returns in the preceding weeks. Furthermore, this return reversal is asymmetric—stocks with low prior returns do not earn

high returns during the informational events, supporting the idea that new CEOs face stronger incentives to manage expectations downward.

The second prediction concerns valuation uncertainty. A CEO’s sequential disclosure of bad and good news relies on the premise that the firm actually holds such undisclosed news. We posit that firms with higher stock volatility are more likely to possess such mixed news. This approach draws on the extensive literature on the links between information uncertainty, divergence of opinion, and stock market volatility (e.g., Ajinkya and Gift 1985; Anderson, Ghysels, and Juergens 2005; Zhang 2006; Carlin, Longstaff, and Matoba 2014; Atmaz and Basak 2018). One way to characterize this idea is that if a firm holds undisclosed bad and good news—meaning it may underperform for some reasons and outperform for others—investors with incomplete private information should demonstrate greater divergence in their opinions about the firm’s fair value, as some investors may only anticipate good news, while others may only anticipate bad news. When these diverging investors trade at different times, their disagreement manifests as stock price volatility. Therefore, we expect the V-shaped return pattern to be more pronounced among firms with high stock volatility. Consistent with this prediction, we find that firms with higher stock volatility experience both sharper declines during and larger gains after the informational events.

Finally, we examine the mechanism—specifically, the nature of the information being manipulated—underlying these patterns by analyzing firms’ earnings surprises, analysts’ earnings forecast revisions, and managerial tones in 10-K/Q filings. In addition, we use ChatGPT to extract managerial optimism over different horizons and quantify the likelihood of strategic disclosure from the Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) sections of 10-K/Q filings. The findings from these analyses suggest that new CEOs initially disclose bad news about firms’ realized and short-term performances, projecting a broadly pessimistic outlook for the firm’s performance in corporate filings, and subsequently disclose favorable news about longer-term prospects.

Our article makes two main contributions. First, our findings deepen the puzzle of how big bath disclosures prevail as firms’ equilibrium strategies in interactions with investors. Prior studies have provided some explanations based on different assumptions. For example, Burgstahler and Dichev (1997) propose that if investors have prospect-theory preferences (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), they may use zero earnings, past earnings, or analysts’ consensus earnings forecasts as reference points to value stocks. Therefore, managers have incentives to avoid reporting earnings that fall just below these reference points by managing earnings upward around them. Conversely, when earnings are sufficiently bad, managers have fewer incentives to manage earnings upward due to investors’ diminishing sensitivity to losses, leading to big bath disclosures. Relatedly, Kirschenheiter and Melumad (2002) model big bath disclosures with investors who infer the precision of reported earnings from financial reports. Guttman, Kadan and Kandel (2006) propose a model in which managers optimally choose a partial pooling strategy that introduces endogenous noise into financial reports, which lowers the costs of manipulation. However, while these models potentially explain why big bath disclosure works in a typical case, they do not readily explain our findings, which arise in a setting where the big bath is predictable due to a forced management turnover.

In our setting, big bath disclosure serves as a tool for managing expectations. Prior research on expectation management has predominantly centered on analysts. A key finding in this literature is that firms strategically walk down analysts' earnings forecasts to beatable levels as earnings announcements approach (e.g., Matsumoto 2002; Richardson, Hong Teoh, and Wysocki 2004). $ ^{5} $ However, it remains unclear whether this “earnings-guidance game” meaningfully impacts investors.

A recent study by Johnson, Kim and So (2020) suggests that firms’ expectation management has indeed influenced returns. Specifically, they apply principal component analysis to summarize firms’ expectation management incentives along three dimensions: attention, pressure, and relevance. They find that firms with strong incentives to manage expectations to beatable levels experience low returns in the month leading up to earnings announcements and high returns during the announcements. Here, an important puzzle arises: investors appear to be consistently misled—how could this happen?

Our findings demonstrate that Johnson, Kim and So (2020) is only the beginning of the story. Investors also get fooled when the expectation management incentives are ex-ante clear and the stakes are very high—the return pattern we document represents massive returns over a short period of time. Moreover, managers’ incentives in our setting are not the subtle manipulation of expectations by a small amount. Investors should recognize new CEOs’ incentives to blame their predecessors, clean up bad news, and lower expectations. Yet, they do not. The fact that our findings occur outside the previously described example of Johnson, Kim and So (2020)—and with significantly larger magnitudes $ ^{6} $—suggests that this return behavior may represent a broader class of mistake, which persists even when the incentives for correcting it are substantial. Specifically, investors appear to take managers’ statements at face value without sufficiently accounting for the incentives managers may have to make misleading statements under particular circumstances. Why does this happen? What does it reveal about the underlying psychology of investor behavior? These are important questions for future research. Before addressing these broader questions, however, it is necessary to first understand the nature of the mistake itself—a task to which our article contributes. $ ^{7} $

Our second contribution is to provide a potential resolution to the mixed findings in the literature on the impacts of CEO turnovers. On one hand, prior research consistently shows that firms' operational performance improves following forced CEO turnovers (e.g., Murphy and Zimmerman 1993; Denis and Denis 1995; Weisbach 1995; Huson, Malatesta, and Parrino 2004; Dasgupta, Li, and Wang 2018). On the other hand, the stock market's reactions to these turnovers are more ambiguous and often economically small. For example, several studies document positive stock price reactions to turnover announcements (e.g., Furtado and Rozeff 1987; Weisbach 1988; Bonnier and Bruner 1989; Denis and Denis 1995; Huson, Parrino, and Starks 2001; Huson, Malatesta, and Parrino 2004). Conversely, other studies report negative reactions (Khanna and Poulsen 1995; Dedman and Lin 2002; Ertugrul and Krishnan 2011), while some find no reaction or mixed evidence (Warner, Watts, and Wruck 1988; Borokhovich, Parrino, and Trapani 1996).

Our study provides a coherent framework to understand new CEOs' incentives for managing market expectations and the timing of bad news and good news disclosures. Unlike prior research, which primarily focuses on short windows around turnover announcements, we examine disclosures and stock returns during and after the first informational events following CEO turnovers. Our findings reveal that new CEOs' expectation management behavior can significantly influence stock returns. Thus, traditional event studies that do not fully control for this effect may underestimate the value implications of CEO turnovers. $ ^{8} $ Moreover, differences in expectation management behavior across samples may help explain the inconsistencies in the literature. For instance, some studies identify

turnover announcements using Wall Street Journal articles (e.g., Denis and Denis 1995; Huson, Malatesta, and Parrino 2004), while others rely on broader datasets, such as ExecuComp and Factiva (e.g., Ertugrul and Krishnan 2011), or examine non-US samples (e.g., Dedman and Lin 2002). These differences in sample construction may lead to significant variations in expectation management behavior across studies, which can, in turn, influence the estimates.

Our article also has important implications for how firms’ different treatment of positive and negative information impacts investors and how investors learn about new CEOs’ skills. For instance, Solomon (2012) finds that investor relations firms spin their clients’ news to generate more media coverage of positive press releases than negative ones and this “selective publicity” significantly influences investors’ expectations and stock returns. Our findings are consistent with Solomon (2012) and further suggest that firms’ predictable timing in releasing bad news and good news has significant impacts on investors. Pan, Wang and Weisbach (2015) find that equity volatility increases following CEO turnovers and then declines in a convex manner because investors learn about the new CEOs’ abilities gradually. $ ^{9} $ Our findings suggest that CEOs’ strategic (incomplete) disclosure can potentially contribute to the gradual nature of this learning.

The remainder of the article proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data and variables. Section 3 presents the empirical results. Section 4 provides a discussion and concludes the article.

## 2. Data and variables

We use data from eight sources: (1) CEO turnover data are from ExecuComp; (2) stock market data are from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP); (3) firms' financial data are from Compustat; (4) data on analysts' forecasts, actual earnings, and management guidance are from I/B/E/S unadjusted summary files, unadjusted actual files, and guidance files, respectively; (5) CEO turnover classification is from Peters and Wagner (2014) $ ^{10} $; (6) the sentiments of company filings are computed using data from Loughran and McDonald (2011) $ ^{11} $; (7) factor returns are obtained from Kenneth French's website $ ^{12} $; and (8) firms' 10-K and 10-Q filings are downloaded from the Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval (EDGAR) system on the Securities and Exchange Commission's website. The sample period is from July 1993 to September 2019, which is determined by the availability of turnover and turnover classification data.

We start with the full ExecuComp sample and then retain only the CEO samples. We identify CEO turnovers by comparing the executive IDs (EXECID) between consecutive firm-year observations and keep only observations with changes in EXECID. We require the departing CEO's age (AGE) and the date of arrival of the new CEO (BECAMECEO) to be non-missing. $ ^{13} $ We restrict our sample to turnovers in which the new CEO takes office within 365 days after the predecessor departs, as a vacancy longer than a year likely reflects a data error or a firm experiencing an unusual situation, such as a merger and acquisition. We also require the departing CEOs to have served for at least 365 days to remove cases with interim CEOs.

We merge the resulting ExecuComp sample with the Compustat and I/B/E/S datasets. We identify the first earnings announcement (EA) and the first management guidance (MG) on or after each CEO turnover date (BECAMECEO). We keep the EAs and MGs that occur within

120 days after the CEO turnover. For accounting and stock market data, we merge the sample with Compustat using gvkey, and then use lpermno in the CRSP/Compustat Merged Database to merge with CRSP. In the few cases where a gvkey maps to more than one permno, we keep only the permno associated with the security that has the largest market value in the month before the turnover. We restrict our sample to common shares (share codes = 10 or 11) traded on the NYSE, Nasdaq, or AMEX exchanges (exchange codes = 1, 2, or 3). We fill missing delisting returns in the daily stock data by -30% (Shumway 1997).

Our study relies on the classification of CEO turnovers as forced or voluntary. The literature acknowledges that such classification is nontrivial because firms often do not explicitly describe a CEO’s departure as “fired” or “forced.” We use the CEO turnover classification constructed by Peters and Wagner (2014), who made a substantial effort to construct this classification following Parrino (1997) algorithm.

We compute the cumulative abnormal return (CAR) from t=m to n relative to the event date of the EA or MG at t=0 as:

 $$ CAR_{i}=\sum_{t=m}^{n}AR_{i,t}, $$ 

where  $ AR_{i,t} $ is the abnormal return of stock i on day t. We use the Fama–French–Carhart six-factor model (Fama and French 2015; Carhart 1997) to measure the expected return.  $ AR_{i,t} $ is calculated as the realized return minus the expected return:

 $$ \begin{aligned}A R_{i,t}&=r_{i,t}-\Bigg[r_{f,t}+\beta_{i,t}^{MKT}(r_{m,t}-r_{f,t})+\beta_{i,t}^{SMB}SMB_{t}+\beta_{i,t}^{HML}HML_{t}\\&\quad+\beta_{i,t}^{CMA}CMA_{t}+\beta_{i,t}^{RMW}RMW_{t}+\beta_{i,t}^{UMD}UMD_{t}\Bigg],\end{aligned} $$ 

where  $ r_{i,t} $ is stock i's return on day t;  $ r_{f,t} $ is the risk-free rate of return on day t;  $ r_{m,t} - r_{f,t}, SMB_t, HML_t, CMA_t, RMW_t, $ and  $ UMD_t $ are the factor returns on day t;  $ \beta_{i,t} $'s are the stock's factor loadings estimated with 1 year (252 trading days) of daily data ending thirty-one trading days prior to the event date. We require firms to have at least 120 days of return data during the parameter estimation period. Finally, we require firms to have nonmissing market values of equity, book-to-market equity, profitability, asset growth, and past 12-month return in the month prior to the CEO turnover.

## 3. Empirical results

### 3.1 CEO turnover, informational event, and stock returns

In Table 2, we examine the drop in stock prices around EAs and MGs by regressing the 3-day CARs on a forced turnover dummy and control variables. Columns 1 and 2 consider all EAs or MGs that occur within 120 calendar days following CEO turnovers. The coefficients on the forced dummy (Forced) indicate that EAs and MGs following forced turnovers are associated with 0.87 percent and 2.08 percent lower 3-day CARs (annualized at -73.1% and -174.7%, respectively) compared with those following voluntary turnovers, with t-values of -2.13 and -2.36, respectively.

Under the strategic disclosure hypothesis, we expect that the low returns should be more pronounced if the informational events occur shortly after the turnovers (versus after a delay) because new CEOs have incentives to disclose bad news sooner rather than later. ### 3.2 Asymmetric reversal of pre-earnings-announcement returns

Next, we investigate whether new CEOs are more likely to disclose bad news if stocks earn higher returns before informational events. In most of the tests that follow, we avoid mixing the EA and MG samples and focus only on the EA sample. We shift our focus because firms that provide MGs are self-selected, which can introduce endogeneity concerns and complicate the interpretation of results regarding the underlying economic mechanisms.

A nuanced aspect of our story is that new CEOs respond asymmetrically to positive and negative returns prior to EAs because they have a strong incentive to disclose bad news to tame investors' overoptimism, but a weaker incentive to disclose good news to boost optimism when expectations are low. Therefore, we consider a specification that allows for different predictive coefficients on positive and negative pre-EA CARs. We expect that in the forced turnover sample, positive pre-EA CARs will reverse during EAs, whereas negative pre-EA CARs will not. The regression equation is

In contrast, the differences in the reversals of negative pre-EA CARs are indistinguishable from zero. The magnitudes of the reversals are similar across the full- and recent-forced-turnover samples, suggesting that a CEO’s incentive to tame expectations remains strong throughout the first few months of their tenure.

The results in Table 5 are rather nuanced, and hence, they lend considerable support to the idea that new CEOs make disclosure and expectation management decisions in response to stock market movements. In later sections, we corroborate these findings using nonprice-based outcomes such as earnings surprises, analysts' forecast revisions, textual tones in corporate filings, and large language model (LLM)-based measures.

### 3.3 Uncertainty, CEO turnover, and stock returns

We next investigate whether the V-shaped return pattern is more pronounced among stocks with higher volatility. The intuition is that firms with high stock volatility are more likely to hold both bad and good news, providing new CEOs with greater opportunities to strategically sequence their disclosures. To test this hypothesis, we modify the regressions in Tables 2 and 3 by interacting Forced with the (natural logarithm of) idiosyncratic volatility of the stock. We expect to see a negative coefficient on the interaction term when predicting the CARs around EAs, and a positive coefficient when predicting post-EA CARs.

Table 6 presents the results. In columns 1 and 2, the coefficients on Forced× log(I−volatility) are −2.45 and −3.87 for the full and recent-turnover samples, respectively, with corresponding t-values of −2.84 and −2.23. These estimates indicate that idiosyncratic volatility more strongly predicts low returns during EAs in the forced turnover sample. The coefficients on the log(I−volatility) main effects are 0.07 and 0.98, which are not significant, suggesting that the inverse relationship between idiosyncratic volatility and EA returns is present only in the forced turnover sample.

Columns 3 and 4 present the results regarding post-EA CARs. Again, consistent with what we expect, the coefficients on Forced×log(I-volatility) are positive and significant, with values of 9.26 and 15.35 (t-values of 2.02 and 2.31) for the full- and recent-turnover samples, respectively. The insignificant coefficients on log (I-volatility) suggest that the positive association between idiosyncratic volatility and post-event return is present only in the forced turnover sample.

### 3.4 Economic mechanism

We have thus far established a set of results regarding stock returns that support the expectation management hypothesis. We consider three types of information: information about realized earnings, about future earnings, and managers' semantic tone. $ ^{22} $

#### 3.4.3 Good news for the longer term

Next, we investigate what good news might lead to the high returns after EAs among firms experiencing forced turnovers. Immediately after new CEOs take office, there is relatively little they can do to alter the firms' short-term performance, as this is largely determined by past investments. However, the CEOs can influence the market's expectations by communicating to the market how their future investment plans will impact the firms' long-term performance. Therefore, we hypothesize that the good news might be related to longer-term earnings (i.e., beyond the current fiscal year). To test this, we measure firms' disclosure of long- versus short-horizon earnings news using analysts' forecast revisions for long- and short-horizon earnings. If the firm announces good news about its long- (short-) horizon earnings, then we should find that analysts revise their long- (short-) horizon forecasts upward, and vice versa.

We consider three measures: (1) analysts’ revisions of long-term growth forecasts,  $ \Delta LTG $; (2) revisions of 1-year-ahead earnings forecast,  $ \Delta FE^{y1} $; (3) the difference between the revisions in 2-year-ahead and 1-year-ahead earnings forecasts,  $ \Delta FE^{y2} - \Delta FE^{y1} $. These are defined as follows:

 $$ \Delta L T G_{i,t}=L T G_{i,t}-L T G_{i,t-1}, $$ 

 $$ \Delta F E_{i,t}^{y1}=F E_{i,t}^{y1}-F E_{i,t-1}^{y1}, $$ 

 $$ \Delta F E_{i,t}^{y2}-\Delta F E_{i,t}^{y1}=(F E_{i,t}^{y2}-F E_{i,t-1}^{y2})-(F E_{i,t}^{y1}-F E_{i,t-1}^{y1}), $$ 

where  $ LTG_{i,t} $ denotes analysts' consensus (mean) long-term growth forecast for firm i in month t, and  $ FE_{i,t}^{y1} $ and  $ FE_{i,t}^{y2} $, respectively, denote the consensus 1-year- and 2-year-ahead earnings forecasts, scaled by the lagged market value of equity.

If the high post-EA stock returns among forced-turnover firms are driven by good news about longer-term earnings, their  $ \Delta LTG $ should be more positive compared to that of voluntary-turnover firms in the post-EA period. In Figure 3, we plot the average  $ \Delta LTG $ from 2 months before to 12 months after the first EAs following CEO turnovers. The figure shows that in the month leading up to the EA, the forecast revisions for forced-turnover firms (solid line) are more negative than those for voluntary-turnover forms (dashed line). During the EA month  $ (t=0) $, analysts, on average, revise their long-term growth forecasts downward by approximately 0.13 percentage points for forced-turnover firms, compared to only 0.07 percentage points for voluntary-turnover firms. However, the solid line rises and mostly remains above the dashed line starting from the fourth month  $ (t=4) $ after the EA. The dashed line is outside the 95 percent confidence interval of the solid line in months 7, 9, and 11.

In Figure 4, we plot analysts' earnings forecast revisions across different horizons. Panel A shows that analysts do not revise short-term earnings forecasts more positively for forced-turnover firms after EAs. Instead, they continue to revise short-term forecasts more strongly downward for these firms compared to voluntary-turnover firms, as indicated by the solid line consistently remaining below the dashed line. This pattern rules out the explanation that the high post-EA returns are driven by good news about short-term earnings.

### 3.7 Alternative explanation: time-varying risk

Thus far, we have attributed our findings to investors responding to CEOs’ expectation management behaviors. However, we do not think that a complex outcome such as the observed V-shaped return pattern is entirely attributable to a single cause. In this subsection, we consider time variation in risks as an alternative explanation.

Why might risks play a role? The V-shaped return pattern is consistent with a model in which a firm becomes more (systematically) risky during its first informational event after a forced CEO turnover. Specifically, besides the narrative about earnings news portrayed in previous sections, we can also hypothesize that the news released during an informational event increases a firm's expected risks. This hypothesis is plausible—though it does not easily generate our empirical results regarding earnings news—because, for instance, if an EA reveals information about the new CEO's investment plans, investors might perceive these plans to be more (systematically) risky than the status quo. As a result, investors would demand a higher risk premium to hold the stock, leading to an initial drop in the stock price. The subsequent high return on this stock could then be attributed to its high risk premium.

### 3.8 Further analysis

The expectation management hypothesis makes additional predictions regarding the roles of investors' sophistication and arbitrage frictions. We present further analyses to evaluate these predictions in Appendix C and summarize the key findings here.

In Table C1, we test the hypotheses that positive pre-EA CAR should more strongly reverse during EAs if the stock has low institutional ownership and high volatility. The intuition behind these hypotheses is as follows: sophisticated investors are likely aware of CEOs’ incentives to disclose bad news during EAs and, as a result, refrain from bidding up the share price before EAs (unless they observe material information that justifies a higher stock price). Following the literature, we use institutional ownership fraction to proxy for investor sophistication. $ ^{26} $

Furthermore, we propose that stock volatility can serve as an ex-ante indicator of CEOs' incentives to strategically disclose information, as firms with high stock volatility are more likely to have both bad and good news awaiting disclosure. Thus, sophisticated investors are less likely to bid up the prices of high-volatility stocks prior to EAs. Conversely, high pre-EA returns for high-volatility stocks may signal relatively low levels of investor sophistication, as these investors appear overly excited about the turnovers too early. Therefore, we expect positive pre-EA returns to reverse more strongly during EAs for high-volatility stocks. The results presented in Table C1 provide strong support for both predictions.

In addition, we investigate the role of arbitrage frictions. If the V-shaped return pattern is about market inefficiencies, we expect the magnitude of this pattern to correlate with measures of arbitrage frictions. To test this, we consider three widely used proxies for different types of arbitrage frictions: illiquidity, firm size, and institutional ownership. Specifically, arbitrage frictions increase with illiquidity because of trading costs (Amihud 2002), decrease with firm size because of the informational environment (Hong, Lim and Stein 2000), and decrease with institutional ownership because of investor sophistication, monitoring, and the supply of shortable shares (Asquith, Pathak and Ritter 2005; Ben-Rephael, Da and Israelsen 2017). $ ^{27} $ The results in Table C2 support these predictions, showing that the V-shaped return pattern is positively correlated with arbitrage frictions.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

This article examines the stock market impacts of new CEOs' expectation management behaviors. We document that firms with recent forced CEO turnovers earn large negative stock returns during, and positive stock returns after, the first informational events following the turnovers. This pattern is more pronounced among firms with higher stock returns and higher stock volatility leading up to the informational events. Consistent with the expectation management hypothesis, these firms sequence the disclosure of bad news and good news. Specifically, they announce negative earnings surprises and receive downward earnings forecast

revisions from analysts during the informational events. After the disclosure of bad news, however, analysts' longer-term earnings forecasts for these firms improve, indicating a subsequent release of good news about these firms' long-term prospects to the market. Furthermore, we use ChatGPT to extract from corporate filings new managers' initial optimism levels over both the short and long horizons, as well as their likelihood of engaging in strategic disclosure. Empirical evidence based on these measures provides additional support for our hypothesis.

In an efficient market, investors price assets using all available information. Hence, it is puzzling that while firms' operational performance generally improves following CEO turnovers (e.g., Murphy and Zimmerman 1993; Denis and Denis 1995; Weisbach 1995; Huson, Malatesta, and Parrino 2004; Dasgupta, Li, and Wang 2018), existing evidence indicates that the stock market fails to fully reflect these impacts during turnover announcements. Our findings provide a potential resolution by highlighting the importance of accounting for new CEOs' expectation management behaviors when assessing the value consequences of CEO turnovers.

While our findings help resolve one puzzle, they significantly deepen another. We find that managers’ expectation management tactics are remarkably effective in influencing stock prices even when such tactics are ex-ante predictable in the context of forced turnovers. These results resonate with those in Johnson, Kim and So (2020) and suggest that investors’ failure to discern the incentives behind firms’ disclosure may represent a general class of mistakes. The substantially larger magnitudes that we document indicate that this class of mistake persists even when the stakes are dramatically scaled up. As such, this form of investor “gullibility” emerges as an economically important source of irrationality—one that has largely escaped researchers’ attention.

Why do investors exhibit such gullibility? What might this reveal about their underlying psychology? A potentially promising avenue for addressing these questions may lie in developing and testing realistic models that incorporate psychological elements such as Theory of Mind (ToM). ToM refers to agents' ability to infer the incentives, desires, and beliefs of other goal-directed agents (e.g., see Bruguier, Quartz, and Bossaerts 2010 and Frydman and Camerer 2016), which is a critical skill for navigating social interactions. In the context of financial markets, ToM could help explain how investors interpret—or misinterpret—the motives and actions of managers and other market participants. In our setting, the limitation in understanding the mental states of new CEOs may contribute to the observed gullibility, as investors overly rely on surface-level information without sufficiently accounting for the underlying motivations driving information disclosures. These insights from psychology raise broader questions about the cognitive processes shaping investor behavior in the presence of multiple frictions such as cognitive constraints, agency problems, and asymmetric information. Such questions appear to be fundamental and might be fruitful for future research.

### Appendix B: Measuring optimism and strategic disclosure using large language model

This section details the measurement of managerial optimism and strategic disclosure likelihood using ChatGPT. Our methods are similar to those in concurrent studies such as Chen et al., (2023), Kim, Muhn and Nikolaev (2023), Lopez-Lira and Tang (2023), Chen et al. (2024), and Jha et al. (2024). We use GPT-3.5, which is the most popular GPT model for everyday tasks at the time of our analysis. We specify the model parameters as follows:

1) temperature = 0. This ensures minimal variability in the generated responses across trials.

2) max_tokens = 4,096. This parameter specifies the maximum number of tokens that the model can generate in its response. Here, 4,096 is the upper limit of the model.

3)  $ top\_p = 1 $. This means the model considers the entire probability distribution of token predictions without applying any filtering. This allows for maximum diversity in the outputs, as it does not limit the selection of the tokens based on their cumulative probability.

4) presence_penalty = 0 and frequency_penalty = 0. By setting these two values to zero, the model applies no penalty based on token appearance or frequency in the generated text.

We measure managerial optimism (overall, short-term, and long-term) and strategic disclosure likelihood from the MD&A section in firms' 10-K or 10-Q filed after the first EAs following the turnovers. We restrict the sample to EAs occurring within 30 days following the turnovers. We first download the 10-Ks and 10-Qs from EDGAR for this sample. Then, we manually extract the MD&A sections and save them as separate files. Finally, we input these MD&A texts into ChatGPT as part of the prompts to generate responses. The prompt used to generate the overall optimism measure is as follows:

Please clear your memory. The following text is the part MANAGEMENT'S DISCUSSION AND ANALYSIS from firms' SEC filings, including annual reports (10-K) or quarterly reports (10-Q). You are a finance expert. Please carefully read the text. Based on only each of the text, please answer the following question. What is the attitude toward the firm's future performance? There are five choices: optimistic, slightly optimistic, neutral, slightly pessimistic, or pessimistic. Please select one of the above five choices for each question and provide a one-sentence explanation of your choice for each question. Note that all these five choices are valid and you can decisively choose any of the options. The format for the answer to the question should be choice—explanation. You should only have one answer to evaluate the overall attitude for each file, and the explanation should be no more than three sentences. If no relevant information is provided related to the question, answer no information is provided. Here is the text: [insert MD&A text here]

When measuring short-term and long-term optimism, we replace the bolded phrase “firm’s future performance” with “firm’s performance in the short term future” and “firm’s performance in the long term future.” We then label the responses with numerical values  $ \{2,1,0,-1,-2\} $, where 2 corresponds to optimistic, 1 slightly optimistic, 0 neutral, -1 slightly pessimistic, and -2 pessimistic. We removed a small number of cases in which ChatGPT finds no relevant information.

To measure the likelihood of strategic disclosure, we provide the following prompt to ChatGPT:

The following text is the MANAGEMENT'S DISCUSSION AND ANALYSIS section from firms' SEC filings, including annual reports (10-K) or quarterly reports (10-Q) after a CEO turnover. You are a finance expert. Please carefully read the text. Based on this text alone, please infer whether the incoming CEO is trying to strategically attribute poor performance to the predecessor and estimate the likelihood that the attribution is directly related to historical managerial decisions. Your answer should be 'Number-Explanation,' where 'Number' is a number between 0 and 1 representing your estimated likelihood, and 'Explanation' is a short explanation of your estimate. If no relevant information is provided, answer 'no information is provided.' Here is the text: [insert MD&A text here]
