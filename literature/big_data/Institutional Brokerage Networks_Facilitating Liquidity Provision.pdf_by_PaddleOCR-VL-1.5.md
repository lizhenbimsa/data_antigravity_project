# Institutional Brokerage Networks: Facilitating Liquidity Provision $ ^{*} $

Munhee Han Sanghyun (Hugh) Kim Vikram K. Nanda

February 6, 2024

## Abstract

We argue that institutional brokerage networks facilitate liquidity provision and mitigate the price impact of large non-information-motivated trades. Using commissions, we map trading networks of mutual funds (institutions) and their brokers. Central funds (institutions) tend to outperform their peripheral counterparts in terms of return gap (execution shortfall). This outperformance is more pronounced when funds experience large outflows and for large trades in less liquid stocks. Central brokers can deliver superior trade execution compared to peripheral brokers, but mainly for central institutions. We use the collapse of Lehman Brothers as a quasi-natural experiment to establish the likely causality of our findings.

JEL classification: G14, G23, G24

Keywords: Institutional brokerage networks, mutual funds, return gap, trading costs, liquidity provision

## 1 Introduction

The U.S. equity market has undergone immense changes in recent years. Significant advances in information processing and communications technologies have led to the emergence of alternative trading venues, dark pools, and high-frequency trading. $ ^{1} $ A natural question in this context is whether traditionally important intermediaries, specifically broker-dealers, retain an economically meaningful role in a world with high levels of automated, algorithmic trading. An indication of their continuing relevance is that, despite the shift to electronic trading, most institutional investors still rely on ‘high-touch’ (non-electronic) trading, where orders are placed with brokers over the phone (Di Maggio et al., 2022). Anecdotal evidence also suggests that large institutions might still prefer to trade in blocks ‘upstairs’ because the proliferation of algorithmic trading and the fragmentation of the market might hinder their ability to obtain best prices and execute large trades quickly. $ ^{2} $

We propose and test the ‘liquidity provision hypothesis’ that centrality in institutional brokerage networks is valuable when mutual funds (and other institutions) are forced to trade large quantities of shares for liquidity reasons. Open-end mutual funds incur substantial trading costs due to the adverse market impact of their trades when they liquidate holdings in response to investor redemptions (Edelen, 1999). Central brokers are likely to be well informed about market conditions and in touch with a wider set of institutional clients. By reassuring counterparties about trading motives, central brokers can mitigate the price impact when mutual funds trade for liquidity reasons (Seppi, 1990). $ ^{3} $

In addition, central brokers will be more capable of expanding the available liquidity pool

by using information about their clients’ latent trading interests and reaching out to a larger set of potential counterparties to facilitate liquidity provision (Grossman, 1992). $ ^{4} $ Thus, even though institutions may have similar access to the available pool of expressed liquidity, such as through an electronic limit order book, those connected to many central brokers may be able to tap into larger pools of ‘unexpressed’ liquidity, especially when trading large blocks of illiquid stocks.

Institutions, however, may be concerned about communicating their trading motives with brokers. Evidence suggests that broker-dealers can leak order flow information that may benefit certain clients at the expense of others (Boyarchenko et al., 2021). In particular, Barbon et al. (2019) show that brokers can foster predatory trading (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2005) by leaking order flow information about impending fire sales to other important clients. While brokers may occasionally leak their clients' order flow information, they are less likely to do so against their important clients, such as central institutions. Consequently, central institutions should be less likely to suffer from order flow information leakage and associated adverse price impact.

We construct institutional brokerage networks using two main sources of data. Our first dataset comes from a dataset on brokerage commissions for a comprehensive sample of mutual funds from Form N-SAR semi-annual reports filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). We also use Abel Noser institutional daily transactions data, which allow for detailed transaction-level analyses for institutions that provide data to Abel Noser for the transaction cost analysis (TCA) service.

Using techniques from graph theory, we map the connections between institutions and their brokers as affiliation networks (Faust, 1997). We then use eigenvector centrality to quantify the importance of a given institution's position in the network in which any pair of institutions is connected only indirectly through their overlapping brokerage connections. Institutions that trade

through many brokers that are themselves central will also be central in the network.

We begin our empirical analysis by showing that mutual funds' centrality positively predicts their trading performance. Since we do not directly observe trading activities of mutual funds, we use the return gap as our measure of trading performance. The return gap is calculated as the difference between the reported fund return (gross of expenses) and the return on a hypothetical portfolio that invests in the previously disclosed fund holdings (Grinblatt and Titman, 1989; Kacperczyk et al., 2008). We find that mutual funds' centrality positively predicts future return gap, which we refer to as the centrality premium. The economic magnitude of the centrality premium is quite meaningful. When sorted on network centrality, top quintile funds outperform bottom quintile funds by 6.9 basis points per month.

Since the return gap reflects not only trading costs such as bid-ask spread, price impact, and brokerage commissions, but also a host of other factors including interim (intra-quarter) trading performance (Kacperczyk et al., 2008; Puckett and Yan, 2011), we provide further evidence on the centrality premium based on a direct measure of trading costs using Abel Noser data. For each trade order, we compute the execution shortfall as the percentage difference between the execution price and the benchmark price prevailing at the time when the broker receives the order. We then measure the institution's trading costs by monthly institution fixed-effects, as proposed by Anand et al. (2012), from a regression of execution shortfall on a host of trade-, stock-, and market-level control variables that can impact trading costs. Consistent with our argument, we find that network centrality is associated with lower execution shortfall. When sorted on network centrality, top quintile institutions incur 4.3 basis points lower trading costs than bottom quintile institutions.

Next, we turn to testing key predictions of our liquidity provision hypothesis. The primary prediction is that the centrality premium should be more pronounced when funds' trading activities are largely driven by liquidity motives and this is either evident to their brokers or can be credibly communicated to them. We use fund flows to identify such periods of liquidity-motivated trading. When a mutual fund is experiencing large outflows, the fund is forced to liquidate a large fraction

of its holdings in several stocks and their trading is, to a large extent, uninformed (Coval and Stafford, 2007). In our double-sorts tests based on network centrality and fund flows, we find that the centrality premium as measured by return gap is more pronounced when funds experience large outflows, consistent with our liquidity provision hypothesis.

The potential contribution of brokers to expanding the available liquidity pool will likely be more significant for large trades of illiquid stocks that are less actively traded. To test this prediction, we first measure the illiquidity of each stock in each quarter using the Amihud (2002)'s illiquidity measure. We then aggregate the stock-level illiquidity measure to the fund level by computing the value-weighted average of the illiquidity measures across all stocks held by each fund at the end of each quarter. When double sorted on network centrality and illiquidity of stock holdings, we find that the centrality premium as measured by return gap is greater among mutual funds that tend to hold less liquid stocks.

Further, we extend our holdings-level analysis using mutual funds data to a transactions-level analysis using Abel Noser data. When double sorted on network centrality and order size (relative to the average daily volume), we find that when placing large trade orders with the brokers, central institutions incur significantly lower trading costs than do peripheral institutions. This outperformance, however, substantially weakens and becomes statistically insignificant when institutions place small trade orders with the brokers.

Trading costs, however, are closely linked to trading desks’ execution skills over and above selecting better brokers (Anand et al., 2012). This raises the concern that network centrality and associated execution costs might be correlated with institutions’ execution skills. To disentangle institutions’ execution skills from brokers’ liquidity provision, we allow the institution’s trading costs to vary across the brokers they trade through by using the monthly institution×broker fixed-effects. When institution-broker pairs are double sorted on the network centrality of institutions and that of brokers, we find that the outperformance of central institutions over peripheral institutions in trade execution is greater when institutions trade through central brokers rather than through

peripheral brokers. Central brokers connected to many institutions can deliver superior trade execution compared to peripheral brokers, but mainly for central institutions.

To further mitigate potential endogeneity concerns, $ ^{5} $ we use the exogenous shock to the brokerage industry, as in Di Maggio et al. (2017), to show that brokerage connections help lower trading costs in a quasi-natural experiment setting. For each institution, we compute the fraction of commissions paid to Lehman Brothers in the year prior to its collapse as a measure of its trading relationship with the broker. We find that the mutual funds/institutions that were more connected to Lehman Brothers prior to its collapse experienced a significant decrease in network centrality in the aftermath of its collapse. More importantly, this decrease in network centrality following the collapse of Lehman Brothers was accompanied by a similar decrease (increase) in return gap (execution shortfall) for mutual funds (institutions) that were more connected to Lehman Brothers prior to its collapse, suggesting that the relation that we document is likely causal.

Finally, our liquidity provision hypothesis requires that potential counterparties connected through common brokers should be in a position to provide liquidity for the institutions facing liquidity shocks. But what if these connected institutions hold overlapping portfolios and suffer contemporaneous liquidity shocks? We explore this question by following Coval and Stafford (2007) and find that price impacts are larger for fire sales in which liquidating mutual funds are more densely connected through overlapping brokerage relationships. Thus, brokerage connections can have a downside: While brokerage connections tend to benefit mutual funds when faced with idiosyncratic liquidity shocks, connections to funds with similar holdings can be detrimental when there are correlated liquidity shocks.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we discuss our paper in the context of related literature. To provide institutional background, we discuss the status of off-exchange block trading in the U.S. equity market in Section 3. Section 4 introduces our data

and describes how we construct our samples and variables. We report our results in Sections 5, 6, 7 and 8. Section 9 concludes.

## 2 Related Literature

Our paper uncovers novel network effects in equity markets by documenting superior trading performance (lower trading costs) associated with centrality in institutional brokerage networks. Our focus on the potential role played by the network centrality of institutions and brokers in determining trading outcomes is closely related to recent papers on dealer networks in decentralized fixed-income markets. The evidence on whether dealer centrality improves trading outcomes is mixed: Hollifield et al. (2017) find that central dealers charge customers lower spreads than peripheral dealers in structured product markets, whereas Di Maggio et al. (2017) and Li and Schürhoff (2019) find the opposite relation in corporate and municipal bond markets, respectively. By examining institutional trading in the U.S. equity market, our paper provides novel evidence on whether central brokers deliver better prices to clients than peripheral brokers and, more importantly, whether central institutions obtain better trade execution from brokers than peripheral institutions.

Unlike our paper, many studies using fixed-income market data, such as the Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine (TRACE) data, are unable to measure the network centrality of institutions (clients) because the data lack customer identifiers. An exception can be found in studies using the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) data, which provide identifiers for insurers and dealers involved in trades. Schultz (2001) finds that large insurers obtain better prices than small insurers. O'Hara et al. (2018) finds that more active traders obtain better prices, especially from prominent dealers who are more capable of discriminating among clients. Henderson (2020) shows that while one-third of insurers use only one dealer, many insurers establish relationships with multiple dealers, trading off the benefits of repeat business and faster execution. Our paper is broadly consistent with these papers on client-dealer networks using the

NAIC data.

Investors place significant value on the formal and informal research provided by brokers (Di Maggio et al., 2022). Irvine et al. (2007) shows that buy-side institutions can gain informational advantage through early access to sell-side research, or 'tipping.' Chung and Kang (2016) documents strong return co-movement among hedge funds sharing the same prime broker, suggesting that the prime broker provides profitable information to its hedge fund clients (Kumar et al., 2020). Di Maggio et al. (2019) shows that central brokers can extrapolate large informed trades from order flows and selectively leak this information to their more important clients, thereby promoting 'back-running' (Yang and Zhu, 2019). Our paper suggests that, despite such rent-extraction behavior, central brokers tend to obtain 'best execution,' especially for central clients.

The evidence suggesting an active role of brokers in leaking order flow information, as found in recent papers, is troublesome for clients. Barbon et al. (2019) documents that brokers appear to foster predatory trading (Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2005) by disclosing order flow information about impending fire sales to other important clients. Our paper complements Barbon et al. (2019) in the sense of Carlin et al. (2007), who presents a multi-period model of trading where both cooperation and conflict can coexist. In their model, traders cooperate most of the time through repeated interaction, but this cooperation occasionally breaks down when stakes are high, leading to predatory trading. Our paper shows that whether brokers facilitate liquidity provision or foster predatory trading depends on the nature of connections between brokers and their clients.

## 3 Institutional background

In this section, we briefly describe the nature of off-exchange block trading in the U.S. equity market and its use by brokers in facilitating liquidity provision. Unlike low-touch trading, where small orders can be directly routed to an exchange or other trading platforms with little price impact, high-touch trading involves a trader submitting large orders to a broker, typically over the phone. This voice trading allows traders and their brokers to communicate additional instructions

(beyond order details such as symbol, side, quantity, and price) and insights on current market conditions. According to Greenwich Associates (2017), despite the shift to electronic trading over the last 15 years, high-touch trading still represents the largest execution channel for institutions, accounting for 54% of equity trading commissions in 2016 and an estimated 49% in the three subsequent years.

For a large block trade, instead of immediately routing the order to an exchange, a broker can seek to find another institution willing to take the other side of the trade. Brokers with a wide set of trading relationships with institutions might be expected to be more effective at locating counterparties, as well as reassuring them about trading motives. Once a counterparty is located, orders from both institutions would be ‘crossed’ against each other and reported to the consolidated tape. The crossing price is typically set to be the prevailing mid-price. Alternatively, a broker can act as a dealer and ‘internalize’ a large block order, i.e., execute the order against its own account. According to Rosenblatt Securities (2022), such ‘capital commitment’ (internalization) and ‘manual crossing’ of institutional trading interests constituted 22% of off-exchange volume and 9% of the total volume in May 2022.

Using NYSE Trade and Quote (TAQ) data, we examine the level of off-exchange block trading activities in the U.S. equity market for the period from 2008 to 2022. Our finding is that block-trading accounts for a substantial level of overall trading volume. To identify block trades, we first restrict trades to those executed off-exchange (TAQ Market D) since a block trade, once executed off-exchange, is reported via a trading reporting facility (TRF). We then define a trade as a block if the trade is larger than the threshold of 5% of the average daily volume (ADV). We also use 1% of ADV as an alternative threshold. The ADV is defined for each stock as the daily CRSP volume averaged over the past 30 days. The 1% of ADV definition may identify too many trades as 'blocks,' whereas the 5% of ADV definition is deliberately conservative and likely to underestimate the prevalence of block trading activities. $ ^{6} $

Figure 1a shows the time-series of block volume as a percentage of off-exchange volume. Until 2019, block volume hovered around 14-34% of off-exchange volume, depending on the threshold used to identify blocks. In mid-2020, block volume abruptly dropped to 8-24% of off-exchange volume, likely influenced by the surge in retail trading activities rather than a decline in institutional block trading. $ ^{7} $ Block volume rebounded to 12-29% of off-exchange volume by the end of 2022 as the surge in retail trading subsided. Figure 1b depicts the time-series of block volume as a percentage of consolidated volume (measured by CRSP volume). Block volume, as a percentage of CRSP volume, hovered around 7-14% until 2019, dropped to around 4-10% during mid-2020, and then rebounded to around 7-14% by the end of 2022. Overall, our results suggest that block trading has not declined significantly in the last 15 years and still represents about 10% of the total equity trading volume.

Furthermore, block trades are typically quite sizable, as shown in figures 1c and 1d. Figure 1c provides the time-series of daily average block trades in terms of the number of shares. A typical block trade involves around 30,000 shares (when blocks are defined as those larger than 1% of ADV) and 40,000 to 80,000 shares (when blocks are defined as those larger than 5% of ADV). Figure 1d shows the time-series of daily average block trades in terms of dollar amounts, suggesting that block trades can be worth several millions of dollars.

### 4.1 Mutual funds sample

Our data on brokerage commissions comes from the Form N–SAR reports, which we combine with other mutual funds datasets. We obtain data on mutual fund monthly returns, total net assets (TNA), and fund expenses from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) Survivor-Bias-Free Mutual Fund Database. The returns are net of fees, expenses, and brokerage commissions, but before any front-end or back-end loads. The stock holdings of mutual funds are from Thomson-Reuters Ownership Database (Thomson s12). We use the MFLINKS files available through Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS) to merge CRSP and Thomson datasets. For funds with multiple share classes in CRSP, we aggregate share-class-level variables at the fund-level by computing the sum of total net assets and the value-weighted averages of returns and expenses.

Since our focus is on U.S. domestic equity funds, we eliminate international, municipal, bonds and preferred, and metals funds using the investment objective code from Thomson (ioc) and screen for U.S. domestic equity funds using the investment objective code from CRSP (crsp_obj_cd). We also exclude all observations where the fund's TNA does not exceed $1 million or the number of stocks held does not exceed 10.

Under the Investment Company Act of 1940, all registered investment companies are required to file Form N-SAR with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) on a semi-annual basis. N-SAR reports are filed at the investment company (registrant) level. An investment company can consist of multiple funds or be part of a fund family. N-SAR filings disclose information about fund operations and financials under 133 numbered items with alphabetized sub-items. Many items are reported at the fund level, but some items, such as brokerage commissions, are aggregated and reported at the investment company level. We extract N-SAR reports through the SEC's Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval (EDGAR) system.

Of particular interest to our study from N–SAR reports are brokerage commissions paid to the 10 brokers that received the largest amounts from the investment company during the reporting period and the identities of those brokers (Item 20). We provide an example of brokerage commission payments along with descriptive statistics in Section A of the Internet Appendix. $ ^{8} $

As described in Anand et al. (2012), a typical institutional trading process starts with a portfolio manager making investment decisions, determining which stocks to buy or sell and the quantities to be traded. The portfolio manager then sends the orders to a trader within the same institution (trading desk). Finally, the trader aggregates all orders from portfolio managers and submits them to brokers. Thus, to reflect the institutional trading process, we aggregate brokerage commission payments across investment companies within the same fund family, following Reuter (2006). Specifically, we first convert brokerage commission payments into equal monthly installments over the reporting period since we do not know the exact timing of those payments. We then sum these monthly payments across investment companies to estimate the total brokerage commission payments made by each mutual fund family (institution) to each broker every month. To reduce noise in the data, we require that brokers have at least 10 institutional clients each month.

Using brokerage commission payments, we map trading networks of institutions and their brokers as affiliation networks where institutions are connected to all other institutions through their overlapping brokerage connections (see Section B of the Internet Appendix for details on our network construction). Borrowing techniques from graph theory and social network literature, we use eigenvector centrality (Bonacich, 1972, 1987) to quantify the importance of an institution's position in the network. Figure 2 shows institutional brokerage networks constructed using our N-SAR data for June 2016, with the size of a node proportional to its eigenvector centrality.

Our mutual funds sample covers 2,966 funds from 285 families (institutions) trading through 134 brokers for the period from June 1998 to December 2017. We report descriptive statistics in Table 1, Panel A. To examine the characteristics of network centrality, we first sort mutual funds/institutions into quintiles each month on the basis of network centrality measured at the end of the previous month. We then average each variable across all mutual funds/institutions belonging to each quintile and report the time-series average of each variable in each quintile and the difference between the extreme quintiles (Q5 - Q1).

Central institutions (Q5) tend to be large fund families compared to peripheral institutions (Q1). Central institutions pay a larger amount in brokerage commissions, averaging $1.7 million each month, whereas peripheral institutions pay only an average of $20,000 monthly. Central institutions also employ a larger number of brokers, trading through an average of 18 brokers each month, whereas peripheral institutions employ an average of only 6 brokers monthly. Thus, as we might expect, institutions that trade through the brokers that many other institutions also trade through tend to be central in the network. $ ^{9} $ In addition, central funds tend to be larger and have a lower expense ratio than peripheral funds, while central funds have a similar turnover ratio as peripheral funds. ### 4.2 Abel Noser institutions sample

We complement our analysis based on our mutual funds sample by using institutional daily transactions data obtained from Abel Noser Solutions (formerly Ancerno Ltd.), a leading consulting firm that provides transaction cost analysis for institutional clients. Our Abel Noser data cover equity transactions made by a large sample of institutions. For each transaction, the data provide information on the date of transaction, the stock traded (identified by CUSIP), the number of shares traded, whether it is a buy or sell by the institution, the transaction price, various benchmark prices, the broker executing the trade, and the commissions paid to the broker.

Abel Noser clients include investment managers such as Fidelity, Putnam Investments, and Lazard Asset Management and pension plan sponsors such as the California Public Employee's Retirement System (CalPERS), the Commonwealth of Virginia, and the YMCA retirement fund. We note that this dataset is limited to institutions that provide their transactions to Abel Noser for transaction cost analysis. Because of this selection, Abel Noser institutions might be more mindful about their best execution obligations than the average 13F institution (Anand et al., 2012). Despite data coverage limitations, Abel Noser data allow us to extend our analysis to a more granular level and provide greater insight into the findings from our mutual funds sample.

By aggregating brokerage commissions for each institution-broker every month, we separately construct institutional brokerage networks for our Abel Noser sample, as in Section 4.1. Our Abel Noser sample covers 817 institutions trading through 318 brokers for the period of January 1999 to March 2011. We report descriptive statistics in Table 1, Panel B. We note, however, that the stocks traded by central institutions tend to be more frequently traded, as measured by the ADV, more likely to be listed on the NYSE, less volatile, and have a higher price compared to those traded by peripheral institutions.

## 5 The centrality premium

### 5.1 Evidence from the comprehensive sample of mutual funds

Despite extensive disclosure requirements, mutual funds are only required to disclose their holdings on a quarterly basis and their trading activities are generally unobservable. To examine how institutional brokerage networks affect mutual fund trading performance, we use the return gap as our measure of trading performance. The return gap is calculated as the difference between the reported fund return (gross of expenses) and the return on a hypothetical portfolio that invests in the previously disclosed fund holdings (Grinblatt and Titman, 1989; Kacperczyk et al., 2008):

 $$ Return~Gap_{i,t}=RET_{i,t}+EXP_{i,t}-HRET_{i,t} $$ 

where  $ RET_{i,t} $ is fund i's reported return (net of expenses) during month t,  $ EXP_{i,t} $, is the expense ratio for fund i reported prior to month t, and  $ HRET_{i,t} $ is the fund i's holdings return during month

t, which is defined as:

 $$ H R E T_{i,t}=\sum_{k}w_{i,k,t-1}R_{k,t}, $$ 

where  $ w_{i,k,t} $ is the fund i's portfolio weight on stock k at the end of month t and  $ R_{k,t} $ is the return on stock k during month t.

Mutual funds are first sorted into quintiles each month on the basis of network centrality measured at the end of the previous month. $ ^{11} $ The return gap is then averaged across all mutual funds belonging to each quintile. In Table 2, we report the time-series average of the return gap in each quintile and the difference between the extreme quintiles (Q5 – Q1). We find that central funds (Q5) earn the average return gap of 0 basis points per month, whereas peripheral funds (Q1) earn the average return gap of −6.9 basis points per month. The difference in average return gap between extreme quintiles of 6.9 basis points per month is both statistically significant and economically meaningful. We obtain similar results when we run panel regressions with institution fixed-effects (see Table A4 of the Internet Appendix). Our results suggest that network centrality positively predicts trading performance as measured by return gap, which we refer to as the centrality premium.

### 5.2 Further evidence from the Abel Noser sample of institutions

We argue that the centrality premium documented in Section 5.1 is driven by lower trading costs. The return gap, however, reflects not only trading costs such as bid-ask spread, price impact, and brokerage commissions, but also a host of other factors, such as interim (intra-quarter) trading performance (Kacperczyk et al., 2008; Puckett and Yan, 2011). To corroborate our trading costs hypothesis for the centrality premium, we exploit Abel Noser daily transactions data.

For each trade order, we compute the execution shortfall by comparing the execution price

of the trade with the stock price when the trading desk sends the order to the broker:

 $$  Execution Shortfall=\frac{Execution Price-Benchmark Price}{Benchmark Price}\times D $$ 

where  $ \underline{Execution} $ Price is the volume-weighted execution price of the trade,  $ \underline{Benchmark} $ Price is the stock price prevailing at the time the broker receives the order, and D denotes the trade direction, taking the value of 1 for a buy order and -1 for a sell order.

Following Anand et al. (2012), we estimate the institution’s trading costs by the monthly institution fixed-effects from a regression of execution shortfall on a host of trade-, stock-, and market-level control variables that can impact trading costs. We standardize all continuous variables so that the estimated fixed-effects are of the same magnitude as the execution shortfall. In Table A2 of the Internet Appendix, we report the average coefficients from the monthly institution fixed-effects regressions and t-statistics computed using the Fama and MacBeth (1973) procedure. Our results are qualitatively and quantitatively similar to those of Anand et al. (2012). Execution shortfall tends to decrease with average daily volume (ADV) and increase with order size. Seller-initiated orders tend to be more expensive than buyer-initiated orders. Trading stocks listed on the NYSE is less costly than trading stocks listed on NASDAQ.

To examine the relation between network centrality and trading costs, Abel Noser institutions are first sorted into quintiles each month on the basis of network centrality measured at the end of the previous month. The institution's trading costs are then averaged across all institutions belonging to each quintile. In Table 3, we report the time-series average of the trading costs in each quintile and the difference between the extreme quintiles (Q5 - Q1). We find that central institutions (Q5) incur an average trading costs of 9.9 basis points per trade, whereas peripheral institutions (Q1) incur the average trading costs of 14.2 basis points per trade. The difference in the average trading costs between extreme quintiles of -4.3 basis points per trade is both statistically and economically significant. We obtain similar results when we run panel regressions with institution fixed-effects (see Table A5 of the Internet Appendix). Our results suggest that

central institutions outperform peripheral institutions in terms of trade execution by incurring lower trading costs, consistent with our argument that the centrality premium measured by return gap is indeed driven by lower trading costs.

## 6 Inspecting the mechanism

### 6.1 The centrality premium when funds are forced to trade

The primary prediction that follows from our hypothesis is that the centrality premium should be more evident when funds' trading activities are largely driven by liquidity motives and brokers can discern the trading motive and/or funds can credibly signal this to their brokers. We use fund flows to identify such periods of liquidity-motivated trading. When a mutual fund is experiencing large outflows, the fund is forced to liquidate a sizable fraction of its holdings and its trading is likely to be largely uninformed (Coval and Stafford, 2007). We expect that funds facing such liquidity shocks will seek to persuade their relationship brokers that their orders are liquidity driven, rather than information motivated. Brokers, in turn, will strive to communicate the liquidity-driven nature of these trades to institutional clients on the other side of the trades.

Following the literature, we estimate monthly net flows for each fund i during month t as follows:

 $$ FLOW_{i,t}=\frac{TNA_{i,t}-TNA_{i,t-1}\times(1+R_{i,t})}{TNA_{i,t-1}} $$ 

where TNA is the total net asset of fund i during month t, and R is the return of fund i during month t.

We proceed by double-sorting funds on the basis of network centrality and fund flows. We sort mutual funds into quintiles each month on the basis of network centrality measured at the end of the previous month. Independently, we sort fund-months into quintiles on the basis of fund flows using breakpoints computed from the full sample. Our double-sort results are reported in Table 4. We find that during the months when funds experience large outflows, central funds (Q5) outperform peripheral funds (Q1) in terms of return gap by 6.9 basis points per month. During other months, central funds still tend to outperform peripheral funds, but the centrality premium substantially weakens both in terms of the economic magnitude and statistical significance. Our results suggest that central funds tend to outperform peripheral funds in terms of return gap,

especially when funds are forced to liquidate to accommodate large outflows, consistent with our liquidity provision hypothesis that institutional brokerage networks mitigate price impact of large liquidity-motivated trades.

### 6.2 The centrality premium and liquidity of stock holdings

Our liquidity provision hypothesis requires an active role on the part of brokers in searching for potential counterparties ('unexpressed liquidity') to facilitate liquidity provision. We expect

the role of brokers to be limited in the case of large-cap, liquid stocks that are actively traded on exchanges. On the other hand, the role of brokers in expanding the available liquidity pool is likely to be important for large trades involving illiquid and thinly traded stocks.

To test this prediction, we use double sorts on network centrality and (il)liquidity of stock holdings. First, we measure the illiquidity of each stock in each quarter using the Amihud (2002)'s illiquidity measure:

 $$ Illiq_{i,q}=\frac{1}{D_{i,q}}\sum_{t=1}^{D_{i,q}}\frac{\left|R_{i,t}\right|}{DVOL_{i,t}} $$ 

where $D_{i,q}$ is the number of days for which data are available for stock $i$ in quarter $q$, $R_{i,t}$ is the return on stock $i$ on day $t$, and $DVOL_{i,t}$ is the dollar volume on stock $i$ on day $t$. Then, we aggregate the stock-level illiquidity measure to the fund (portfolio) level by computing the value-weighted average of the illiquidity measures across all stocks held by each fund at the end of each quarter.

We sort mutual funds into two sub-samples (halves) each month on the basis of the illiquidity of the most recent end-of-quarter stock holdings. Within each holdings-illiquidity sub-sample, mutual funds are then sorted into quintiles on the basis of network centrality measured at the end of the previous month. Our double-sort results are reported in Table 6. Consistent with our expectations, the centrality premium is more pronounced among funds with illiquid stock holdings. Among funds with illiquid stock holdings, central funds (Q5) outperform peripheral funds (Q1) in terms of return gap by 5.9 basis points per month. This centrality premium reduces to 2.9 basis points per month among funds with more liquid stock holdings. Our double-sort results suggest that brokerage connections become more important for mutual funds that tend to hold less liquid stocks and where brokers can be beneficial in expanding the available liquidity pool to facilitate liquidity provision.

### 6.3 The centrality premium and order size

We extend the above holdings-level analysis using mutual funds data to a transactions-level analysis using Abel Noser data. The trading problem is especially difficult for orders that are large relative to the average daily volume (ADV) for a security (Anand et al., 2012). The granular nature of Abel Noser data allows us to more directly test our prediction that expanding the available liquidity pool should be more important for large trades of illiquid stocks that are less actively traded.

To test the prediction that brokerage connections will be particularly important for more difficult orders, we use double sorts on network centrality and order size (relative to the average daily volume computed from CRSP over the past 30 days). All trade orders are first sorted into two sub-samples each month on the basis of the order size scaled by ADV. The institution's trading costs are then measured by the monthly institution fixed-effects using the regression specification in Section 5.2 for each order-size sub-sample.

Within each order-size sub-sample, Abel Noser institutions are sorted into quintiles on the basis of network centrality at the end of the previous month. We report our double-sort results in Table 7. When placing large trade orders with the brokers, central institutions (Q5) incur significantly lower trading costs than do peripheral institutions (Q1). The difference in the trading costs of -4.8 basis points per trade is statistically significant and economically meaningful. This outperformance, however, substantially weakens and becomes statistically insignificant when institutions place small trade orders with the brokers. Our results suggest that brokerage connections are particularly important when institutions trade large orders relative to the average daily volume for which brokers likely need to expand the available liquidity pool to facilitate liquidity provision.

### 6.4 Trading skills vs. liquidity provision

A potential concern about the liquidity provision hypothesis is that there might exist some unobservable (to the econometrician) factors that are correlated with both network centrality and trading performance. In particular, Anand et al. (2012) show that trading costs are closely linked to the institution's trading desks' execution skills over and above selecting better brokers. In light of their paper, a potential concern is that if central institutions have superior trading desks, the centrality premium could be driven by the execution skills of trading desks, rather than the liquidity provision facilitated by brokers. To disentangle execution skills from the liquidity provision, we exploit Abel Noser data for an institution-broker level analysis, allowing us to measure the trading costs incurred by each institution trading through each broker separately.

Specifically, we measure the institution’s trading costs incurred through each broker separately by the monthly institution×broker fixed-effects from a regression similar to that in Section 5.2. Abel Noser institutions and brokers are independently double sorted into quintiles each month on the basis of network centrality of institutions and network centrality of brokers, respectively, measured at the end of the previous month. We report our double-sort results in Table 8. First, we note that within each broker quintile, central institutions (IQ5) outperform peripheral institutions (IQ1) in terms of trade execution. More interestingly, this outperformance is more pronounced when institutions trade through central brokers (BQ5) than when they trade through peripheral brokers (BQ1). Trading through central brokers (BQ5), central institutions (IQ5) incur 10.7 basis points lower trading costs compared to peripheral institutions (IQ1). This difference in trading costs reduces to 5.7 basis points per trade when trading through peripheral brokers (BQ1).

Looking across brokers within each institution quintile, we find that the outperformance of central brokers over peripheral brokers in trade execution is only prevalent for central institutions (IQ5). Central institutions (IQ5) incur trading costs of 11 basis points through central brokers (BQ5) while incurring trading costs of 15.1 basis points through peripheral brokers (BQ1). A difference in trading costs of -4.1 basis points per trade is both statistically and economically significant.

By contrast, the superior trade execution by central brokers relative to peripheral brokers is substantially weaker and becomes statistically insignificant for less central institutions (IQ1 – IQ4). For robustness checks, we estimate panel regressions with both institution and broker fixed-effects. Our results remain little changed (see Table A6 of the Internet Appendix).

Connections to other institutions (potential counterparties to the trade) through the overlapping brokerage relationships can help mitigate trading costs in equity markets. ## 7 Quasi-natural experiments

Having established that central brokers can deliver superior execution, especially for central institutions, we present further evidence on the role of brokers in facilitating liquidity provision. Given the persistence in trading relationships between institutions and their brokers (Goldstein et al., 2009), connected brokers may not be easily substituted and losing connection to a broker, especially a central one, could hurt an institution's ability to reach out to potential counterparties through connected brokers, thereby resulting in higher trading costs.

We follow Di Maggio et al. (2017) to exploit the collapse of Lehman Brothers in 2008 as an exogenous shock to the brokerage industry. $ ^{12} $ A quasi-natural experiment setting allows us to further address potential endogeneity concerns, as discussed in Section 6.4, and corroborate our hypothesis that the centrality premium is indeed driven by the liquidity provision facilitated by brokers, rather than the execution skills of trading desks.

## 8 Asset fire sales and network structure

We have thus far shown that brokers can facilitate liquidity provision when institutions facing liquidity shocks are forced to trade. Flow-induced selling can be especially costly for open-end mutual funds when there is significant overlap with the securities held by other funds also experiencing severe outflows. While brokerage connections are beneficial when an institution facing idiosyncratic liquidity shocks is forced to trade, it is unclear whether brokers can still facilitate liquidity provision when connected institutions (potential counterparties) also face liquidity shocks of their own and simultaneously demand liquidity to sell overlapping securities. In this section, we examine whether

network structure can mitigate or exacerbate the price impact of forced selling of the securities held in common by distressed funds. $ ^{14} $

Following Coval and Stafford (2007), we identify stocks where large trading volume is accounted for by mutual funds experiencing significant outflows as fire sale stocks. Specifically, we define the variable Pressure as follows:

Pressure $ _{k,t} $

 $$ \begin{aligned}=\frac{\sum_{i}\left(\max(0,\Delta H o l d i n g s_{i,k,t}|f l o w_{i,t}>P e r c e n t i l e(90\mathrm{th}))-\max(0,-\Delta H o l d i n g s_{i,k,t}|f l o w_{i,t}<P e r c e n t i l e(10\mathrm{th}))\right)}{AvgV o l u r e_{k,t-12:t-6}}.\end{aligned} $$ 

where i denotes mutual funds, k denotes stocks, and t denotes time (in month). Stocks with Pressure below the 10th percentile are considered fire sale stocks.

For each fire sale, we measure brokerage connections among distressed funds using network density of a sub-network of distressed funds. Network density is defined as a ratio of the number of actual connections between pairs of institutions to the number of all possible connections between all pairs of institutions. The higher the density, the more connections institutions have with each other through overlapping brokers. Then, we measure the price impact of fire sales using the cumulative abnormal return (CAR) over the fire sale quarter.

Fire sale stocks are first sorted into quintiles each quarter on the basis of network density measured at the beginning of the fire sale quarter. The CARs are then averaged across all fire sale stocks belonging to each quintile. In Table 10, we report the time-series average of the CARs in each quintile and the difference between the extreme quintiles (Q5 – Q1). We find that fire sales by distressed funds with higher network density (Q5) earn the average CARs of  $ -4.8\% $ per month, whereas fire sales by distressed funds with lowest network density (Q1) earn the average CARs of  $ -2.4\% $ per quarter. The difference in average CARs between extreme quintiles of  $ -2.4\% $ per quarter is both statistically significant at the 10% level and economically meaningful. Our results suggest that brokerage connections among distressed funds facing correlated liquidity shocks tend

to exacerbate the price impact of flow-induced forced trading.

## 9 Conclusion

Our paper provides evidence that institutional brokerage networks facilitate liquidity provision and mitigate the price impact of large non-information motivated trades. This is consistent with central brokers being connected to a wider set of institutional clients and more capable of discerning their clients' trading motives. Central brokers are also likely better positioned to expand the available liquidity pool by using information about their clients' latent trading interests. Thus, even though all institutions may have similar access to the available pool of expressed liquidity, central institutions, through connections to many central brokers, will be better positioned to tap into larger pools of 'unexpressed' liquidity.

In addition to standard data sources such as CRSP Mutual Fund and Thomson-Reuters Ownership, we rely heavily on two datasets. The first is a unique dataset on brokerage commissions using Form N-SAR semi-annual reports filed with the SEC. The second is Abel Noser institutional daily transactions dataset, which allows for detailed transaction-level analyses for institutional clients of Abel Noser. We use return gap and execution shortfall to measure trading performance. Our finding is that network centrality is associated with superior trading performance (a reduction in trading costs), which we refer to as the centrality premium. The economic magnitude of the centrality premium is meaningful with top quintile mutual funds/institutions outperforming bottom quintile counterparts by 6.9 basis points per month (4.3 basis points per trade) in terms of return gap (execution shortfall).

Using double sorts on network centrality and order size, we find that central institutions incur significantly lower trading costs than peripheral institutions, especially when placing large orders with the brokers. We also find that the outperformance of central institutions over peripheral

institutions in trade execution is greater when institutions trade through central brokers rather than through peripheral brokers. Central brokers can deliver superior trade execution compared to peripheral brokers, but this benefit accrues mainly to central institutions.

To address endogeneity concerns, we use the collapse of Lehman Brothers as an exogenous shock that significantly decreased the network centrality of institutions that were more connected to Lehman. Indicative of causality, we find that this decrease in network centrality is accompanied by worsened trading performance for mutual funds/institutions that were more connected to Lehman Brothers prior to its collapse. Overall, our results indicate that the structure of institutional brokerage networks is economically consequential. While brokerage connections tend to lower institutional trading costs on average, our results suggest that brokerage connections are more beneficial when mutual funds face idiosyncratic flow shocks. However, brokerage connections to other funds with similar holdings can exacerbate price impact when connected funds suffer correlated liquidity shocks.

We compute the return gap as the difference between the reported fund return (gross of expenses) and the return on a hypothetical portfolio that invests in the previously disclosed fund holdings (Grinblatt and Titman, 1989; Kacperczyk et al., 2008):

 $$ Return~Gap_{i,t}=RET_{i,t}+EXP_{i,t}-HRET_{i,t} $$ 

where  $ RET_{i,t} $, is the fund i's reported return (net of expenses) during month t,  $ EXP_{i,t} $, is the expense ratio for fund i reported prior to month t, and  $ HRET_{i,t} $ is the fund i's holdings return during month t, which is defined as:

 $$ HRE T_{i,t}=\sum_{k}w_{i,k,t-1}R_{k,t} $$ 

where  $ w_{i,k,t} $ is the fund i's portfolio weight on stock k at the end of month t and  $ R_{k,t} $ is the return on stock k during month t. We then compute the average return gap across all mutual funds belonging to each quintile and report the time-series average of the return gap in each quintile, along with the difference between the extreme quintiles (Q5-Q1). ### B Institutional brokerage networks

Using brokerage commission payments, we map trading networks of institutions and their brokers as affiliation networks represented by weighted bipartite graphs. In a graph, agents can be represented by nodes, and connections (ties) between agents are represented by edges. In a bipartite graph, nodes can be partitioned into two types, and nodes of one type can only be connected to nodes of a different type, not with ones of the same type. Like any graph, a bipartite graph can be represented by an adjacency matrix denoted as G, where rows index institutions and columns index brokers. Each element  $ g_{i,k} $ of G identifies a connection between institution i and broker k, measured by the commissions institution i paid to broker k, scaled by the norm of a vector of commissions institution i paid to all brokers.

To measure an institution’s connections to all the other institutions through their overlapping brokerage connections, we reduce the bipartite graph of institutions and brokers into a monopartite graph of institutions only by defining its adjacency matrix  $ A $ as  $ A = GG^T $. By construction, the strength of connections between any pair of institutions is simply measured as the cosine similarity between their brokerage commission vectors, i.e.,  $ a_{i,j} = g_i \cdot g_j = \frac{c_i \cdot c_j}{\|c_i\| \|c_j\|} $. Intuitively,  $ a_{i,j} $ measures the extent to which two institutions’ brokerage connections overlap.

Borrowing techniques from graph theory and social network literature, we use eigenvector centrality (Bonacich, 1972, 1987), to quantify the importance of an institution's position in the network. Eigenvector centrality is defined as the principal eigenvector of the adjacency matrix defining the network. That is,

 $$ \lambda v=Av $$ 

where A is the adjacency matrix of the graph,  $ \lambda $ is a constant (the eigenvalue), and v is the eigenvector.

The independent variables are defined as follows: Stock volatility is the absolute value of the daily stock return; Market volatility is the absolute value of the daily S&P 500 return; Buy dummy is equal to one for a buy order and zero for a sell order; Prev. day's return is the daily stock return on the previous trading day; log(Average daily volume) is the logarithm of the average daily volume (ADV) computed from CRSP over the past 30 days; NYSE stock dummy is equal to one for a NYSE-listed stock and zero otherwise; Price is the closing stock price on the previous trading day; Order size/ADV is the number of shares executed (scaled by the average daily volume (ADV) computed from CRSP over the past thirty days); Size quintile, Book-to-market quintile, and Momentum quintile are quintile assignments for each stock based on NYSE quintile breakpoints as of the previous June, following Daniel et al. (1997). All continuous variables are standardized. ##### Table A3: Relationship between return gap and execution shortfall

This table presents the results of the following linear regression model:

 $$ Return gap_{i,t}=\beta\times Execution shortfall_{i,t}+\gamma\times Covariates_{i,t-1}+\theta_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t} $$ 

where i indexes mutual funds and t indexes time (in months). The dependent variable, Return gap $ _{i,t} $, is the return gap of fund i in month t. Execution shortfall $ _{i,t} $ is the value-weighted average of execution shortfall incurred by mutual fund i during month t. Covariates $ _{i,t} $ include a host of fund-level control variables such as the logarithm of total net assets (TNA) (in million dollars), expense ratio, turnover ratio, and holdings characteristics, computed as the value-weighted averages of stock characteristics, including the logarithm of market capitalization (in million dollars), book-to-market ratio, and past 12-month return (excluding the last month) of the fund's previously disclosed stock holdings.  $ \theta_t $ denotes month fixed-effects and standard errors are clustered by the fund. Statistical significance at the 10%, 5%, and 1% level is indicated by *, **, and ***, respectively.
