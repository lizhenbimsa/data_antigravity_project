## ABSTRACT

Navigating the complex landscape of corporate climate disclosures and their real impacts is crucial for managing climate-related financial risks. However, current disclosures oftentimes suffer from imprecision, inaccuracy, and greenwashing. We introduce  $ CLIMATEBERT_{CTI} $, a deep learning algorithm, to identify climate-related cheap talk in MSCI World index firms' annual reports. We find that only targeted climate engagement is associated with less cheap talk. Voluntary climate disclosures are associated with more cheap talk. Moreover, cheap talk correlates with increased negative news coverage and higher emissions growth. Hence, cheap talk helps assess climate initiatives' effectiveness and anticipate reputation and transition risk exposure.

## 1. Introduction

In this era of profound economic transformation, assessing the impact of climate change, climate policies, and corporate climate ambition statements on firm value and portfolio strategy presents a significant challenge for investors (e.g., Deng et al., 2022). Financial stakeholders must not only comprehend a company's climate risk exposures but also discern the effectiveness of its communicated climate actions and commitments in mitigating forward-looking transition and physical risks (Bingler and Colesanti Senni, 2022) on the need for forward-looking climate risk analyses. As financial markets shift towards sustainable practices, investors and financial regulators must distinguish between cheap talk and authentic climate risk management commitments and strategies, enabling market participants to assess forward-looking climate risks accurately.

This paper addresses these concerns by introducing CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $, a natural language processing (NLP) methodology which builds on the neural network-based CLIMATEBERT model (Webersinke et al., 2022). $ ^{1} $ CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ enables researchers and stakeholders to discern between

genuine and unspecific climate commitments and to shed light on the quality of firms' disclosed climate-related pledges. This allows for subsequent large-scale analyses between firms' climate commitments and their alignment with actual actions and outcomes. Specifically, we utilize  $ CLIMATEBERT_{CTI} $ to develop a “cheap talk index” that quantifies the share of superficiality in companies' climate commitments. This index facilitates a systematic analysis of the relationship between cheap talk and voluntary climate initiatives, corporate climate performance, and media coverage of environmental incidents. Through this investigation, we aim to contribute to a more informed understanding of the dynamics between corporate communications, stakeholder perceptions, and the genuine implementation of climate commitments, ultimately unlocking climate-aligned business practices and enhancing market efficiency.

A growing body of literature emphasizes the importance of climate-related information, in the form of news or corporate disclosures, for actively managing climate-related financial risks and opportunities. $ ^{2} $ Research by, for instance, Engle et al. (2020), Monasterolo and De Angelis (2020), Hsu et al. (2023), Carbone et al. (2021), Bolton et al. (2022), suggests that financial markets have begun to incorporate firms' climate-related transition risks and climate commitments into pricing and are considering climate-hedging strategies. Yet, there are ongoing concerns regarding market inefficiencies, particularly in terms of accurately reflecting physical climate risks in risk premia, as indicated by studies such as Hong et al. (2019), Baldauf et al. (2020), Bakkensen and Barrage (2021), and Gostlow (2022). A major challenge lies in the current lack of standardization and precision in climate risk disclosures, leading to concerns that companies are not providing sufficiently granular and decision-useful information in their climate-related reporting. $ ^{3} $ In general, companies may have the incentive to use vague language in their climate disclosures to reduce the risk of being sued for potentially misleading information. At the same time, they face mounting pressure to take active climate action and transparently communicate their commitments to reduce litigation risks due to inaction or omitting materially relevant information, as discussed in Weber and Hoesli (2020), Hoesli (2021).

In recent years, numerous industry-led or voluntary market initiatives have emerged to promote corporate climate action and decision-useful disclosures. Amongst others, climate action as promoted by the Science-Based Targets initiative (SBTi), the disclosure recommendations of the Task Force on Climate-Related Financial Disclosures (TCFD), and the Climate Action 100+ (CA100+) engagement initiative are amongst the most prominent examples. However, research suggests that especially the SBTi and TCFD initiatives are of limited effectiveness in improving climate disclosures and do not trigger ambitious climate action where it is needed most (Bingler et al., 2022; Bolton and Kacperczyk, 2021c). Furthermore, the initiatives have faced criticism for allegedly facilitating greenwashing or not driving decisive climate action. Given these initiatives' paramount presence and market importance, we investigate their association with corporate climate action disclosures, using CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $'s cheap talk index as an indicator.

Recent studies have looked into how firms' involvement in corporate climate initiatives like the SBTi and the CDP affects their climate performance (Coen et al., 2023). Indeed, there is a growing interest in understanding how these initiatives can reduce emissions and how making formal commitments to climate action can strengthen corporate efforts in this area. Using a keyword-based approach, Coen et al. (2022) find that corporate climate talk does not consistently translate into climate action, with some commitments being genuine and others constituting 'greenwashing'. Their findings suggest that despite positive rhetoric, corporate climate strategies and commitments often amount to mere symbolism (see also, among others, Bowen and Aragon-Correa, 2014; Doda et al., 2016). Our approach using CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ enhances the literature by offering a more precise approach to distinguish between specific and superficial commitments and by assessing which relevant initiatives might be associated with more or less profound commitments, compared to superficial greenwashing wording (Cheap Talk). $ ^{5} $

The connection between specific reporting and actual actions is particularly strong through legally binding documents such as annual reports. Annual reports offer a combination of quantitative and qualitative information, which is crucial for understanding a firm's quantitative, backward-looking performance along with its more forward-looking strategic qualitative information, as demonstrated by Edmans et al. (2016). In light of this, we analyze 14,618 annual reports of the MSCI World index companies for the years 2010 to 2020.

In addition, we employ the cheap talk index as a proxy for the likelihood of companies turning climate reporting into tangible actions. Companies with specific climate commitments are more accountable to external stakeholders and are better positioned to understand and explicitly define their approach to sustainability, akin to the integrated reporting approach (Churet and Eccles, 2014). Therefore, we first examine the relationship of cheap talk with changes in greenhouse gas emissions. By examining this relationship, we aim to understand whether companies with lower levels of cheap talk in their climate disclosures are more likely to demonstrate a reduction in emissions over time.

We also explore the relationship of cheap talk with negative media coverage of environmental incidents. $ ^{6} $ Hence, we want to understand whether companies exhibiting higher levels of cheap talk regarding climate commitments are more prone to encountering negative environmental news coverage. In addition, we investigate whether such incidents are associated with increased cheap talk in the following annual reports, which would suggest that companies attempt to restore their reputation rhetorically. $ ^{7} $

Our analysis reveals four main insights: (1) Only engagement-driven climate initiatives effectively reduce cheap talk in corporate climate disclosures; (2) Companies that support voluntary disclosure frameworks exhibit higher levels of cheap talk; (3) Cheap talk in annual reports is associated with higher emissions growth; (4) Cheap talk is associated with more negative news coverage and controversies. ## 2. Research questions

Climate disclosures are of paramount importance to enable stakeholders to assess companies' climate actions and strategies. Fig. 1 provides an initial overview of the evolution of climate-related information in annual reports. It presents the changes in climate-related commitments identified by using our CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ algorithm within the annual reports of the MSCI World Index constituents. The left-hand panel of Fig. 1 illustrates the progression of firms' climate-related commitments from 2010 to 2020. A substantial increase in commitments can be observed, particularly following the Paris Agreement in December 2015, with a growth of nearly 25% from 2015 to 2020. However, the right-hand panel of Fig. 1 indicates that this growth differs across key sectors. The energy sector has experienced a decline in commitments, while utilities have recently increased, and the financial sector's commitments have skyrocketed by almost 160% since 2010.

Given the significant expansion of commitments within certain sectors, examining these pledges' credibility and specificity and subsequent actions is crucial. It is essential to determine if companies are only adopting a superficial climate or environmental leadership stance in response to heightened public awareness of climate change following the Paris Agreement or whether their disclosed strategies align with the need for ambitious climate action.

We focus on two sets of research questions. First, we want to explore which climate initiatives are associated with disclosing more precise corporate climate commitments. Joining a climate initiative is an effective way for companies to underpin the credibility of their climate strategy. For this reason, firms also publicly communicate and advertise joining initiatives. In this sense, we analyze how companies 'talk their walk.' Second, we ask whether cheap talk influences how they walk their talk. We want to understand whether a firm's cheap talk relates to climate activities like emission reductions or reputational risks. Hence, we want to understand whether cheap talk has some real effects.

### 2.1. How companies talk their walk

For a successful transition to a decarbonized economy, companies must take action and publish decision-relevant information about it. In addition, companies are required to mitigate climate risks to their business. To facilitate this call to action and reporting, numerous initiatives have been launched in recent years that policymakers and regulators expect to reinforce corporate efforts. It has been shown that broad ESG initiatives such as the PRI are insufficiently targeted to make a difference in climate-related commitments. $ ^{8} $ Therefore, we examine the role of climate initiatives in reporting companies' climate-related commitments and actions.

Our study examines the impact of three prominent climate initiatives on the disclosure practices of companies: the Science Based Targets Initiative (SBTi), the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD), and the Climate Action 100+ (CA100+). We focus specifically on these initiatives due to their prevalence in political discussions surrounding climate disclosure standards. The initiatives are regularly referred to as cornerstones in government-approved self-regulatory standards for the financial industry, such as in Switzerland and Japan. Moreover, each initiative serves a distinct economic channel, with TCFD acting as a form of collective signaling, SBTi providing verified credibility to climate targets, and CA100+ offering an engagement channel for investors to pressure companies to improve their climate efforts. We investigate how these different channels influence companies' provision of decision-useful information to the public. In particular, we analyze the initiatives' impact on the share of non-specific commitments, which we define as the companies' cheap talk.

Signaling and credibility-motivated disclosures of specific climate commitments aim to reduce information costs for investors, thereby reducing a general or sector-specific climate risk uncertainty premium (Bolton and Kacperczyk, 2021a; Chen et al., 2020). Publicly announcing the support of the TCFD, an industry-led initiative established in 2015 by the Financial Stability Board (FSB), could operate as a signaling mechanism. However, a critical aspect is the quality of disclosure. Literature on the role of the quality of disclosure and its impact on firm value and the cost of capital finds mixed empirical evidence. The TCFD's recommendations, released in 2017, help companies provide adequate climate-related disclosures. A commitment to implement the

disclosures can maximize value ex-ante if all shareholders interpret the TCFD commitment as a signal for climate risk awareness by the firm, and it improves the risk-sharing amongst shareholders. Committing to a widely accepted international industry-led framework such as the TCFD should underscore the firm's intention to explicitly mitigate climate-related financial risks to its business with specific climate mitigation and adaptation activities. This observation leads to our first hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1 (Collective Signaling). A firm’s public support for the TCFD recommendations is negatively associated with cheap talk.

While Matsumura et al. (2014) show that voluntary disclosures might mitigate the adverse valuation effects of high carbon emissions, additional verification efforts are relevant to improve the credibility of claims. This is intended to signal against greenwashing and mitigate considerable information asymmetry concerns between the investor and the investee. Firms might be better off working towards third-party verification of their climate commitments to differentiate themselves from firms that apply managerial “cheap talk” (Almazan et al., 2008; Bingler et al., 2022). One initiative that works through the credibility channel is the SBTi, which was launched in 2015 to assist companies in setting targets in line with climate science. $ ^{9} $ Moreover, the SBTi provides independent assessment and validation of targets. This gives rise to our second hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2 (External Credibility). A firm's public announcement to set a third-party verified science-based target (SBTi) is negatively associated with cheap talk.

The third channel is the ownership and engagement channel. The impact of institutional ownership and targeted engagement strategies on corporate sustainability, environmental performance, and transparency has been researched from various perspectives. $ ^{10} $ The evidence on the magnitudes of the effects of institutional ownership is mixed. Nevertheless, the literature generally agrees on the following findings: First, institutional ownership is associated with higher ESG transparency in most cases. Second, targeted engagement strategies concentrate shareholder attention, and active ownership enhances corporate sustainability performance and transparency. These effects seem to be more pronounced for material downside sustainability risks. Focusing specifically on climate-related disclosures, Ilhan et al. (2020), Krueger et al. (2020) and Flammer et al. (2021) find that institutional investors demand and value climate-related disclosures and that engagement processes for climate risk strategies and disclosures could increase transparency, especially as long as climate risk disclosure is not mandatory for all firms.

A well-known initiative for the active engagement channel is the CA100+ initiative, an institutional investor-led initiative founded in 2017. $ ^{[11]} $ Since then, it has grown into the largest investor engagement initiative on climate change. In signing up for CA100+, investors commit to engaging with at least one of the focus companies. They seek commitments along three dimensions: (1) Implement a strong governance framework on climate change; (2) Take action to reduce greenhouse gas emissions across the value chain; (3) Provide enhanced corporate disclosure. We exploit the fact that being a CA100+ focus company is like an interaction variable between institutional ownership and climate-related engagement. Thus, we formulate our third hypothesis as follows.

Hypothesis 3 (Ownership and Active Engagement). Being part of the CA100+ active ownership and engagement focus companies is negatively associated with cheap talk.

### 2.2. How do companies walk their (cheap) talk?

So far, our cheap talk index has focused on quantifying the extent of nonspecific commitments or verbal communication related to corporate climate action. Hence, an interesting next step would be to examine whether climate initiatives help reduce a company's cheap talk and whether companies that engage in a lot of cheap talk take fewer climate actions relative to their peers. Many companies may promise to address climate change to improve their public image but often fail to take concrete action to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. For this reason, we want to examine the relationship between cheap talk and a company's actual efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. In doing so, we want to determine whether there is a relationship between the frequency of cheap talk and the extent of a company's actual commitment to reducing its environmental impact with the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4 (Cheap Talk and Emission Reduction). A high level of cheap talk in climate commitments indicates that companies are not genuinely committed to significantly reducing greenhouse gas emissions.

Our subsequent hypothesis is that companies that experience environmental controversies are more likely to engage in cheap talk in their subsequent climate disclosures, potentially as a way to restore their reputation and legitimacy. This hypothesis is grounded in the notion that companies use non-financial information to signal their social responsibility and commitment to sustainability (Deegan, 2002). Hence, it is reasonable to assume that companies that have experienced such incidents are more likely to engage in cheap talk in subsequent climate disclosures. Indeed, companies often communicate conspicuously about their social responsibility when defending their legitimacy or responding to legitimacy problems. The extent of communication is usually proportional to the extent of the legitimacy problem. $ ^{12} $ Thus, we expect to find a positive relationship between environmental controversies and cheap talk in climate disclosures.

Hypothesis 5 (Cheap Talk and Restoring Reputation). Heightened controversial news coverage concerning environmental incidents prompts an increase in cheap talk about a company's climate commitments.

Our final hypothesis posits that companies engaging in cheap talk within their climate commitments are more prone to encountering future incidents and controversies related to their climate strategies. Cheap talk in climate commitments may signify inadequate management and inconsistent climate strategies, potentially elevating the likelihood of future disputes. Since disclosing detailed and accurate information on commitments could incur net costs for firms, resorting to cheap talk or boilerplate disclosure serves as a means to mitigate these expenses. In contrast, firms providing precise and comprehensive information about their climate commitments likely possess more effective internal climate management strategies. This can contribute to enhanced performance and a reduced risk of incidents and controversies. Consequently, the degree to which a firm engages in cheap talk could serve as an indicator for predicting future news coverage concerning its climate strategies.

Hypothesis 6 (Cheap Talk and Reputation Risk). A high level of cheap talk in climate commitments leads to more controversial news coverage.

Given our Hypotheses 1 to 6, we can now scrutinize the effectiveness of various climate initiatives in curbing cheap talk and test potential links between a company's cheap talk levels and their actual climate mitigation endeavors, as well as any subsequent environmental incidents or controversies. First, however, we need to ask ourselves a pivotal question: How do we precisely measure cheap talk in the realm of corporate climate disclosures? The challenge lies in devising an index that accurately captures the degree of vagueness, ambiguity, and lack of specificity in a company's climate pledges.

## 3. Measuring firm-level climate cheap talk

Given the complexity of designing a firm-level measure of climate-related cheap talk, we build CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ by fine-tuning the CLIMATEBERT language model (Webersinke et al., 2022) on each of the following downstream tasks. More details about the training and training data of CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ can be found in Appendix D and F.

Climate vs non-climate. The first downstream task assesses if a paragraph is climate-relevant or non-climate-relevant. This step allows us to focus only on interesting paragraphs and discard irrelevant ones.

Sentiment analysis. Our sentiment analysis considers both the content and tone of the paragraph. A paragraph is labeled as an opportunity if it mainly discusses business opportunities or the positive impacts of mitigating or adapting to climate change. It is considered neutral if it is about facts without putting them into a positive or negative perspective. Lastly, it is labeled a risk if it mainly talks about business risks or the negative impacts of climate change.

Commitments and actions. An accurate assessment of whether a company is actively committed to climate change mitigation and adaptation must understand whether it commits to climate action or whether its climate communication focuses on generalized descriptions of climate change. Therefore, we extend our analysis of annual reports by identifying the companies' climate-related commitments and actions. To this end, we classify climate-related corporate mitigation and adaptation commitments and actions. This task reveals whether a company has undertaken climate action or is committed to climate mitigation and adaptation. A paragraph gets labeled as commitment-yes if it reports that the company undertook activities in this regard, if it reports that it will likely do so, or if the company sets targets in this paragraph. Conversely, a paragraph is labeled as commitment-no if it does not discuss corporate or firm-level climate activities.

Specificity. Corporate statements about climate action can still be formulated in boilerplate terms. Therefore, we additionally analyze the specificity of commitments made, i.e., we assess how specific the language of a paragraph is. The motivation for this task is to separate factual and specific content from vaguely formulated climate disclosures. We define specificity in line with the definition of materiality by the (International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), 2018). Information is specific if omitting, misstating, or obscuring, and if it could reasonably be expected to influence the decisions that the primary users of climate-related corporate disclosures make based on those disclosures, which provide climate-related information about a specific reporting entity. Hence, a paragraph gets labeled as specific if a paragraph contains detailed performance information, details of actions, or tangible and verifiable targets. It is labeled as non-specific if this is not the case. We deliberately do not use the terms material and non-material, as these already imply an interpretation of the impact. We want to leave such an interpretation of the information to the user.

## 4. Data and variable constructions

We now move to the data and explain the rationale for using annual reports. Then, we describe the construction of the variables and the selection of the controls used in the regression analysis.

### 4.1. Annual reports

We retrieved 14,618 annual reports from Refinitiv Workspace and the company websites of the 1543 companies included in the MSCI World Index as of January 2021. This allows us to analyze a global sample of companies that account for a significant share of worldwide carbon emissions and are thus of particular interest concerning specific or non-specific commitments and actions. $ ^{13} $ For our analysis, we consider the period from 2010 to 2020. This period allows us to investigate the impact of important climate-related developments and reporting milestones, such as the 2015 Paris Agreement and the launch of the SBTi, TCFD, and CA100+ initiatives.

We focus on annual reports as a text source instead of sustainability reports or earning calls for the following reasons: First, many initiatives, such as the TCFD, encourage their supporters and signatories to publish climate-related information through this channel. Second, the annual report is the outlet where stakeholders expect to find financially relevant corporate disclosures and where companies are legally required to publish climate-related information in the near future. $ ^{14} $ Third, annual reports' legally binding character provides stronger incentives than corporate sustainability reports to disclose accurately. $ ^{15} $ Lastly, there is ample evidence that the disclosures are indeed valuable and informative (e.g., Campbell et al., 2014; Gaulin, 2017).

### 4.2. Cheap talk index (CTI)

To assess corporate climate commitments and actions, we use CLI-MATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ and construct a variable from annual reports that we call the cheap talk index (CTI). To obtain a firm-specific CTI, we first sort out all non-climate-related paragraphs in a given firm's annual report. We then infer the labels for the commitment and specificity task for all climate-related paragraphs. Finally, we aggregate the results to firm-year observations using the following equation:

 $$ CTI_{i,t}=\frac{COMMIT\cap NONSPEC_{i,t}}{COMMIT_{i,t}}, $$ 

where  $ CTI_{i,t} $ is the Cheap Talk Index of the company i for the year t and  $ COMMIT_{i,t} $ is the number of climate-related paragraphs classified as commitment and action. Lastly, by  $ COMMIT \cap NONSPEC_{i,t} $, we denote the number of climate-related paragraphs that are classified as commitment and non-specific. Therefore, a company's cheap talk index is the proportion of non-specific climate-related commitments and actions to all climate-related commitments and actions. Below, we

give an example of such a non-specific commitment.

Key issues for our company include reducing our use of electricity and natural gas, reducing waste and increasing recycling at our facilities, analyzing and reducing our water consumption, reducing our existing volatile organic compounds, and further improving safety performance in our facilities.

Although the company appears to make a commitment in this paragraph, users of this information have no way of assessing its scope and impact. The targeted reduction could be 0.5% or maybe 50%. Hence, we classify it as non-specific. In contrast, the following paragraph provides an example of a specific commitment.

Volkswagen Financial Services and Naturschutzbund Deutschland e.V. (NABU) are expanding their successful collaboration in the field of nature conservation and climate action. To this end, the provider of financial and mobility services in the Volkswagen Group is providing Euro 450 thousand for the nature conservation organization to restore the Aller river in the district of Verden between Hülsen and the mouth of the Weser River. The aim is to create more natural conditions for the riverbed and floodplain to increase biodiversity and also to improve flood protection. Along with moorland protection, the restoration of watercourses is a further focus of the financial services provider's involvement.

Here, the company not only commits to taking action but also provides information that allows users to assess the scope and impact of the action. For example, the amount of money given allows the reader to contextualize this information. A company that makes many specific climate-related commitments and consequently achieves a low cheap talk index increases transparency and supports stakeholders in internalizing climate-related information. We remark that, for our paper, we focus on the specificity of the content — we do not assess whether the commitments and actions are sufficient or aligned with the national and international climate mitigation and adaptation targets.

To verify that our algorithm detects cheap talk in climate commitments and actions, we conduct another evaluation in addition to our evaluation on unseen test data in Appendix G. For this purpose, we first draw a random sample of 50 paragraphs of specific and 50 paragraphs of non-specific climate commitments and actions from our MSCI World constituents sample. We then annotated these paragraphs independently, which resulted in four labels for each paragraph. We then merged our individual labels into a single label by majority voting (i.e., the label with at least three votes will be the final label) and discussed conflicts for ambiguous cases (i.e., there is no label with at least three votes). Finally, we compared our results to the classes predicted by our algorithm. We achieved a strong agreement of 72%, similar to our evaluation results on our test set, demonstrating that our models can confidently apply the learned patterns to unseen data. Although misclassifications may occur at the individual paragraph level, these do not affect our overall results significantly, given the large sample size.

### 4.3. Data on climate initiatives

We retrieve the company lists from the initiatives' websites to determine the commitment, signature, or membership of the SBTi, TCFD, or CA100+ focus companies. $ ^{16} $ For the TCFD supporters and the SBTi committers we also consider the year of joining. As of December 2021, more than 2000 businesses worldwide were committed to submitting a science-based target. Joining the TCFD and the SBTi are company-level decisions. In contrast, being part of the CA100+ companies is a decision based on external assessment. At the time of writing this paper, 157 focus companies have been selected by CA100+ for engagement, accounting for up to 80 percent of corporate industrial greenhouse gas emissions worldwide.

### 4.4. Emission data

For the choice of greenhouse gas emissions data, there are, in principle, many different providers we could choose from. In making our choice, we rely on the European Central Bank's (ECB) assessment of Urgentem (Alogoskoufis et al., 2021). Urgentem's unique dataset covers granular Scope 3 emissions, which are difficult to measure and rarely reported but represent over 85% of the world's overall footprint. Urgentem uses a statistically robust inference model to estimate Scope 3 emissions if a corporation fails to report this data. The dataset has a long time series of companies' emissions with consistency across all relevant data points and a high quality of data due to sophisticated data-cleaning and validation processes. Urgentem estimates the emissions of any listed or unlisted company based on that firm's revenue and sector of activity using its inference model. Moreover, Urgentem provides additional information on whether the emissions are reported or estimated. Given that the European Central Bank relies on Urgentem's data for economy-wide climate stress tests and applied the data in a paper that estimates corporate climate commitments and market perception of firms' credit risks (Carbone et al., 2021), we deemed it appropriate to use this provider for our analysis of the MSCI world constituents.

### 4.5. Reputation risk

To measure reputational risk, we use the RepRisk database. This database collects news from a wide range of media sources, including traditional print and online sources, blogs, social media (including Twitter), and other Internet channels — 80,000 sources in total. The news is collected in various languages and has a global reach, which is important given that our firm universe consists of the MSCI World Index constituents. The three ESG dimensions are used to define the impact of the news on reputational risk and are divided into 28 categories according to a taxonomy created by RepRisk. $ ^{17} $ The RepRisk approach offers the major advantage of collecting data from sources beyond English-language sources. This has the advantage that researchers can identify reputational risk at the early stages when information is first published in local-language media before being disseminated internationally. However, RepRisk is not concerned with assessing the credibility of claims, as it believes that the public's perception of media reports is critical to reputational risk.

Since we are interested only in incidents that relate to the environment, we construct a controversy index by first checking whether an incident has occurred in at least one of the environmental categories. Then, we weigh this incident by multiplying it by RepRisk's severity, reach, and novelty score. $ ^{18} $ By multiplying the binary variable of an

incident with severity, novelty, and reach, we can capture how critical the incident is for the firm's reputation. Given its global coverage, the RepRisk database is an ideal source for analyzing the reputational risks for our sample of MSCI World companies.

### 4.6. Control variables

Our analysis controls for a range of potentially important factors that, if left uncontrolled, could confound the inferences. We control for these by including a battery of firm-specific and climate-related variables.

#### 4.6.1. Sentiment score

Marquis et al. (2016) demonstrate that the pressure for environmental disclosures might lead to selective disclosures of positive or generalized information. Hence, with financial markets becoming more sensitive to firm-specific climate-risk issues, firms have considerable incentives to frame their climate risks positively and focus on climate change-related opportunities. Moreover, it might be more convenient for companies to report positively and focus on opportunities arising from climate change, especially when it comes to commitments and actions. Indeed, Hail et al. (2021) find that managers talk in earnings calls more positively and optimistically about climate change issues than about financial topics. Therefore, our analysis includes a score that captures the firm's sentiment toward climate change.

For measuring sentiment, we apply the trained sentiment model based on CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $ to all climate-related paragraphs in the annual reports. See appendix C.2 for more details on how the sentiment analysis was computed. We focus on sentiment related to the business outlook, i.e., the amount of information about downside risks and business opportunities related to physical and transition climate-related developments. We aggregate the results to the  $ OppRisk_{i,t} $ variable by dividing the number of paragraphs classified as opportunity  $ OPP_{i,t} $ by the number of paragraphs classified as risk  $ RISK_{i,t} $ for each company i and year t:

 $$ O p p R i s k_{i,t}=\log\left(\frac{O P P_{i,t}+1}{R I S K_{i,t}+1}\right). $$ 

Since it could be the case that reports do not include any climate-related paragraph with risk sentiment, we add 1 to both the numerator and denominator. Finally, we log-transform the data to reduce the skewness of its distribution.

## 5. Results

We start the discussion of the empirical results by first providing a descriptive analysis of our data.

### 5.1. Descriptive analysis

To provide an initial overview of our specificity measure for climate-related commitments, Fig. 3 displays the trend of the cheap talk index over time. The graph indicates a steady increase in the index from 2010 to 2020, with an overall rise of nearly 80% for all companies in the MSCI World Index. Further examination of the cheap talk index by sector reveals that the three sectors selected for the climate-related commitments plot in Fig. 1 exhibit a similar trend. For all sectors, the CTI increases significantly. Notably, while the energy sector shows a slight decrease in climate-related commitments in Fig. 1, its cheap talk index experiences a substantial increase of over 80%. Similarly, the utility sector sees its cheap talk index rise by almost 100%, while the financial sector experiences an increase of more than 60% (see Fig. 2).

### 5.2. How do companies talk the walk?

Our first three hypotheses in Section 2 are concerned with whether the three climate initiatives serving three different economic channels are associated with how corporations disclose their climate-relevant commitments. Hence, we test Hypotheses 1 to 3 by estimating the following panel model with country fixed effects and interaction of year and sector fixed effects:

 $$ \begin{aligned}CTI_{i,t}&=\alpha+\beta_{T}TCFD_{i,t}+\beta_{S}SBT_{i,t}+\beta_{C}ClimAct100_{i,t}+\beta_{OR}OppRisk_{i,t}\\&+\beta_{GHG}GHG_{i,t}+\beta_{M}Material_{i}+\beta_{X}X_{i,t}+\eta_{i}+\delta_{i}\times\nu_{t}+\epsilon_{i,t},\end{aligned} $$ 

where the dependent variable  $ CTI_{i,t} $ is the Cheap Talk Index of the company i for the year t, and  $ TCFD_{i,t} $,  $ SBT_{i,t} $ and  $ ClimAcr100_{i,t} $ are dummy variables indicating if a company is committed or signatory to the respective initiative. By  $ OppRisk_{i,t} $ we denote the climate reporting sentiment and by  $ GHG_{i,t} $ and  $ Material_{i} $, the estimated GHG Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions and the SASB materiality dummy, respectively.  $ X_{i,t} $ is a vector of control variables. To control for country characteristics such as reporting regimes, we include country fixed effects  $ \eta_{i} $. Following Sautner et al. (2022),  $ \delta_{i} \times \nu_{t} $ includes the interaction of sector and year fixed effects. All standard errors are double-clustered at the firm and year levels to account for cross-sectional and serial correlation in the error terms.

We perform multiple robustness checks to address potential concerns. For instance, we replace country fixed effects in Eq. (3) with a mandatory climate-related disclosures dummy  $ Mandatory_{i,t} $ and a rating of climate policies  $ ClimatePolicyGrade_{i,t} $ to account for country-specific climate policy characteristics. $ ^{20} $ In doing so, we precisely control climate reporting requirements and policies as an alternative to country-fixed effects, which absorb general time-constant country characteristics. We also re-estimate our model, lagging the variables of interest by one year. In this way, we can address concerns about the direction of the effects. In our regression analysis, we also include a lagged version of Eq. (3), with the lagged variables  $ TCFD_{i,t}^{lag1} $,  $ SBT_{i,t}^{lag1} $, and  $ ClimAct100_{i,t}^{lag1} $.

Table 3 reports the results. We first estimate the main model (I) without additional controls. In addition, we specify three alternative models: The main model with additional control variables (II), a model with control variables and lagged variables of interest to assess a meaningful direction of effect, and a model accounting for mandatory climate-related disclosure requirements and climate policy stringency at the country level (IV). Lastly, we also include a lagged version of the mandatory model (V).

We find that our Hypothesis 1, the signaling channel through the TCFD, gets clearly rejected. Indeed, we find a positive relationship with the CTI, significant at the 5% level. Hence, publicly supporting the TCFD is significantly associated with increased cheap talk compared to the baseline of not supporting the TCFD. This result suggests that signaling by supporting the TCFD is not associated with increased tangible and specific climate-related disclosures on commitments and actions. This finding does not change when we consider the lagged TCFD support. This suggests that TCFD endorsement cannot be considered as a valuable indicator of corporate climate action. Our findings suggest that it is rather a simple way for companies to achieve a marketing effect without addressing real climate action in a legally binding disclosure document, such as in their annual report.

We also do not find a statistically significant association between setting a science-based target and lower cheap talk (Hypothesis 2). This result remains insignificant when we re-estimate our models using only validated targets. Companies may require time to develop and disclose meaningful climate-related commitments and actions after expressing commitment to set a science-based target. Hence, we incorporate a lagged model in column (III) of Table 3 to account for this delay. Nevertheless, the lagged SBTi-support remains insignificant.

In contrast, Hypothesis 3 is strongly confirmed by our analysis. Active institutional ownership with targeted engagement strategies through the CA100+ initiative exhibits a statistically significant negative relationship with cheap talk. This finding supports the notion that the channel proposed by Dyck et al. (2019) and Dimson et al. (2015) is effective for investees' specific reporting on climate commitments and action. Furthermore, our results for the CA100+ initiative remain robust when accounting for a lag.

All these results and findings remain robust when we control for mandatory climate disclosure regimes in specific countries and countries' climate policy performance, as seen from Columns (IV) and (V). Policymakers should thus promote initiatives that safeguard and enhance investors' rights while motivating investors to actively engage with their investee companies on climate-related matters. These findings and interpretations are also in line with the results of Hoepner and Yu (2017), Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021c), Bingler et al. (2022).

The implications of our findings underscore the critical role of transparent and comprehensive emissions data. By promoting greater transparency and expanding the scope of emissions reporting, stakeholders can better assess the credibility of companies' climate commitments and determine whether they are truly addressing the pressing problem of climate change or merely paying lip service. Our results also suggest that cheap talk can have tangible consequences, as shown by the association with emissions growth. When companies engage in cheap talk, they make superficial or insincere climate commitments, often to create a positive public image without taking real action to reduce their carbon footprint. This disingenuous behavior can have real environmental impacts, leading to continued or even increased emissions. Furthermore, these superficial commitments can mislead investors and stakeholders into believing that the company is actively addressing climate change when it is merely maintaining the status quo. Such misleading activities worsen future transition risks and impede market participants from correctly pricing these future risks.

This suggests that companies engage in high levels of cheap talk regarding climate commitments when they are exposed to negative environmental incidents. Indeed, companies may use cheap talk as a strategic tool to address or deflect unfavorable media coverage of their environmental practices. In this context, cheap talk serves as a mechanism for managing public perception and maintaining a positive image amidst criticism, as documented in prior research (Brown and Deegan, 1998; Bowen and Aragon-Correa, 2014, e.g.,).

Our final hypothesis explores the link between cheap talk and reputation risk, proposing that a high degree of cheap talk in climate commitments could be associated with more controversial news stories. Hence, our analysis investigates the possible predictive power of prior cheap talk in determining the subsequent year's incidents. Applying the controversy index from RepRisk, we test this hypothesis using the following panel model with country-fixed effects and interaction of year and sector-fixed effects:

 $$ \begin{aligned}Controv_{i,t}&=\alpha+\beta_{CTI}CTI_{i,t-1}+\beta_{OR}OppRisk_{i,t-1}+\beta_{GHG}GHG_{i,t}\\&+\beta_{M} Material_{i}\\&+\beta_{X} X_{i,t}+\eta_{i}+\delta_{i}\times\nu_{t}+\epsilon_{i,t}.\\ \end{aligned} $$ 

Table 7 reveals a highly statistically significant positive relationship, particularly during the most recent period (Columns III and IV) and when accounting for mandatory reporting. This observation implies that the regulatory environment pertaining to mandatory disclosures might have a considerable influence on the interplay between cheap talk and future incidents. Overall, our results support the notion that the quality of a company's environmental commitments may indicate its susceptibility to negative events and, hence, may also reflect the quality of the management of environmental issues.

Before concluding, we remark that we also regressed the return on assets (ROA) on the CTI. Our analysis reveals no significant differences in ROA or stock returns between firms with high and low CTI, suggesting that financial markets may not effectively penalize firms that engage in "cheap talk" regarding their climate actions. This non-result indicates that current market mechanisms might be insufficiently attuned to the nuances of climate disclosures, possibly due to a gap in investor literacy regarding the interpretation and implications of climate commitments and their actual environmental impact. The tolerance for high CTI without direct financial repercussions may signal that market participants underestimate the long-term risks associated with poor climate governance. Given this apparent market indifference, there is a compelling need for stronger regulatory frameworks. Policymakers should consider implementing more stringent disclosure standards that require clear, quantifiable, and auditable climate action statements, and enhance legal liability for misleading or inaccurate climate disclosures. By reinforcing the frameworks governing environmental disclosures, regulators can help ensure that such information is informative and instrumental in driving genuine climate action, thus safeguarding both investor interests and broader environmental objectives.

## 6. Conclusion

Our work contributes to the literature on sustainable finance by providing a comprehensive analysis of cheap talk in corporate climate commitments. Utilizing  $ ClimATEBERT_{CTI} $, our innovative deep learning algorithm, we carefully analyzed the specificity of climate-related commitments across 14,618 annual reports from MSCI World index firms spanning 2010 to 2020. Our exploration into how these firms engage with key climate initiatives – specifically the TCFD, the SBTi, and the CA100+ – reveals exposing insights into the credibility of their climate commitments. We found that genuine engagement with initiatives like CA100+ is linked to a reduction in cheap talk, signifying more authentic climate commitments. Conversely, mere participation in voluntary disclosures and target-setting initiatives does not necessarily correlate with a decrease in cheap talk.

Furthermore, we demonstrate that companies with higher levels of cheap talk are associated with higher negative environmental news coverage and with higher emissions growth. These relationships suggest that companies with a high level of cheap talk prioritize maintaining a positive public perception over making meaningful changes to their business practices. Consequently, this can impede progress toward global emissions reduction targets, as companies that engage in cheap talk fail to take the necessary steps to mitigate their climate impacts and manage their climate risks.

By quantifying the prevalence of non-specific climate commitments, our findings indirectly highlight the importance of specificity and actionability in corporate climate strategies. A non-CTI index, the inverse of the CTI score, would indicate firms' dedication to specific, measurable climate actions. Given its inherent reflection of CTI results, the underlying message is clear: specificity in climate commitments is crucial. Specific commitments are implied to be more genuine and impactful, contrasting sharply with the superficial nature of cheap talk.

Our insights have important implications for investors, financial supervisors, and policymakers. By using CLIMATEBERT $ _{CTI} $'s cheap talk index as an indicator, stakeholders can better assess the credibility of climate-related commitments and actions reported in annual reports. Additionally, our results emphasize the need for further development and refinement of climate initiatives to ensure they effectively drive tangible climate actions and transparent, high-quality disclosures. Financial supervisors and policymakers should consider mandating standardized disclosure and climate target-setting requirements to replace the voluntary approaches. As climate change continues to pose significant risks to financial markets, the role of accurate and reliable corporate climate disclosures and ambitious climate action will only grow in importance.
