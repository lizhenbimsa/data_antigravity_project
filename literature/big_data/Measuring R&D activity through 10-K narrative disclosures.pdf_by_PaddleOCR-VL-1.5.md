## ABSTRACT

This study develops a textual R&D measure based on narrative disclosures in firms' 10-K filings. Using a tailored lexicon and a core-contextual word-pairing approach, our measure captures qualitative narratives of firms' R&D activities. We validate this measure using discriminant, construct, reliability, predictive, and external validity tests. The results show that the measure captures latent R&D activity and significantly improves the prediction of important outcomes, including patents, citations, and firm valuation, particularly for firms with missing or zero reported R&D data. We find that narrative R&D is positively associated with growth opportunities, suggesting a mechanism through which it influences firm valuation. Further, positive changes in narrative R&D are not associated with the R&D puzzle, suggesting that the measure facilitates more immediate market integration of innovation-related information than traditional metrics. The textual R&D measure provides a new perspective on the drivers of future innovation output and firm performance, shedding light on key factors that shape long-term competitiveness. Importantly, the textual measure helps to mitigate the limitations of traditional R&D metrics, particularly in cases where firms under-report R&D expenditures or engage in non-patentable forms of innovation.

## 1. Introduction

Firms' R&D activity plays a pivotal role in shaping their competitive advantage, market positioning, and long-term growth prospects (Hsu, 2009; García-Manjón and Romero-Merino, 2012). $ ^{1} $ Traditionally, investors have heavily relied on R&D expenditure as a financial metric to evaluate a firm's commitment to innovation. Practitioners, policymakers, and academics also have mostly relied on input measures of R&D expenditures or output metrics based on patents to understand the nature and effectiveness of firms' R&D activity (Leuz and Wysocki, 2016; Roychowdhury et al., 2019). However, a possible issue with these measures is that they do not capture the entire extent of a firm's R&D activity. Not all firms that engage in R&D activity choose to explicitly disclose their R&D expenditure, possibly either for confidentiality and competitive reasons or due to difficulties in accurately measuring it (Bellstam et al., 2021). $ ^{2} $ Prior studies show that R&D expenditures alone offer an incomplete picture of the firm's R&D activities, as over 10% of the firms with missing R&D expenditure display substantial evidence that they engage in R&D activity and innovation (Koh and Reeb, 2015). Also, other proxies for R&D, such as patents and citations, provide valuable but incomplete insights (Reeb and Zhao, 2020), as they only reflect patentable innovations and overlook non-patentable

advancements such as process improvements, software development, and early-stage research.

Our study seeks to address this critical gap by introducing a novel textual R&D measure through narrative disclosures in 10-K filings. This measure is computed using a tailored lexicon, introduced in this study, and a core-contextual word-pairing approach, with the objective of systematically analyzing linguistic patterns in 10-K filings to extract R&D-related content. Unlike traditional indicators, our R&D measure captures the qualitative aspects of R&D, including the strategic direction and future innovation potential. By analyzing the language used in 10-K filings, we identify key R&D-related terms and their contextual usage to provide a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of a firm's R&D activities.

Prior literature suggests that neither the reported R&D expenditure (Koh and Reeb, 2015) nor the patent filings capture the entire scope of R&D activity (Manso et al., 2021). Contrarily, narrative R&D disclosures provide a unique perspective into the intellectual property, technological advancements, and capabilities developed through R&D, giving investors a clearer picture of the firm's competitive positioning (Merkley, 2014; Andreou et al., 2025).³ In addition, these disclosures shed light on the type of research conducted, the target markets involved, and the potential disruptions the firm is considering or facing (Hoberg and Phillips, 2010; Andreou et al., 2020; Li and Lin, 2024). In this vein, our empirical investigation — covering the period from 1995 to 2022 and comprising 11,386 firms with 97,691 firm-year observations — suggests that our textual measure uncovers latent R&D activity in the narrative disclosures of 10-K filings and offers a complementary perspective that enhances our understanding of a firm's innovation efforts.

The first part of this paper focuses on methodological contributions and aims to test the effectiveness of the lexicon in capturing R&D-related activities. It includes discriminant and construct validity tests applied to the lexicon, alongside reliability, predictive, and external validity tests applied to the textual R&D measure. In the discriminant validity test, we ensure that our R&D lexicon does not significantly overlap in terms of word usage with any other (non-R&D) lexicon, such as that of Loughran and McDonald (2011), among others. The construct validity shows that: (i) our R&D lexicon's core words appear more frequently than core-contextual word pairs, highlighting the importance of contextual words in accurately identifying R&D activity; (ii) firms use diverse word combinations beyond the core words "research" and "development" to convey their R&D activity, demonstrating the lexicon's comprehensiveness; (iii) the R&D narrative disclosure varies significantly across industry sectors, aligning with established R&D intensity levels; and (iv) the textual R&D measure's prominence across U.S. states aligns with their historical levels of R&D expenditure.

The reliability validity test suggests that the textual R&D measure exhibits high persistence over time, with firms tending to remain in the same decile from one year to the next, confirming its reliability as a measure of firm-level R&D activity. The predictive validity test shows a strong positive relation between the textual R&D measure and future patent counts, citation impact, and value relevance of patents, highlighting its effectiveness as a measure of firm innovation. Lastly, the external validity test shows that our R&D measure can be generalized and applied to firms outside the U.S. regulatory framework.

In the second part, we show the measure's ability to uncover the impact of R&D on firms' future value, performance, and growth opportunities, particularly for firms with zero or missing R&D expenditures and missing patents, which are technically impossible to examine using standard R&D measures. $ ^{4} $ Building on the limitations of traditional R&D measures to capture the multidimensional nature of innovative activities, we show that our measure: (i) exhibits low correlation with (other) widely used R&D measures; (ii) provides significant incremental explanatory power for future firm performance beyond traditional R&D measures; (iii) captures firms' future growth prospects; (iv) reflects innovation activities that investors can more efficiently incorporate into valuations and thereby is not associated with the R&D puzzle; and (v) uniquely explains firm valuation for firms with zero citations or missing R&D data—a context previously inaccessible to conventional R&D expenditure measures.

Our study contributes to the literature in several important ways. First, we develop and validate a novel lexicon that captures firms' R&D activity through narrative disclosures in 10-K filings. In contrast to traditional metrics, which are often constrained to quantifiable and realized innovation outputs, our approach uses the qualitative content disclosed in firms' financial statements. The lexicon is developed based on institutional definitions of R&D (e.g., the Frascati Manual, NSF guidelines) and draws on terminology widely used in structured survey instruments (OECD, 2015), enabling a comprehensive operationalization of a firm's R&D activity, even when quantitative R&D disclosures are incomplete or missing.

Second, we use this newly developed textual measure to address a specific puzzle that existing R&D metrics cannot resolve: the impact of latent R&D activities on firm performance, as well as the potential for future growth and valuation. Although prior studies have shown that firm value depends on R&D expenditure (Eberhart et al., 2008), patent citations (Gao and Chou, 2015), and innovative efficiency (Hirshleifer et al., 2013), these measures are inherently backward-looking and fail to capture the full spectrum of innovation activities. As a result, markets tend to underreact to the information they convey, leading to delayed price adjustments and excess future returns (Lev and Sougiannis, 1996; Chan et al., 2001). In this vein, we provide insights into the future-oriented nature of R&D, including strategic direction and innovation potential, which are critical for understanding a firm's long-term performance, future growth prospects, and valuation.

Third, we contribute to the literature on the informativeness of narrative disclosures by focusing specifically on R&D-related narratives. Whilst prior research has examined intellectual capital disclosure as a whole (Kaplan and Luigi, 2011; Macagnan, 2009), our focus on R&D allows us to uncover valuable insights that may be obscured in aggregate analyzes. We also address the potential for managerial discretion in narrative R&D disclosures, which can serve as both a communication tool to reduce information asymmetry and a strategic mechanism to obfuscate poor performance or emphasize long-term plans (Patelli and Prencipe, 2007; Asay et al., 2018; Andreou et al., 2025; Mazzi et al., 2025).

Finally, our study contributes to the broader literature in finance and accounting that uses textual data to analyze corporate behavior. Unlike prior studies that focus on specific industries, countries, or short time periods (e.g., La Rosa and Liberatore, 2014; Li and Lin, 2024), we utilize a large-scale corpus of 10-K filings, enabling us to generalize our findings across a wide range of firms and industries. This approach reduces variability and selection biases and demonstrates the scalability and applicability of our method. By converting unstructured text into a structured format, we provide a robust framework for future research on the role of qualitative disclosures in shaping investor perceptions and market outcomes.

## 2. The R&D activity lexicon

We develop a lexicon consisting of core and contextual words that describe a firm's R&D activity. The lexicon construction follows a structured four-step procedure that integrates theoretical foundations with empirical validation, as depicted in Fig. 1.

First, we build the lexicon of core words by utilizing the National Science Foundation (NSF, 2018) document that compiles all the official definitions of research and development in the U.S., unedited and as they appear in their original sources. $ ^{5} $ Such sources include: (i) the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development's Frascati Manual 2015; (ii) the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), federal procurement, tax, and accounting guidance; and (iii) surveys from the National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics (NCSES). By scrutinizing lexical tokens (nouns, verbs, and adjectives) within these definitions, we identify word stems that detail R&D activity. We include both singular and plural forms of each identified word to capture all possible variations. $ ^{6} $ Subsequently, we employ Princeton University's

WordNet Lexical Database and the Harvard IV-4 Dictionary to identify synonyms, as it is likely that managers use variants of the same words in their disclosures. Additionally, to ensure financial relevance, we refine the word list by retaining only those terms that appear in the (Loughran and McDonald, 2011) business lexicon.

Second, to guide the structure of the lexicon, we apply established contingencies for R&D classification: (i) industry/sector (Frascati Manual disciplinary fields), (ii) organizational structure and processes (R&D life cycle stages), (iii) nature of R&D activities (basic/applied research vs. product development), and (iv) collaborative dimensions of R&D.

Starting with the sectoral dimension, the Frascati Manual's Table 2.2 categorizes R&D into broad disciplinary fields. Although this classification is structured by research domains (e.g., natural sciences, engineering, medical sciences), it indirectly aligns with core conceptual themes observed in industry-specific R&D research. For instance, the "Engineering and Technology" category highlights the central role of technology in R&D, spanning fields such as civil, electrical, mechanical, and nano engineering. Similarly, the "Medical and Health Sciences" category supports the inclusion of clinical research as a critical R&D dimension, encompassing basic medicine, clinical medicine, and medical biotechnology.

Beyond industry classifications, the organizational structure and processes of R&D vary across sectors but share commonalities based on the stage of the R&D life cycle. Some activities are industry-specific, such as clinical trials in pharmaceuticals or software and systems development in technology and manufacturing. However, certain phases — research, development, and patenting — are fundamental across industries, which has raised the need to categorize each R&D phase separately. The Frascati Manual defines research as "basic research" or "applied research". Basic research is experimental or theoretical work undertaken primarily to acquire new knowledge of the underlying foundations of phenomena and observable facts, without any particular application or use in view. Applied research is original investigation undertaken to acquire new knowledge. It is, however, directed primarily towards a specific practical aim or objective: to maximize cost savings and increase productivity.

The Frascati Manual also defines “development” as experimental, describing it as systematic work that draws on knowledge gained from research and practical experience to produce additional knowledge, develop new products or processes, or improve existing products or processes. Once a product or a process is developed, patenting is a key R&D step, given its role in the protection and commercialization of innovations (Yuan et al., 2021; Garavito and Rueda, 2021). By securing patents, businesses gain legal protection that prevents rivals from producing or distributing their patented inventions. This legal safeguard allows firms to establish a unique market position, attract consumers, and gain a competitive edge by restricting competitors' ability to copy product designs (Bekkers et al., 2023).

Finally, the collaborative dimension of R&D emerges from industry life cycle literature (Fritsch and Lukas, 2001; Moeen and Agarwal, 2017). As industries evolve, firms increasingly engage in R&D collaborations to mitigate risks, share resources, and access complementary knowledge (Tether, 2002; Titman and Roberto, 2010; Kapoor and Patia, 2014). Early-stage industries often rely on in-house R&D due to technological uncertainty (Chesbrough and Crowther, 2006), but as the industry matures, collaboration becomes more prevalent (Kapoor and Patia, 2014). Empirical studies further confirm that firms collaborate to access emerging technological expertise (Howard et al., 2016) or to overcome resource constraints in high-risk R&D environments (Okamuro, 2007).

Ergo, the lexicon we develop categorizes firms' R&D activities into six core components, namely, Research, Development, Technology, Patents, Clinical, and Collaboration, based on strong theoretical foundations from the Frascati Manual and previous literature. These six components collectively cover the various facets of the R&D process, from exploration (“research”) to practical application (“development”), the tools and systems used (“technology”), legal aspects (“patents”), domain-specific advancements (“clinical”), and the collaborative nature of R&D endeavors (“collaboration”).

Around each core word indicated above, we identify contextual words that are verbs or adjectives describing the stage, nature, or process of R&D activity (e.g., if a product is in the initial stage or in the development stage). $ ^{7} $ The development of the contextual lexicon and categorization is in line with prior literature on R&D measurement (Kerssen-van Drongelen et al., 2000). A contextual perspective suggests that R&D classifications should be influenced by several contingencies, including: (i) the industry or sector in which a firm operates (Kerssen-van Drongelen and Bilderbeek, 1999); (ii) the organizational structure of R&D functions (Kerssen-van Drongelen and Cook, 1997; Kerssen-van Drongelen et al., 2000); (iii) the nature of R&D activities, whether basic/applied research or new product development (Poh et al., 2001); and (iv) the resources available for R&D, including funding, expertise, and time (Kerssen-van Drongelen and Cook, 1997). Core words thus anchor the lexicon, whilst contextual words specify their use in practice. For example, the contextual word “process” refers to experimental methods when linked to “research”, but to manufacturing when linked to “development”. $ ^{8} $ Together, these form core-contextual word pairs. This approach provides a comprehensive and theoretically justified framework for analyzing the diverse and dynamic nature of R&D activities, ensuring a holistic view of innovation across industries.

To demonstrate our method, we present the following extract from Microsoft Corporation (2020) 10-K filing:

Developing new technologies is complex. It can require long development and testing periods. Significant delays in new releases or significant problems in creating new products or services could adversely affect our revenue... We expect to continue making acquisitions or entering into joint ventures and strategic alliances as part of our long-term business strategy... To defend against security threats to our internal IT systems, our cloud-based services, and our customers' systems, we must continuously engineer more secure products and services, enhance security and reliability features, improve the deployment of software updates to address security vulnerabilities in our own products as well as those provided by others...

Microsoft's 2020 10-K filing reveals the details of their R&D activity. The firm highlights the complex nature of developing new technologies, the potential risks of delays or problems in new product releases, and the importance of acquisitions, joint ventures, and strategic alliances for growth. Our R&D lexicon simplifies technical language in corporate filings by identifying R&D contextual terms within a span of ten lexical tokens of one of the core words in the same sentence (indicated in bold). Core words relate directly to R&D activities (e.g., "technologies", "ventures"), whilst contextual words offer insights into the broader R&D environment (e.g., "improve", "new", "joint"). The core-contextual combinations identified from the extract are "technologies & developing" (component 3), "product & new" (component 3), "ventures & joint" (component 6), "software & improve" (component 3), "services & improve" (component 3), "products & improve" (component 3).

In Microsoft’s example, the combination of core and contextual words highlights the interconnectedness of R&D and business strategy,

emphasizing both the complexity of technological development and the broader strategic goals. This core-contextual approach allows for the decoding and clarification of the language used in corporate filings, enabling stakeholders to better understand the strategic importance and potential impact of a firm's R&D activity. Additional examples and extracts from 10-K filings can be found in Internet Appendix B.

Conducting textual analysis of 10-K filings using core-contextual word pairs allows us to identify significant information within management disclosures that is associated with the underlying semantic patterns related to R&D activity. Therefore, the final lexicon is based on the occurrence of 264 core-contextual words that are most likely associated with R&D activity. ### 2.1. Human and AI content validity of the R&D lexicon

We primarily utilize human expertise, and as a supplementary tool to cross-validate findings, ChatGPT (Chat Generative Pre-trained Transformer), to assess the content validity of our R&D lexicon, ensuring that the chosen core and contextual words are accurate and comprehensive. For human validation, we distributed a survey to 60 industry professionals in the R&D field (either financial/business experts or engineering experts from both public and private organizations). In the first part of the questionnaire, they were asked to read 30 statements that were randomly extracted from 10-K filings and to determine their relevance to firm-level R&D activity, as well as to categorize them into six R&D components (i.e., "research", "development", "technology", "patent", "clinical", "collaboration"). In the second part, participants were asked to match the six core words (which are the previously mentioned six R&D components) to a list of possible contextual words that they think commonly appear together and describe firms' R&D activity. The questionnaire is shown in Internet Appendix C.

The response rate is 50%, with 30 completed surveys. Respondents are 37% female and 63% male, predominantly from finance, accounting, and business backgrounds, with the rest from non-business fields like engineering and psychology. Public organization employees constitute 43%, whilst private organization employees make up 57% $ ^{9} $.

To assess inter-rater reliability, we calculate Cohen's kappa ( $ \kappa $), which accounts for agreement between respondents beyond chance. The kappa value for “Not Relevant” responses is 0.59, whilst for “Relevant” responses it is 0.61, indicating moderate to substantial agreement according to Landis and Koch (1977). The overall combined kappa was 0.58, further supporting the reliability of our assessment.

Whilst human judgment remains the foundation of our approach, ChatGPT serves as a supplementary tool to cross-validate findings, identify inconsistencies, and improve the lexicon's structure. It provides a systematic and scalable way to benchmark results, but does not replace human expertise. Instead, it helps refine our methodology by highlighting areas where human and AI assessments align or diverge, offering additional insights into contextual challenges. Given the resource constraints on expert evaluations, incorporating ChatGPT allowed us to efficiently expand the validation process beyond the limited number of human-reviewed cases.

Similar to human validation, ChatGPT is used to validate the same 30 statements, indicating whether each is R&D-related and providing structured reasoning. $ ^{[10]} $ The results show a high consistency between human and AI assessments. Both humans and ChatGPT accurately evaluate 29 out of 30 statements, demonstrating a shared understanding of the R&D terms. Below are the two statements that humans and ChatGPT respectively had trouble responding to correctly:

“Statement 3 out of 30 - Our general expectations concerning these industries and markets are based on market research, industry publications, other publicly available information and assumptions made by us based on this information and our knowledge of these industries and markets, which we believe to be reasonable”.

This is correctly identified by the majority of the respondents (73.3%) as “Not Relevant”. The respondents who answered “Relevant” (16.7%) did so because of the word “research”, a common mistake that a topic modeling algorithm, such as the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), would have made. $ ^{11} $ That is why contextual words are important when building this R&D lexicon, as they complement the meaning and overall understanding of the topic, which aligns with conclusions in prior studies (e.g., Andreou et al., 2020).

On the other hand, the following statement has troubled the respondents, with 53.3% saying it is “Relevant” and the remaining 46.7% split between 40.0% saying it is “Not Relevant” and 17.9% saying “Cannot Say”.

“Statement 11 out of 30 - Management believes that future growth of our firm will primarily occur through the exploration and development of our existing properties. However, we may elect to proceed through collaborative agreements and joint ventures in order to share expertise and reduce operating costs with other experts in the oil and gas industry”.

When participants were asked to identify the key words that indicate R&D involvement, 53.3% of those who responded “Relevant” correctly specified the category “collaboration”, but the remaining participants did not. This indicates that the perception of whether a firm is involved in R&D activity depends on the general understanding and expertise of the reader, as well as the overall context of the narrative. However, ChatGPT correctly identifies the exploration and development of existing properties, in addition to collaborative agreements and joint ventures, as activities relevant to research and development.

The overall alignment in responses between human experts and ChatGPT lends credence to the content validity of the R&D lexicon, suggesting that it accurately captures the intended meanings and complexities of R&D activity. Importantly, the presence of only one statement inaccurately assessed by the majority of humans and another by ChatGPT underscores the effectiveness of our methodology. By combining human contextual understanding with AI's comprehensive analysis of 10-K filings, we validate our lexicon and ensure it effectively captures firm-level R&D activity.

In the second part of the questionnaire, participants were required to match core R&D words with up to four contextual words they believed could co-occur in a sentence. The purpose of this part of the survey is to evaluate participants' ability to accurately identify which contextual words are commonly associated with each core R&D term. Due to their prior exposure and experience from the first part of the questionnaire, human participants were well-positioned to grasp context-specific interpretations, resulting in more accurate pairings compared to ChatGPT. Comparing the results with ChatGPT highlights the limitations of the AI model and other topic modeling approaches. Despite using the R&D lexicon, ChatGPT struggles to select the most relevant contextual words due to its inability to analyze large datasets of 10-K filings comprehensively. This limitation underscores the value of human judgment in understanding contextual usage and the importance of a well-defined lexicon.

### 2.2. Discriminant validity of the R&D lexicon

To establish that our R&D-focused lexicon does not overlap significantly in terms of word usage with any other (non-R&D) lexicon, we investigate its discriminant validity. Discriminant validity is a test used to ensure that a specific measure is distinct from other constructs and assesses whether a particular concept is truly measuring that concept and not something else. In this case, we examine the possibility that the R&D words in our lexicon may proxy for tone or sentiment reported by Loughran and McDonald (2011). Fig. 2 classifies all the R&D words of our lexicon into various word categories provided in the Loughran-McDonald Master Dictionary, which has been extensively used in the literature. The lexicon classifies business words as they appear in 10-K filings into the following categories: “positive”, “negative”, “uncertainty”, “litigious”, “constraining”, “superfluous”, “interesting context”, “modal”, and “irregular verbs”. Interestingly, the overlap between our R&D words and various categories is minimal. Only one stem word (“claim*”) out of 264 core-contextual R&D-related words falls into the negative category, and 19 out of 264 core-contextual R&D-related words fall into the positive category (i.e., “collabor*”, “improv”，“advance*”, “innovat*”, and “breakthrough”), whilst the overwhelming majority of the words are unclassified. This minimal overlap demonstrates the discriminant validity of our lexicon, indicating that our R&D words are distinct from those in other widely used dictionaries, further underscoring the unique focus and specificity of our approach.

To further support the validity of our lexicon, we conduct a comparative analysis between our R&D lexicon and the one proposed by Merkley (2014), highlighting several critical distinctions that underscore the enhanced specificity and breadth of our approach in capturing R&D-related disclosures. Merkley developed a lexicon of R&D keywords and phrases (shown in Appendix B) to classify sentences as R&D-related disclosures, with the aim of enhancing the understanding of how firms communicate their R&D efforts and investments. Whilst Merkley's lexicon provides a valuable baseline, our approach differs in both the construction and scope of testing, particularly in its ability to capture the contextual specificity of R&D-related disclosures within the reporting practices of each firm.

Further, our lexicon systematically incorporates both singular and plural forms of key terms, such as “trial” and “trials”, which Merkley’s lexicon omits, ensuring a more inclusive analysis of relevant text. A notable strength of our lexicon lies in its inclusion of detailed verb forms across various tenses and grammatical structures. For instance, whilst Merkley’s lexicon may use “innovative” or “innovation”, our lexicon extends this by including variants such as “innovating”, “innovated”, and “innovates”, allowing for a more subtle capture of R&D discourse. Additionally, we have enriched our lexicon by integrating synonyms and words with similar meanings, further broadening the scope of detectable R&D-related language.

Most importantly, our methodology diverges significantly from Merkley's in its approach to capturing compound forms. Merkley's lexicon identifies R&D terms primarily through compound phrases such as "clinical candidate", thereby capturing only a single occurrence per phrase. In contrast, our method leverages core-contextual combinations, which allow for a more granular and frequent identification of R&D activity. For example, with the core words "candidate" and "candidates", our lexicon generates 14 distinct combinations with various contextual terms that can precede or follow the core words, such as "candidate & clinical", "candidate & preclinical", "candidate & experimental", and so forth. $ ^{12} $ This approach not only captures the diversity of R&D activity but also significantly increases the likelihood of detecting these activities within the text.

In summary, our lexicon's incorporation of specific, varied, and contextually rich terms, coupled with a systematic approach to verb forms and the innovative use of core-contextual pairings, ensures a more detailed and accurate measure of R&D activity. This methodological advancement over Merkley's lexicon provides a robust tool for analyzing R&D disclosures, particularly in its ability to capture a broader spectrum of R&D-related language within corporate filings.

## 3. The textual R&D activity measure and its validation

Once the lexicon has been validated, we proceed with the quantification of R&D activity per annual 10-K filing. We first acquire 10-K filings from the SEC EDGAR database, utilizing the “Stage One 10-X”

involves comprehensive cleaning and normalization. The steps of the parsing process are described in detail in Internet Appendix E. The resulting corpus consists of a clean, standardized, plain-text version of the filings that preserves the linguistic content and other relevant sections, suitable for natural language processing.

Then, for every firm-year 10-K filing, from 1995 until 2022, we count the occurrences whereby a contextual word appears within a span of ten lexical tokens  $ (-10 $ and  $ +10) $ of one of the core words within the same sentence and then normalize the count based on the firm's 10-K filing length. $ ^{14} $ To enhance precision, we exclude any core-contextual pair when a negation term (i.e., “no”, “not”, “none”, “neither”, “never”, “nor”, “nothing”, “nobody”, “nowhere”, “without”, “cannot”, or the two-word phrase “no one”) appears before the core word within the specified window span. Specifically, our textual R&D activity measure is defined as:

 $$  Narrative\ R\&D=\frac{Sum\ of\ all\ R\&D\ core-contextual\ word\ pairs}{Total\ number\ of\ words\ in\ firm^{\prime}s\ 10-K\ filling}\times100 $$ 

Eq. (1) is consistent with the literature suggesting that textual measures often involve counting occurrences or frequencies of specific elements (e.g., words, phrases, or characters) within a document. Longer documents naturally tend to have higher counts than shorter ones, even if the actual content or relevance is similar. Normalization allows for a fair comparison by standardizing the measures across different document lengths and thus prevents length bias (Andreou et al., 2020; Loughran and McDonald, 2011). In this spirit, the aggregated frequencies of the core-contextual word pair occurrences give substance to a simple and parsimonious measure to proxy for a firm's level of R&D activity.

The textual analysis method used in this paper has several advantages. First, it allows sampling from a broad range of publicly listed U.S. firms that make R&D investments. This is an improvement compared to prior empirical work that uses hand-collected samples and/or focuses on general voluntary disclosures and restricted industries or countries. Second, the measure focuses on qualitative disclosures that firms provide alongside accounting performance measures, which reduces selection bias concerns, as firms with material innovative investments are mandatorily required to provide such information through their 10-K filings. Firms' 10-K filings are provided at the same time as the audited financial statements and typically include more detailed information than the annual report to shareholders.

### 3.1. Validation tests of the textual R&D activity measure

Textual analysis is extensively employed in finance research (Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Hoberg and Phillips, 2010; Hoberg et al., 2014). The development, refinement, and implementation of the coding scheme is key for the quality of textual analysis (Carley, 1993), yet (Short et al., 2010) have asserted that very few papers using textual analysis describe the process of selecting their word dictionaries; similarly, few studies support the validity of their textual measures (Short et al., 2010). Validating a measure is an essential step in ensuring the accuracy and credibility of research efforts. As highlighted by Short et al. (2010), establishing construct validity is a key element that enables researchers to showcase the suitability of their measurements in accurately capturing the concepts they aim to investigate. Accordingly, we conduct four validity tests, namely construct, reliability, predictive, and external validity tests, to determine how well our textual measure captures R&D-related activity.

#### 3.1.1. Construct validity

To construct our sample, we merge data from Compustat-CRSP with textual-related variables, which we estimate using 10-K filings from the SEC Edgar database. Following prior studies and common practice in financial research, we drop observations with less than 1 million in total assets (Merkley, 2014; Campello and Giambona, 2013). Smaller firms often lack the rigorous financial reporting standards of larger firms, which can lead to less reliable data and limit the availability of meaningful information in their 10-K filings. Additionally, their limited market impact and R&D activities may not significantly influence broader innovation outcomes. Excluding these firms enhances the robustness and stability of our analysis. $ ^{15} $ Our main sample consists of 11,386 firms with 97,691 firm-year observations.

#### 3.1.2. Reliability validity

We conduct a reliability assessment, a critical step in ascertaining the textual measure's consistency (Peter, 1979). Prior studies suggest that firms' R&D activity has been stable over the years, as the accumulation of R&D capital and self-sustained engagement in R&D activity are long-lasting (Mahou et al., 2015). Ergo, in principle, if our textual R&D measure is properly constructed to capture R&D activity, then it should exhibit persistence over the years. Table 1 presents the mean annual transition probabilities by deciles of the textual R&D measure. If the textual R&D measure is non-random and indeed a persistent quantity, then it is expected to remain in the same portfolio decile in two subsequent periods with a greater than 10% probability. Consistent with our expectations, we observe that firms in the lowest (1st) decile of the measure in a year have a 69% chance of remaining in the lowest decile in the following year, whilst firms in the highest (10th) decile remain in the same decile the following year with a 77% probability. In general, the transition probabilities of all diagonal elements are notably higher than 10%, whilst the elements representing decile changes (off-diagonal elements in the matrix) exhibit rapid probability declines. These results indicate that our measure is indeed persistent over time and lends support to the notion that it potentially reflects the behavior of an R&D-related variable.

#### 3.1.3. Predictive validity using patents and citations

We test the ability of our textual R&D measure to predict future patents and citations. Patents incentivize investors to invest in R&D and stimulate an interest in ongoing discovery and the production of new things (Antonipillai and Lee, 2016). Citations, on the other hand, are typically observed years after the grant of the patent, establishing that the original R&D that led to innovation was feasible and commercially worthwhile. To perform this test, we use two alternative proxies for future patents and citations as dependent variables, following (Kogan et al., 2017). The first is the natural logarithm of the number of patents summed over the next three years, i.e.,  $ \log(1 + \text{Patents}_{t+1 \rightarrow t+3}) $, and the second is the ratio of the number of citations to the number of patents summed over the next three years, i.e.,  $ \log(1 + \text{Citations}_{t+1 \rightarrow t+3} / \text{Patents}_{t+1 \rightarrow t+3}) $. Consistent with prior research (e.g., Merkley, 2014; Bellstam et al., 2021), our model specification includes a comprehensive set of control variables to account for observable characteristics that may independently influence future innovation outcomes: Sentiment (the difference between the percentage of words in 10-K filings identified as having a positive tone and the percentage of words identified as having a negative tone), R&D (R&D expenditure/total assets), Advertising (advertising expenses/total assets), Tangibility (net property, plant, and equipment divided by total assets), Leverage (long-term debt over total assets),  $ \log(\text{Age}) $ (the natural logarithm of the number of years since the firm first appears in CRSP),  $ \log(\text{Total Assets}) $, and Software Cost (capitalized software and development costs over total assets). These variables capture aspects such as firm size, age, capital structure, investment in intangible assets (e.g., R&D, advertising, software), asset composition, and managerial sentiment. Controlling for these factors helps mitigate omitted variable bias and allows us to more precisely identify the predictive power of our textual R&D measure on subsequent patents and citations. All continuous control variables are winsorized at 1%. The definition of all variables is tabulated in Appendix C.

Traditional patent metrics, such as the patent counts or citation-based measures, provide useful insights into R&D productivity, in terms of the quantity or impact of patents. However, these metrics often fall short of fully capturing the true economic value generated by a firm's innovative activities. To address this, we also employ the value relevance of patents, which serves as a proxy for investment opportunities incorporating the replacement cost of patent capital. This patent capital measure is derived from the market's reaction to newly granted patents, capturing the economic value embedded in a firm's patent portfolio more accurately than simple counts or citations (Woeppel, 2022; Gutiérrez and Philippon, 2017).

The construction of this variable is grounded in event-study methodology, where the market value of patents is estimated using the three-day idiosyncratic stock returns following patent announcements (Woeppel, 2022). This approach accounts for the probability of successful patent applications and adjusts for industry-specific depreciation rates, ensuring that the measure reflects the dynamic and economically relevant value of patents over time. Unlike traditional metrics, this method directly ties patent value to investor perceptions and market outcomes, providing a more precise assessment of R&D impact.

Our empirical analysis, reported in Table 5, supports the notion that narrative R&D disclosures in 20-F filings exhibit a statistically significant relation at 1% with innovation outcomes. Specifically, model specifications (1) and (2) show that our measure remains predictive of future patenting activity and citation impact, similar to its performance in 10-K filings. Moreover, whilst our analysis focuses on English-language filings, the lexicon can be adapted to non-U.S. contexts and other languages by (i) identifying native-language R&D keywords, (ii) adjusting for jurisdiction-specific narrative styles, and (iii) validating performance against innovation outcomes. A brief summary of these steps and a full table is provided in the Internet Appendix F, highlighting the potential for applying our methodology across diverse regulatory and linguistic environments.

As an alternative type of filing, we use the S-1 form to assess adaptability beyond domestic regulatory frameworks. The S-1 form, filed with the SEC, is required for firms planning to go public for the first time and provides extensive disclosures about business operations, risks, and financial, including R&D activities. Since these filings are forward-looking and intended to attract investors, they often emphasize innovation strategies and expected technological advancements. Likewise, in the 20-F cases, the results in models (3) and (4) show that narrative R&D disclosures in S-1 filings exhibit robust statistical significance in predicting future patents and citations.

## 4. Narrative R&D activity and firm performance

Recognizing the limitations of traditional R&D metrics, our analysis further investigates the impact of our narrative R&D measure on Tobin's Q, especially for firms with zero or missing R&D expenditures or patents. By capturing qualitative insights into R&D strategies through narrative disclosures, we aim to highlight the relation between this innovative measure and performance outcomes. This additional analysis is vital, as it demonstrates the effectiveness of our narrative R&D measure in contexts where traditional metrics are insufficient.

Tobin's Q has traditionally reflected a firm's future development expectations, as it is derived from stock market prices and indicates the value investors allocate to a firm's tangible and intangible assets based on predicted future revenue and cost streams (Bracker and Ramaya, 2011; Bardhan et al., 2013). Tobin's Q is a forward-looking measure of valuation (Dong et al., 2022) that is affected by investors' perceptions and attitudes about future events. According to Demsetz and Villalonga (2001), Tobin's Q is also favored by economists who have a better understanding of market constraints. It is more appropriate than any accounting profit ratio, as the latter is affected by accounting practices.

In contrast, the second component, i.e., growth opportunities, is inherently forward-looking and more likely to be influenced by qualitative information about a firm's innovation strategy. According to prior research, R&D-intensive firms systematically invest in the creation and cultivation of future growth options, which amplify the portion of firm value attributable to growth opportunities (Trigeorgis and Lambertides, 2014). These investments, often aimed at developing new products, technologies, or processes, enhance the firm's future earning potential (Lin, 2012). Market participants incorporate this forward-looking information when forming expectations about firm value. In this context, narrative R&D disclosure plays a crucial role by providing additional signals about the firm's innovation pipeline and long-term strategy, thereby influencing expectations about future growth. Although R&D activities may involve higher risk and cost of capital, they can lead to a higher overall market valuation through growth opportunities if investors believe these activities will generate value in the future (Kogan et al., 2017).

Building on these arguments, we propose that growth opportunities serve as a key channel through which narrative R&D contributes to firm value. To empirically assess this mechanism, we examine whether our measure of narrative R&D is associated with the rate of growth opportunities. We employed a widely recognized measure of growth opportunities that effectively captures firms' future growth potential and investors' expectations (Bali et al., 2020). Specifically, the growth option rate GOR is estimated by dividing the growth options value by the overall market value of the firm. The growth options value is estimated by subtracting from the overall market value the perpetually discounted stream of the firm's average 3-year operating cash flows under a no-growth assumption. The overall market value of the firm is calculated as the sum of total liabilities and the market value of equity, where equity market value is computed as the number of shares outstanding multiplied by the year-end stock price. If our narrative R&D measure meaningfully captures firms' innovative efforts, it should be positively associated with this indicator.

These findings further underscore the informativeness of our measure and support the notion that narrative R&D contributes to firm value primarily by enhancing investor expectations about future growth opportunities.

a plethora of empirical studies persistently show that firms investing heavily in research and development earn returns that cannot be explained by standard asset-pricing models. This literature suggests a positive relation between R&D intensity and excess future returns as a well-documented empirical fact and is largely split into two strands, seeking to explain whether this R&D puzzle is due to systematic mispricing by investors or rational compensation for unmeasured risk.

A dominant explanation centers on market inefficiency driven by accounting rules and investor behavior because the immediate expensing of R&D is a critical catalyst for mispricing. The seminal study by Lev and Sougiannis (1996) finds that R&D expenditures are value-relevant and that capitalizing them leads to better measures of earnings and value (see, also, Chan et al., 2001). High-R&D firms often appear less profitable, and investors often undervalue them relative to their true economic potential. However, as the benefits of their investments emerge in future earnings, the market gradually corrects this mispricing, leading to higher long-term returns. Aboody and Lev (2000) show that information asymmetry makes it difficult for outside investors to value R&D-intensive firms accurately, leading to initial underpricing and a subsequent drift upwards as information is gradually revealed. An alternative view argues that the premium is rational for bearing distinct, hard-to-quantify risks associated with R&D. For instance, Berk et al. (2004) argue that R&D projects resemble compound options whose values are highly sensitive to underlying uncertainty, whereas Eberhart et al. (2004) support a mispricing explanation.

Overall, the consensus is that the R&D puzzle arises because the market has persistent difficulty in accurately pricing the complex, uncertain, and option-like nature of intangible R&D investments. Traditional measures, whilst widely used, rely on simplified proxies that may obscure the complexity or strategic distinction of the R&D activity they aim to capture. This has led to persistent evidence of valuation errors tied to R&D. Ergo, the traditional R&D measures embed a downward bias that leads markets to misinterpret the value implications of R&D, which load directly on investor misperceptions (Damodaran, 1999, 2009). The proposed textual measure offers a novel lens that enables us to explore dimensions previously inaccessible, such as contextual framing, that reveal patterns possibly overlooked by purely quantitative methods and enable investors to assess them more accurately.

Consequently, to re-test the R&D puzzle, we assess the long-term abnormal performance following corporate disclosures. To this end, we apply the Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return (BHAR) methodology, using the 10-K filing date, the point at which annual financial and narrative disclosures become publicly available, as the event date. This timing ensures that our return measurement begins only after the market has access to the full set of annual financial and narrative disclosures, including R&D-related information. Our approach mirrors the methodological framework used by Chambers et al. (2002).

Our empirical design also draws inspiration from (Eberhart et al., 2004), who document that increases in R&D expenditures are followed by significantly positive abnormal stock returns. They interpret this as evidence of investor underreaction, suggesting that increases in R&D spending represent new information that the market is slow to incorporate. Building on this insight, we consider in the regression analysis both a positive change in the traditional R&D to sales ratio and a positive change in our proposed narrative R&D measure as distinct signals of innovation-related information. Each variable captures a different dimension: the former reflects a financial commitment to innovation, whilst the latter captures a shift in strategic emphasis or narrative framing. To ensure the integrity of our analysis, we exclude firms with missing R&D expenditure data whilst retaining those that report zero R&D, since a reported zero still conveys information and reflects a disclosure choice. By including both measures separately, we test whether the market responds differently to quantitative versus qualitative innovation information and whether either (or both) serve as catalysts for abnormal returns.

The results presented in Table 11 show a clear divergence between the two measures. Consistent with prior literature, particularly (Eberhart et al., 2004), we find that a positive change in the traditional R&D to sales ratio is significantly associated with future abnormal returns. In contrast, there is a non-significant relation between the narrative R&D measure and future excess returns.

Whilst this section focuses on the Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return analysis to illustrate the implications of our narrative R&D measure for the R&D puzzle, we also conducted a broader set of asset pricing tests, including the construction of a narrative R&D-based factor used for conducting spanning tests using 30 intangible asset portfolios (Hou et al., 2020). These results, available in Internet Appendix I (Table IA5), show that the narrative R&D factor is priced and spans a larger set of intangible portfolios than a factor based on the traditional R&D to sales ratio. Overall, these findings suggest that the narrative R&D measure captures more complete and forward-looking information that investors incorporate more efficiently into valuations.

## 7. Policy implications

Our findings have important implications beyond academic venues, highlighting the policy implications of requiring comprehensive R&D narrative disclosures. Encouraging or even mandating detailed R&D narratives can promote transparency, helping investors, analysts, and other stakeholders make better-informed decisions about a firm's prospects and potential risks (Mazzi et al., 2022). For investors, this measure provides a qualitative lens beyond traditional metrics such as R&D expenditure and patent counts, enabling better assessments of market alignment, R&D quality, growth signals, and potential risks. This deeper insight into a firm's innovation strategy enhances investment decisions and reduces exposure to misleading claims. Fig. 9 illustrates how the R&D narrative disclosure measure provides insights for investors, policymakers/regulators, and firms, whilst simultaneously driving demand for credible and standardized disclosure practices.

Policymakers can use the measure to understand industry priorities and evaluate policy effectiveness. Particularly, they can make more informed decisions regarding public funding and adjustment of regulatory frameworks by identifying key areas of R&D focus and tracking the narrative changes post-policy implementation (Neubig et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2024). Our textual measure also enables policymakers to benchmark innovation performance across regions, fostering a competitive landscape that rewards transparency and accountability in R&D activities (Chen et al., 2022). Ultimately, by integrating our narrative disclosure measure, stakeholders can manage the complexities of innovation more effectively and promote a robust and dynamic innovation ecosystem.

Finally, regulators could consider implementing standardized guidelines for R&D narrative disclosures, promoting consistency across industries and enhancing comparability (Leuz and Wysocki, 2016). Such standardization could address concerns about vagueness and subjectivity in reporting, ultimately improving how firms are evaluated and understood by the market.

This allows us to gain a more thorough understanding of the complex relation between R&D narrative disclosures and future patents, citations, and the overall value of the firm. #### Measure of firm valuation

Tobin's Q is the book value of current assets (act) minus book value of common equity (ceq) plus the market value of common equity (csho  $ \times $ prcc_f), divided by total assets (at).

### Measure of rate of growth opportunities

GOR is the growth option rate estimated by dividing the growth options value by the overall market value of the firm. The growth options value is estimated by subtracting from the overall market value the perpetually discounted stream of the firm's average 3-year operating cash flows (oancf+xint-dpc) under a no-growth assumption. The overall market value of the firm is calculated as the sum of total liabilities (It) and the market value of equity, where equity market value is computed as the number of shares outstanding (csho) multiplied by the year-end stock price (prcc_f).

### Main explanatory variable

Narrative R&D is the intensity of R&D-related core-contextual words, as listed in Appendix A (Table A.1), across the entire 10-K

filing. It is calculated by dividing the number of R&D-related core-contextual pairs by the total number of words in the 10-K document and multiplying by 100 to express it as a percentage.

##### Control variables

Total Assets is total assets (at) measured in millions U.S. Dollars.

Leverage is long-term debt (dltt) divided by total assets (at).

Cash Flow is cash  $ (ch) $ divided by total assets  $ (at) $.

R&D is R&D expenditure (xrd) to sales (sales).

Tangibility is property, plant and equipment (ppent) divided by total assets (at).

Software Cost represents the software and development costs (capsft) to total assets (at).

Advertising is advertising expenses (xad) to total assets (at).

Age is the number of years that the firm is covered in the Compustat universe.

ROA is income before extraordinary items (ib) divided by total assets (at).

Market to Book is the market value  $ (prcc\_f \times csho) $ divided by the book value of equity  $ (ceq) $.

Return is the average weekly return during the fiscal year.

Idiosyncratic Volatility is the standard deviation of firm-specific weekly returns during the fiscal year.

Sentiment is the difference between the percentage of words in 10-K filings identified as having a positive tone and the percentage of words identified as having a negative tone, according to the (Loughran and McDonald, 2011) lexicon. Specifically, the percentage of positive (negative) words is obtained by dividing the number of positive (negative) words by the total number of words in the 10-K filing and multiplying by 100. The sentiment measure is then computed as the percentage of positive words minus the percentage of negative words.

Readability is the Flesch Reading Ease score that is expressed as 206.835−(1.015×ASL)−(84.6×ASW) where ASL represents the average sentence length (total words divided by total sentences), and ASW denotes the average number of syllables per word (total syllables divided by total words) (Flesch, 1948). A higher Flesch Reading Ease score indicates greater readability, with scores from 70 to 100 signifying “fairly easy” to “very easy” texts, whilst scores between 60 and 70 reflect standard difficulty. Scores below 50 denote complex texts.
