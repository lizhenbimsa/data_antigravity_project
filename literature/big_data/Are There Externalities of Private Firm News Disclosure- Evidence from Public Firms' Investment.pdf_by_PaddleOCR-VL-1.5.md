# Are There Externalities of Private Firm News Disclosure? Evidence from Public Firms' Investment

Abstract: This study examines whether and how voluntary news disclosure made by private firms affects the investment sensitivities of public peer firms. Analyzing data from U.S. public firms from 1996 to 2018, we discover that public firms' investment sensitivities intensify in industries with active private firm disclosures; a one-standard-deviation increase in private firm news disclosure raises public firms' investment sensitivities by 14.5%-17.6%. To mitigate endogeneity, we employ instrumental-variable methods, leveraging the staggered implementation of prudent investor rules and enforceability of non-compete agreements. Our results show that these effects are magnified in industries marked by the higher expected industry return volatility and less local newspaper coverage. We find that news from private firms significantly enhances public firms' investment sensitivities, regardless of its sentiment. This research highlights the crucial role of private firm disclosures in influencing public firms' investment decisions, enhancing our understanding of information spillovers in corporate disclosure.

Keywords: Private firm news disclosure; Disclosure externality; Investment sensitivity

JEL Classification: D80; G31; G32; M41

# Are There Externalities of Private Firm News Disclosure? Evidence from Public Firms' Investment

## I. Introduction

Private firms represent a substantial segment of the U.S. economy, contributing significantly to sales, profits, and investments. Studies by Biery (2013) and Asker, Farre-Mensa, and Ljungqvist (2015) underscore their considerable economic contributions. $ ^{1} $ Despite their importance, the related literature has largely centered on public companies due to the greater accessibility and abundance of their financial data. $ ^{2} $ This focus in the literature has overshadowed the important role of private firms in shaping economic outcomes, especially regarding how their voluntary disclosures affect public peer firms in the same industry.

Despite the critical importance of disclosures from private firms, most accounting research has emphasized public firm disclosures (Badertscher, Shroff, and White 2013; Shroff, Verdi, and Yost 2017; Bernard, Blackburne, and Thornock 2020; Glaeser and Omartian 2022; Kim and Valentine 2023). Our study addresses this limitation by examining how voluntary disclosures from private firms affect the investment decisions of public firms operating in the same industries as the disclosing private firms. This research helps fill a significant research gap identified by Kim and Olbert (2022) and enhances our understanding of the potential externalities of private firm disclosures—an underexplored but important concept in the disclosure literature.

In this study, we focus on one specific type of voluntary disclosure: firm-initiated press releases. Our specific research question is whether more press releases issued by private firms in

an industry can increase public firms' investment sensitivities to their own investment opportunities. We posit that greater voluntarily disclosed information from private firms can improve the information environment for public firms in the same industry by reducing industry-level uncertainties about demand, supply, cost, and preferences, which in turn allows public peer firms to better identify and exploit investment opportunities and adjust investment decisions accordingly. Analyzing the data of private and public firms as well as their news disclosures from Capital IQ during 1996-2018, we reveal that U.S. private firms voluntarily disclose significant amounts of information. We further find that U.S. public firms' investments are more sensitive to investment opportunities in industries with more private firm news disclosure. This response is both statistically and economically substantial: a one-standard-deviation increase in private firm news disclosure raises public firms' investment sensitivities by 14.5%-17.6% from the mean. This result is robust to different model specifications, as well as alternative proxies for corporate investment, private firm disclosure levels in an industry, and investment opportunities. The main result also holds after controlling for mandatory financial reports filed by public and some private firms.

To address endogeneity concerns, we utilize instrumental-variable (IV) methods with exogenous variations that plausibly influence private firms' disclosure incentives but are unrelated to public firms' investment decisions. One IV leverages the staggered implementation of prudent investor rules (PIR) across U.S. states, which likely increases local private firms' financing opportunities. A second IV capitalizes on variations in non-compete agreement (NCA) enforceability across U.S. states, potentially affecting private firms' capacity to attract talent. Anecdotal evidence suggests that private firms use press releases to secure financing and recruit a

skilled workforce. $ ^{3} $ Our analysis confirms that both IVs—the staggered implementation of prudent investor rules and the variations in non-compete agreement enforceability—meet the relevance criteria for valid instruments. In the second stage of our two-stage least squares (2SLS) analysis, in which we use these instruments for private firm news disclosure, we observe results consistent with our baseline findings. We acknowledge that potential threats to the exclusion restriction may remain, and therefore, these results should be interpreted as complementary evidence rather than definitive proof of causality.

We further investigate how cross-sectional variations affect public firms’ investment sensitivities to private firms’ disclosures. Our analysis reveals that public firms demonstrate heightened investment sensitivities in industries characterized by greater uncertainties, as measured by higher expected industry return volatility (Eisdorfer 2008; Jurado, Ludvigson, and Ng 2015). Similarly, this effect is more pronounced in industries with fewer alternative information sources, as indicated by reduced local newspaper coverage (Engelberg and Parsons 2011; Peress 2014). These heterogeneity tests provide consistent support for our hypothesis that private firms’ disclosures significantly mitigate investment uncertainty for public firms.

In addition, we analyze the impact of sentiment in private firms' news disclosures on the investment decisions of public firms using the Loughran and McDonald (2011) sentiment dictionary. Our analysis reveals that positive, negative, and neutral news issued by private firms all increase the investment sensitivities of public firms.

Finally, we utilize machine learning techniques to classify the contents of news disclosures according to the topical categories mandated by the SEC's Form 8-K disclosures. This

classification shows that 81% of private firms' news disclosures align with 8-K-related topics, demonstrating private firms' use of news releases to communicate material information. By contrast, only 67% of public firms' news disclosures correspond to the topical categories of 8-K filing mandates. Moreover, our findings indicate that private firms' news disclosures that conform to 8-K topical categories enhance the investment sensitivities of public firms. These analyses lead us to conclude that private firm news disclosures contain material information relevant to investment decisions.

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. Firstly, it responds to calls from Minnis and Shroff (2017) and Roychowdhury, Shroff, and Verdi (2019) for more exploration into the real economic impacts of private firms' disclosures, a topic previously underexplored due to the focus on public firms' disclosures. Our research aligns closely with the motivation of Kim and Olbert (2022), who observe a reduction in equity demand among public firms following disclosures from private firms. Our results complement Kim and Olbert (2022) by demonstrating that private firm news disclosure, which is effectively voluntary in nature, can have significant positive externalities on the investment decisions of their public counterparts, enriching the discussion around the informational role of private firms in terms of cost-benefit analysis. In this regard, our evidence on the externalities of private firm disclosures underscores the important economic roles played by private firms.

Second, our study contributes to the information spillover literature, which has traditionally concentrated on how public firm disclosures influence external stakeholders, including other public or private peer firms (Badertscher et al. 2013; Durnev and Mangen 2009, 2020; Beatty, Liao, and Yu 2013; Shroff, Verdi, and Yu 2014; Li 2016; Shroff et al. 2017; Cho and Muslu 2021; Kim and Valentine 2023; Tseng and Zhong 2024). Our research expands on this stream of literature

by exploring the less-studied reverse effect—how private firm disclosures significantly impact public firms. We document positive externalities arising from private firms’ news disclosures, illustrating their substantial influence on public firms’ investment behaviors.

Our study offers new insights on private firm disclosures for capital market regulators and participants. While regulators recognize that cost-benefit considerations of financial reporting differ between private and public companies (Private Company Council 2013), prior literature emphasizes disclosure costs for private firms (Ali, Klasa, and Yeung 2014; Bernard 2016). We highlight potential benefits, showing that private firm disclosures can generate significant positive externalities, particularly in improving public firms' investment efficiencies. Our finding suggests that the net benefits of private firm disclosures may be greater than previously thought. Thus, our findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the incentives and impacts of private firm disclosures, which are crucial for accurate cost-benefit analyses of financial reporting decisions (Bradshaw et al. 2014).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews relevant literature and formulates our hypothesis. Section III describes our data sources and the methodology for sample construction. Section IV details our main empirical findings. Section V discusses additional tests to validate our results. Finally, we conclude the study in Section VI.

## II. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

##### Literature review on disclosure externalities

Firms operate under shared economic conditions, including common demand, supply, and cost factors. Disclosures from one firm can illuminate these conditions for stakeholders of other firms, a phenomenon known as “disclosure externalities.” Theoretical literature, including Dye (1990), Admati and Pfleiderer (2000), and Lambert, Leuz, and Verrecchia (2007), highlights the

importance of this phenomenon. Empirical research supports the beneficial effects of public disclosures. For instance, Shroff et al. (2014) show that peer firm disclosures reduce agency problems within multinational corporations by informing the parent firm about the economic environment of its foreign subsidiaries. Shroff et al. (2017) and Kajüter, Klassmann, and Nienhaus (2019) further find that these disclosures decrease capital costs and provide spillover benefits for smaller firms, respectively. Arif and De George (2020) observe informational benefits from U.S. industry leaders' quarterly earnings disclosures for foreign firms with semi-annual reporting schedules. Consistent with information spillovers from regulated to unregulated (private) firms, Breuer, Hombach, and Müller (2022) document that unregulated private firms reduce their own disclosures in the presence of regulated firms' disclosures. However, exploration into disclosure externalities of private firms is nascent. Kim and Olbert (2022) began this inquiry by examining how private firm disclosures affect global investors' demand for public firm equity across countries. They find that increased private firm disclosure transparency reduces global investors' demand for public equity, consistent with negative pecuniary externalities. Our study complements and extends this line of research but arrives at different conclusions, given that we examine different dimensions. First, while Kim and Olbert focus on financial statement disclosures, we examine the effects of news disclosures, providing insights into how different types of information affect investment decisions. Second, we find that private firm news disclosures actually increase public firms' investment sensitivities, suggesting positive information externalities rather than negative pecuniary externalities.

The different focus, setting, and diverse findings between our study and Kim and Olbert's highlight the complexity of disclosure externalities across various types of information and economic contexts. For instance, our focus on news disclosures may capture timelier and

potentially more industry-specific information compared to the annual financial statement disclosures examined by Kim and Olbert. These differences suggest fruitful avenues for future research, such as exploring how different types of disclosures (e.g., financial statements vs. press releases), might have varying effects on capital allocation decisions and firm behaviors across different economic contexts.

### The effect of private firm news disclosure on public firms’ investment

Theoretical models suggest that uncertainty about investment prospects significantly influences firms' decisions to continue or abandon projects (Berk, Green, and Naik 1999, 2004). This uncertainty prompts firms to seek clarity to adjust their investments appropriately (Pástor and Veronesi 2009). Empirical evidence demonstrates that firms learn from their peers' successes and failures when navigating investment uncertainties (Yang and Steensma 2014; Krieger 2021; Tseng 2022). These findings indicate that firms make investment choices based on what they learn from their peers, regardless of whether they are public or private.

Recent studies, likely due to data availability, have focused on how public firm disclosures influence corporate investment decisions. Badertscher et al. (2013) argue that the mix of public and private firms in an industry significantly affects industry-level transparency, given public firms' mandated disclosures. Badertscher, Shanthikumar, and Teoh (2019) and Bernard et al. (2020) show that public firms' misvaluations and access to competitors' information can influence investment levels and product decisions. $ ^{4} $ Cho and Muslu (2021) find that public firms adjust their capital investment and inventory based on optimism or pessimism in peer firms' MD&A narratives. These studies suggest a consensus that public firm disclosures can influence their peers.

Private firms disclose information for various reasons, $ ^{5} $ suggesting that their disclosures could have peer effects similar to those of public firms, as the underlying economic mechanisms should apply to both. Barrios et al. (2024) demonstrate that disclosures from recent Initial Public Offering (IPO) firms, via their S-1 filings, can significantly impact peer firms, evidenced by increased new business registrations following IPOs, especially in areas of high economic uncertainty. Beyond S-1 filings, Dambra, Schonberger, and Wasley (2024) find that private firms commonly engage in voluntary disclosure activities such as issuing press releases and attending investor conferences, even before filing their IPO prospectus.

Our study extends this framework by proposing that news disclosures from private firms provide additional information, reducing industry-level uncertainties about demand, supply, cost, and preferences. This information externality allows public peer firms to make more informed investment decisions aligned with growth opportunities. However, our hypothesis is not without tension. Improved private firm news disclosure might lead investors to allocate resources away from public firms (i.e., negative pecuniary externalities, see Kim and Olbert 2022), potentially increasing public firms' financing costs. Higher investment costs could lead firms to seek higher-return investments, potentially lowering investment sensitivities (Modigliani and Miller 1958; Myers 1974). Given these competing effects, we present our hypothesis in null form:

Hypothesis: The information conveyed from news disclosures of private firms has no effect on the investment sensitivities exhibited by public peer firms in the same industry.

## III. Data Sources and Sample Construction

Following Gao, Harford, and Li (2017), we start with all U.S. public and private firms with non-missing values for total assets in Capital IQ (an affiliate of Standard & Poor’s) from 1996 to 2018. $ ^{6} $ Capital IQ collects and verifies private firm data from a variety of sources, with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) filings being a primary source. A private firm is subject to mandatory disclosure requirements by the SEC in two situations. First, if the firm decides on a registered public debt offering, the Securities Act of 1933 requires it to file a registration statement (Form S-1) with the SEC. Second, even if the firm has not registered a security offering, it must file an Exchange Act registration statement if it has more than 10 million in total assets and 500 or more shareholders for a class of equity securities, such as common stock. Consequently, our private firm sample predominantly comprises larger, more established entities rather than typical early-stage firms due to this requirement. These firms are subject to mandatory SEC disclosure requirements, including filing Exchange Act registration statements and ongoing reporting via annual and quarterly reports. In addition, the firm is required to continue filing periodic financial reports. Therefore, private firms must file their financial reports with the SEC because of their qualification in scale or public debt issuance. $ ^{7} $ Besides sourcing from SEC filings, Capital IQ has significantly increased the coverage and comprehensiveness of its private firm data offering by sourcing from a variety of providers, including Preqin, Crunchbase, CreditSafe, and Dun & Bradstreet. $ ^{8} $ After requiring Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes to be available

and excluding firms in the financial service or utilities industries (i.e., firms with SIC codes between 6000 and 6999 or between 4900 and 4999), our initial sample includes 120,880 unique private firms headquartered in the U.S. and 16,106 unique public firms.

The news disclosure data are obtained from the Capital IQ Key Developments database, which provides structured summaries of firms’ material news with more than 100 key development types, such as executive changes, mergers and acquisitions (M&A), and changes in corporate guidance. The Key Developments database is uniquely suited to address our hypothesis for several important reasons. First, the database provides press releases initiated by firms, rather than news articles initiated by the media. Our research thesis is about the externalities of private firms’ disclosure through news, which requires the disclosure to be initiated by the firms themselves. Second, the Key Developments database provides extensive coverage of private companies. In our sample, the volume of press releases initiated by private firms accounts for approximately 12% of total press releases from all public and private firms combined. $ ^{9} $ The richness of the data allows us to investigate the intensity and variations in private firms’ news presence in each industry. Third, the database provides well-summarized news content and classifies press releases into news event types, which allows us to conduct textual analyses on the topical categories as well as the sentiment of press releases.

To construct our news disclosure sample, we first compile all news issued by our sampled public and private firms from 1996 to 2018. We then identify the press releases by ensuring that the sources of news are from companies' websites or the press release distributors (e.g., PR Newswire, Business Wire, and GlobeNewswire). Our final news disclosure sample includes

180,944 press releases from 14,302 unique private firms and 1,348,315 press releases from 13,867 public firms in 63 2-digit SIC industries. Further details on our news disclosure sample selection procedures are presented in Appendix B.

To examine public firms’ investment, we also construct a sample of all NYSE-, AMEX-, and NASDAQ-listed U.S. firms from 1996 to 2018. To ensure that our results are not driven by micro firms, we also require firm-years to have assets greater than 10 million dollars (Doidge and Dyck 2015). After merging this dataset with our news disclosure sample and dropping firm-year observations with missing control variables, the final regression sample includes 69,290 firm-year observations.

## IV. Research Design and Empirical Results

### Private firm news disclosure

To measure the news disclosure of private firms, we scale private firm news disclosure by their assets in an industry, thus reflecting the scope of the disclosure. We then take the natural logarithm of one plus this ratio. Specifically, the measure,  $ Private\ newspre_{j,t} $, is constructed as follows:

 $$ Private\ newspre_{j,t}=Ln(1+\frac{The\ number\ of\ press\ releases\ issued\ by\ private\ firms\ in\ industry\ j\ at\ year\ t}{Total\ assets\ (in\ billion\ )\ of\ private\ firms\ in\ industry\ j\ at\ year\ t}). $$ 

We observe significant variations in the news presence of private firms across industries. Table 1. Panel A lists the top 10 (bottom 10) industries in which private firms exhibit the highest (lowest) news presence. Private firms in industries such as Miscellaneous Manufacturing Industries, Metal Mining, Legal Services, and Electronic & Other Electric have the highest news disclosure presence, with their average news disclosure greater than 2, which indicates that every billion dollars of assets owned by a private firm in these industries are associated with 6.39 press releases in a year. In contrast, private firms in industries such as Membership Organizations,

Railroad Transportation, Local & Interurban Passenger Transit, and Agricultural Services have an average news disclosure presence well below 0.26, meaning that every billion dollars of assets owned by a private firm in these industries are associated with only 0.30 press releases in a year.

Next, we examine factors that determine Private newspre. We argue that variations in Private newspre could be driven by factors both exogenous and endogenous to the investment decisions of public peer firms. Specifically, Private newspre is likely driven by (1) the endogenous factors that are related to industry fundamentals, which generate variations in both Private newspre and the investment decisions of public peer firms, and (2) the exogenous factors that plausibly generate variations through the disclosure incentives related to Private newspre but not in the investment decisions of public peer firms. To address the latter factors, we leverage variations in financial and labor market regulations.

Specifically, we exploit variations in financial and labor market regulations through the adoption of prudent investor rules (PIR) and enforceability of non-compete agreements (NCA). PIR adoption enables local pension fund investment in venture capital, a crucial financing source for private firms. Similarly, NCA enforcement particularly affects private firms' ability to attract talent, as they typically have fewer resources compared to public firms. $ ^{10} $ These regulations, therefore, create stronger incentives for private firms to enhance their disclosure to attract capital and talent.

Panel B of Table 1 presents the determinant analysis of Private newspre. It shows that the two exogenous factors significantly correlate with Private newspre after controlling for industry

fundamentals. Specifically, consistent with our expectation, private firm news disclosure is positively associated with both the PIR adoption and the NCA enforceability. Overall, Panel B of Table 1 indicates that both exogenous and endogenous factors drive the variations in Private newspre. This determinant analysis further reveals that time-varying industry fundamentals may concurrently influence both private firms' news disclosures and public firms' investment decisions, with the extent of this influence contingent on the fixed effect structure employed. Thus, we add control variables that proxy for industry fundamentals, including Industry ROA (the profitability of the industry), Industry growth (the growth potential in the industry), and Industry leverage (the financial leverage of the industry). After controlling for possible endogenous variables consistent with our main regression design below, the regression results plausibly capture how the exogenous variations in Private newspre affect public peer firms' investment decisions.

#### Research design

Our main prediction is that public firms benefit from private firm news disclosure by increasing investment sensitivities to their own investment opportunities, while we control for the separate effect from public firm news disclosure. We estimate the following regression to test this prediction:

 $$  Investment measures_{i,t}=\beta_{1}Private\ newspre_{j,t-1}+\beta_{2}Investment\ opportunity_{i,t-1} $$ 

 $$ +\beta_{3}Private\ newspre_{j,t-1}\times Investment\ opportunity_{i,t-1}+\beta_{4}Public\ newspre_{j,t-1} $$ 

 $$ +\beta_{5}Public\ newspre_{j,t-1}\times Investment\ opportunity_{i,t-1}+Controls_{i,j,t-1}+\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}, $$ 

where  $ Investment measures_{i,t} $ represents the investment levels of public firm i in year t. Specifically, we use two investment measures, CAPEX and INV, following Badertscher et al. (2013). CAPEX is measured as capital expenditure divided by the beginning-of-year total assets; INV is measured as the change in gross fixed assets divided by the beginning-of-year total assets. Following the literature (e.g., Badertscher et al. 2013; Asker et al. 2015), we use a firm's Tobin's Q to proxy for

investment opportunity, which is measured as the market value of equity plus the book value of debt divided by the book value of total assets. The key variable of interest in Equation (2) is the interaction of Private newspre and Tobin's Q. The coefficient  $ \beta_{3} $ of this interaction term captures the effect of private firm news disclosure on public firms' sensitivity of investment decisions to investment opportunities.

We separately include Public newspre in Equation (2), which is defined as the natural logarithm of one plus the total number of press releases issued by public firms in the industry scaled by their assets. This design allows us to quantify the information role of news disclosure of private firms after controlling for that of public firms. We further control for a set of firm and industry characteristics that are shown to be related to investment decisions (Asker et al. 2015). Specifically, we control for a set of firm characteristics that are shown to affect investment levels, including Size (the natural log of total assets), Leverage (debt in current liabilities plus long-term debt divided by total assets.), Cash (cash and short-term investments divided by total assets.), Fixed assets (property, plant, and equipment divided by total assets), Dividend (dividend paid scaled by the beginning-of-year total assets), ROA (net income scaled by the beginning-of-year total assets), and the proxies of industry fundamentals introduced in the prior subsection. All explanatory variables are in lagged year t-1 values to alleviate the endogeneity concern. Following the related literature (Badertscher et al. 2013; Cho and Muslu 2021), we use a lead-lag (years t on t-1) regression specification because firms take time to digest new information and adjust their investment decisions.

We include industry or firm fixed effects to control for the time-invariant unobservable factors at the industry or firm level. We also control for year fixed effects to capture systematic changes in investment across years. One potential concern is that our initial fixed effect specification only

controls for heterogeneity in investment levels, not investment sensitivities across industries. To mitigate this concern, we further include the interactions of Tobin's Q with control variables and industry fixed effects to ensure the robustness of our main results (see the detail in the following subsection). Standard errors are clustered at the firm level to account for the residual correlations of a firm's investment over time.

Table 2 presents summary statistics for our test variables. All continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% and 99% levels. The average value of Private newspre is 1.57, which indicates that per billion dollars of assets owned by a private firm are associated with 3.81 press releases per year. This number suggests a considerable scope of news disclosures made by private firms in an industry. In contrast, the average value of Public newspre is 2.13, meaning that per billion dollars of assets owned by a public firm are associated with 7.41 press releases in a year. These findings suggest that public firms, on average, have a larger scope of news disclosure than private firms.

### The impact of private firm news disclosure on public firms’ investment sensitivities

Table 3 presents the results of the main regression test related to Equation (2). Columns (1) to (3) present the results using CAPEX as the dependent variable. The coefficient on Private  $ \text{newspre} \times \text{Tobin's } Q $ is positive and significant, regardless of whether we include the industry or firm fixed effect. Taking column (2) for example, the coefficient on  $ \text{Private \text{newspre} \times \text{Tobin's } Q} $ is 0.0015 (t-stat. = 3.95), suggesting that the investments of public firms are more responsive to investment opportunities when private firms in the same industry disclose more news information. Meanwhile, we observe a similar effect from public firm news disclosure. The coefficient on  $ \text{Public \text{newspre} \times \text{Tobin's } Q} $ is 0.0017 (t-stat. = 5.44), suggesting that the news disclosure from public firms in the same industry also improves the investment sensitivities of public firms. To our

interest, the positive and significant externality generated by private firm news disclosure is novel and robust even after controlling the well-documented positive effect of public firm disclosure (Glaeser and Omartian 2022; Kim and Valentine 2023; Tseng and Zhong 2024). This new evidence demonstrates that information embedded in private firm news disclosures is valuable. The aggregated information from private firms within the same industry collectively enhances the industry information environment, reduces industry uncertainty, and helps their public peers better understand investment opportunities. To mitigate the confounding factor concern that differences across firms in their investment sensitivities may happen to be correlated with private firms' disclosures, we further extend Equation (2) by including the interactions of Tobin's Q with industry fixed effects and control variables (Breuer and deHaan 2024). In this model, the main effects of Tobin's Q are absorbed by these fixed-effect interactions. The results in column (3) demonstrate that our findings remain consistent and robust under this specification. $ ^{11} $

In columns (4) to (6), in which we use INV as the dependent variables, we observe similar findings. Therefore, the results are robust to investment measures based on either capital expenditures or changes in gross fixed assets. In terms of economic magnitudes, the results in columns (2) and (5) indicate that a one-standard-deviation increase in private firm news presence increases public firms’ investment sensitivities to growth opportunities by 14.5% and 17.6% from the mean level, when public firms’ investment is measured by CAPEX and INV, respectively. $ ^{12} $

These findings confirm our prediction that private firm news presence in the industry incrementally increases the investment sensitivities of public firms.

In addition, we use the methodology of Oster (2019) to examine the extent of the role that unobservable factors must play in our setting to nullify the effect of Private  $ \text{newspre} \times \text{Tobin's } Q $ on public firms' investment. As shown in Table A1, Oster's delta equals 1.87 and 2.14 for columns (2) and (5) with firm and year fixed effects, suggesting that unobservable factors must be about twice as important as observable, controlled factors to produce no treatment effect (i.e.,  $ \beta_{3} $ from Equation (2) = 0). Given that Equation (2) already controls for several firm and industry factors and firm fixed effects, such unobservable factors are unlikely to drive our main results.

By using Tobin's Q as the proxy for investment opportunities, we encounter the risk of a potential confounding effect in the following sense. Stock price changes could guide managers to make investment decisions (Zuo 2016), and thus provide feedback to the real economy (Dye and Sridhar 2002; Chen, Goldstein, and Jiang 2007; Edmans, Goldstein, and Jiang 2015). In our prediction, we assume that public firm managers improve the efficiency of investment decisions by learning from private peer firms' information disclosures rather than indirectly learning from their own firms' share price dynamics.

We follow the prior literature (e.g., Bloom, Bond, and Van Reenen 2007; Badertscher et al. 2013) to use firms' sales growth instead of Tobin's Q to measure investment opportunities, excluding the price impact from capital markets. In Table 4, we find a positive relationship between private firm news presence in the industry and public firms' investment sensitivities to sales growth. In terms of economic magnitudes, the results in columns (2) and (5) indicate that a one-standard-deviation increase in private firm news presence increases public firms' investment sensitivities to sales growth by 18.1% and 12.7% from the mean level, when public firms'

investment is measured by CAPEX and INV, respectively. $ ^{13} $ In columns (3) and (6), we include interactions of sales growth with industry fixed effects and control variables, and we observe consistent results. $ ^{14} $ To further exclude the confounding effect of stock price learning, we employ an alternative research design based on investment inefficiency measures following voluminous prior research (e.g., Biddle and Hilary 2006; Biddle, Hilary, and Verdi 2009; Chen, Hope, Li, and Wang 2011) and observe results consistent with our baseline findings. $ ^{15} $

## Instrumental variables and 2-stage least squares analyses

We construct instrumental variables (IV) and conduct 2-stage least squares (2SLS) analyses based on the following two regulation settings that likely stimulate the disclosure incentives of private firms in order to attract external financing or talented employees, while being largely unrelated to public firms' investment decisions.

As a first IV strategy, we exploit the staggered state-level adoption of prudent investor rules, which allow pension funds to invest in local venture capital funds across U.S. states. As local venture capital funds tend to invest in local private firms, the PIR adoption would subsequently enhance the influx of financing opportunities for local private firms that otherwise lack public financing opportunities (González-Uribe 2020). While the PIR adoption varies at the state-year level, we construct the IV related to the PIR adoption at the industry-year level to match with the

Private newspre data. Specifically, we identify the geographic locations of all private firms in Capital IQ and collect detailed information on the staggered adoption of PIR across states over time (González-Uribe 2020). The IV, PIR exposure, is estimated as the number of private firms in a 2-digit SIC industry that are located in the PIR-adopting states divided by all private firms in that industry. PIR exposure essentially measures the extent to which the staggered adoption of PIR affects the disclosure incentives of private firms in each industry-year. According to our expectation, the larger the PIR exposure, the more private firms in the industry would experience stronger incentives to increase disclosure to attract external financing from local venture capital funds.

In the second IV strategy, we exploit the various extent of non-compete agreement enforceability across U.S. states. The NCA binds employees to their employers and makes it difficult for less well-resourced companies to attract employees away from more established firms (Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming 2009; Ewens and Marx 2018), thus disproportionately affecting the hiring of many private firms. Compared to public firms that are generally more resourceful and transparent, private firms need to work harder to attract talented employees, especially under a tighter local labor market. Voluntarily disclosing various information through comprehensive news press releases would be one way to reduce the information asymmetry between the private firm and the potential employees. We convert a continuous state-level NCA enforcement index to construct the IV at the industry-year level. $ ^{16} $ Specifically, for each industry-year, we calculate Weighted NCA as the weighted average of the NCA enforcement index across U.S. states, with the weight being the number of private firms in a 2-digit SIC industry for each state divided by all

private firms in that industry. We expect that the larger the Weighted NCA, the more private firms in the industry would exhibit stronger incentives to increase disclosure to attract talented employees. To resolve the concern that changes in the NCA enforceability may lead to changes in public firms' investment activities, we restrict our analysis to public firms residing in states that did not change their NCA enforceability. This sampling restriction helps mitigate concerns about potential violations of the exclusion restriction, although we acknowledge that completely ruling out all potential violations is challenging in this context. $ ^{17} $

The results from our 2SLS regressions are reported in Table 5. The first-stage regressions, as well as the Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistics, confirm that PIR exposure and Weighted NCA are strong instruments for Private newspre. Panel B presents the results from the second-stage regressions. Consistent with our main test results, the coefficients for the fitted Private newspre×Tobin's Q are positive and statistically significant.

Nonetheless, we acknowledge the limitations of our approach as we cannot entirely eliminate potential threats to the exclusion restriction. #### Cross-sectional test results

We further explore how cross-sectional variations influence the investment sensitivities of public firms to private firms' news disclosures. In our first cross-sectional test, we test a conjecture that when the underlying industry uncertainty is higher, additional information from private firms would be more useful to public peer firms. Industry uncertainty is proxied by the conditional

expected stock return volatility in each industry for each year. $ ^{18} $ We estimate the annual expected industry volatility to match with the yearly data for the regression tests. For each industry-year, the conditional expected stock return volatility, Expected variance, is measured by the 12-month-ahead forecasted volatility conditional on information available in the last month of the prior year. A higher value of the expected industry volatility would suggest a higher underlying industry uncertainty. We interact Expected variance with our variable of interest, Private newspre×Tobin’s Q. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 6 show that the coefficients for this three-way interaction term are positive and statistically significant, suggesting that the positive effect of private firm news disclosures on public firms’ investment sensitivities is more pronounced in industries with higher uncertainty. The economic magnitude is also significant. Taking column (1) for example, the results indicate that a one-standard-deviation increase in Expected variance increases the investment response by 40% from the mean level. $ ^{19} $

Our second cross-sectional test examines the interaction effect with local media coverage. We posit that local media coverage and private firm disclosures may serve as substitutes for providing industry-relevant information. In regions with less local media coverage, voluntary disclosures by private firms become a more critical information source for public peer firms. Our conjecture aligns with literature suggesting that media, especially local media, play a crucial role in

information discovery about private firms and their relation to the broader industry (Dyck, Volchkova, and Zingales 2008; Engelberg and Parsons 2011; Graf-Vlachy et al. 2019).

Following the literature (Gentzkow, Shapiro, and Sinkinson 2011; Gao, Lee, and Murphy 2020), we use the number of local newspapers in each U.S. county where a private firm is headquartered to identify the changing intensity of local media coverage between 1995 and 2016. $ ^{20} $ For each industry-year, the local news coverage intensity (Local news coverage) is computed as the weighted average of the number of local newspapers across U.S. counties, with the weight being the number of private firms in a 2-digit SIC industry for each county divided by all private firms in that industry. We then interact Local news coverage with our variable of interest, Private  $ \text{newspre}\times\text{Tobin's }Q $. In columns (3) and (4) of Table 6, the coefficients for this three-way interaction term are negative and statistically significant, suggesting that public firms rely more heavily on direct disclosures from private peers for industry-relevant information in the absence of extensive local media coverage.

Separately, we expect that more relevant private firm information makes their disclosures more informative for public peers. We measure the relevance of peer information using the degree of earnings synchronicity (Shroff et al. 2017) and market position (Hou 2007) of private firms. Earnings synchronicity is measured as the mean adjusted R-squared across all private firms from regressions of a firm's quarterly ROA on the aggregate industry quarterly ROA over the previous sixteen quarters within each 2-digit SIC industry. We interact Earnings synchronicity with our variable of interest, Private newspre×Tobin's Q. Columns (1) and (2) of Table A3 show positive

and significant coefficients for this three-way interaction, indicating that the positive effect of private firm news presence on public firms' investment sensitivities is stronger in industries with higher earnings synchronicity among private firms.

In addition, we expect that news disclosures from private firms with strong market positions would provide public peer firms with a better reference for the industry trends or for broader levels of the economy. We use the total sales of private firms in a 2-digit SIC industry scaled by the total sales of all firms in the same 2-digit SIC industry to measure the market share position (Market position) of private firms in that industry. We then interact the market share of private firms with Private newspre×Tobin’s Q. Columns (3) and (4) of Table A3 show that the coefficient estimate for this three-way interaction variable is positive and statistically significant. Therefore, we conclude that the positive externalities of private firm news disclosure to public firms’ investment sensitivities are strengthened by the stronger market positions of private firms.

## V. Additional Tests and Results

#### Results of private firms’ news sentiment

In addition to the presence of news disclosure in an industry, the sentiment conveyed by private firm disclosure is also of interest to public firms as it may affect the direction of investment adjustments. Literature on shared sentiment (Badertscher et al. 2019) suggests that both private and public firms operate in the same product and labor markets, thus sharing the sentiment about industry prospects and the macroeconomic outlook. The real-world practices confirm that the sentiment and expectations of small business owners reflect broader economic conditions, which

are of interest to public firms. $ ^{21} $ In particular, the collective sentiment of various firms in the same industry can reflect their economic outlook on a specific issue.

We predict that both positive and negative news disclosed by private firms would be informative to the investment decisions of public firms in the same industry and would potentially increase their sensitivities to investment opportunities. Specifically, a greater positive news disclosure by private firms in an industry might suggest promising prospects in investing, which encourages public peer firms to invest and prevents them from foregoing good investment opportunities. In contrast, a greater negative news disclosure by private firms in an industry might suggest a low expected return on investment, thus warning their public peers not to engage in negative net-present-value projects. We also expect that neutral news will contain some useful information to public peer firms, although its impact on the direction of investment levels is somewhat ambiguous.

We rely on textual analysis of the news disclosure content issued by private firms. Using the sentiment dictionary of Loughran and McDonald (2011), we calculate the numbers of positive and negative words for each news summary contained in the Key Developments database. We then define the news as positive (negative) news if the number of positive words is greater (smaller) than the number of negative words, and as neutral news otherwise. Then, we construct our measure of industry-level news disclosure by private firms according to news sentiment. Specifically, for each 2-digit SIC industry, we define a positive (negative) news presence of private firms as the amount of positive (negative) news of private firms in the 2-digit SIC industry scaled by the total

amount of news of all firms in the same industry. Similarly, we define a neutral news presence of private firms as the amount of neutral news of private firms in the 2-digit SIC industry scaled by the total amount of news of all firms in the 2-digit SIC industry. $ ^{22} $

Using the specifications in Equation (2), we investigate how the positive, negative, and neutral news presence of private firms affects the investment sensitivities of public firms in the same industry. The regression results are reported in Table 7. We find that the estimated coefficients for most interaction terms between private firm news sentiment measures and Tobin's Q are positive and statistically significant. Therefore, our findings suggest that positive, negative, and neutral news issued by private firms all increase the investment sensitivities of public firms.

## Results of private firms’ news content

To assess the content of private firm news disclosures, we start by manually examining 100 press releases of private firms. Press release content such as successful external financing, increased investment in new facilities, hires or departures of top management members, new product development, or collaborations with business partners can offer valuable insights into industry prospects for other firms in the same industry. These press releases not only aid peer firms in identifying growth opportunities but also provide insights into potential barriers and challenges associated with similar projects within the industry.

To assess the materiality of private firm news disclosures, we classify the content of private firm press releases according to the topical categories mandated by the SEC's Form 8-K disclosures. Through machine-learning-based textual analyses of the specific information content

of press releases, we classify news topics into 8-K related and 8-K unrelated. $ ^{23} $ We discover that 81% of private firms’ news disclosures relate to topical categories mandated by 8-K disclosures, indicating that private firms indeed use news disclosures to convey material information. In contrast, only 67% of public firms’ news disclosures fall in the topical categories of Form 8-K. Table 8 presents how news content with the 8-K and non-8-K topics from private and public firms could generate externalities to the investment decisions of public firms. We find that the coefficients for Private  $ \text{newspre}(8-K) \times \text{Tobin's } Q $ are positive and statistically significant. The above analyses lead us to conclude that private firm news disclosures contain material information relevant to investment decisions.

To validate our findings, we conduct several robustness checks. First, acknowledging the potential impact of firm size, we implement a size-weighted disclosure measure to address the concern that relative to larger private firms, smaller private firms might exert limited influence on public firms' investment decisions. As illustrated in Table A4, our findings regarding the positive externality of private firm news disclosure on public firms' investment decisions remain consistent even with this adjustment. Second, we additionally control for the characteristics of all private firms in an industry, including their sales, market position, and investment levels. ## VI. Conclusion

Capital market researchers have emphasized the importance of considering the dynamics of private firms to draw appropriate inferences (Ali et al. 2009). In this study, we examine a significant number of press releases that are voluntarily issued by private firms. If greater news disclosure made by private firms complements and reinforces the existing industry information and reduces uncertainty in the industry, we predict that public peer firms can better identify and exploit investment opportunities. We do find that, given its comprehensive, frequent, and voluntary nature, news disclosure made by private firms can generate externalities by improving the investment sensitivities of public peer firms. This result is not driven by underlying industry fundamentals and remains solid through a battery of robustness checks. The sensitivities of investment are further magnified in industries marked by the higher expected industry return.

volatility or less local newspaper coverage. Moreover, we find that a dominant proportion of private firms' news disclosures conform to 8-K topical categories, suggesting that such news disclosures contain material information relevant to investment decisions.

Overall, we show rich evidence about the role of private firm news disclosure in terms of its presence, sentiment, and content. In this regard, our evidence concerning externalities of private firm disclosures highlights the important economic roles played by private firms.

##### Appendix B: Sample construction for the news disclosure samples

This table presents the sample selection criteria for our news disclosure sample. The news disclosure data are obtained from Capital IQ's Key Developments database. To form a comprehensive list of U.S. firms, we collect all U.S. public and private firms with non-missing values for total assets in Capital IQ from 1996 to 2018 (see Gao et al. 2017). After requiring Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes to be available and excluding firms in the financial or utilities industries (i.e., firms with SIC codes between 6000 and 6999 or between 4900 and 4999), our initial sample includes 120,880 unique private firms headquartered in U.S. and 16,106 unique public firms. We then collect all news issued by these sampled public and private firms from 1996 to 2018, which include 283,841 news articles issued by 18,109 unique private firms and 2,080,347 news articles issued by 14,994 unique public firms. We further screen all press releases by retaining news articles sourced from a company's website or the press release distributors (e.g., PR Newswire, Business Wire, GlobeNewswire, etc.). Press releases initiated by firms, rather than news articles initiated by media, are the focus of our study. We then compute the number of press releases issued by private and public firms, respectively, in each 2-digit SIC industry and year, and merge them with the regression sample. Our final news disclosure samples include 180,944 press releases from 14,302 unique private firms and 1,348,315 press releases from 13,867 public firms in 63 2-digit SIC industries.
