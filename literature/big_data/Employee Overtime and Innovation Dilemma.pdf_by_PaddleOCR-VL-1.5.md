# Employee Overtime and Innovation Dilemma

Jingjing Yang $ ^{1,2} $ · Di Fan $ ^{3} $ · Caifu Li $ ^{4} $

Received: 2 February 2024 / Accepted: 21 December 2024 / Published online: 5 January 2025 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2025

## Abstract

We examine the influence of human resource (HR) slack, specifically that accrued through employee overtime, on firm innovation in China. Leveraging textual analysis to gauge overtime levels, our findings reveal that although overtime increases innovation output, it does not enhance its quality. These results remain consistent even in firms with research and development personnel slack or those offering higher salaries. Additionally, no significant differences are observed between state-owned and non-state-owned firms in terms of overtime's impact on innovation. Furthermore, the effect of employee overtime on innovation output is less pronounced in the manufacturing and labor-intensive industries. Our study suggests that governmental leniency towards corporate infringement of employee rights does not sufficiently boost firm competitiveness. This research contributes to the understanding of the economic implications of widespread employee overtime in emerging economies, offering valuable business ethics and policy insights for managers and regulators addressing the fundamental working hour practices.

Keywords Employee overtime · Human resource (HR) slack · Firm innovation · Employee rights

## Introduction

In the competitive landscape of the global economy, organizations often extend work hours beyond the standard schedule, a human resource (HR) practice known as overtime. This phenomenon is frequently regarded as an expedient solution to increasing productivity demands and the relentless pressure to meet deadlines. In this context, employee overtime can be considered a form of organizational slack, as it provides a flexible and immediate means to address temporary workload surges or unforeseen productivity demands. Bourgeois (1981, p. 33), in his seminal work, asserts that employees "work more overtime to remove bottlenecks" in workflow, thereby creating slack as a "technical buffer". Mishina et al. (2004) further refine the concept of HR slack, describing it as a specific type of organizational slack that refers to employees exceeding a firm's current business requirements. In this context, the appropriation of employees' personal time through various means surpasses the scope of a firm's routine business operations and lies outside the boundaries of legally mandated HR planning. Organizational slack refers to surplus resources that an organization can deploy to adapt to changes, seize opportunities, or respond to emergencies without significantly disrupting operations (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020; Cyert & March, 1963; Sharfman et al., 1988).

Since employee overtime creates HR slack, organizations often implicitly or explicitly implement it to maximize the use of their internal human resource (Ko & Choi, 2019). Despite internally accumulated slack, human resources, in general, can enhance a firm's ability to assimilate and generate new knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Smith et al., 2005). Thus, effectively utilizing HR slack is a crucial input into research and development (R&D) (Frantzen, 2000; Wang & Yu, 2023), which is essential for innovation (Fonseca et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2023), improving competitive advantages (Allen & Bunn, 2007), and enhancing firm

performance in the context of strategic change (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020).

Yet, obtaining HR slack via employee overtime has been criticized for violating business ethics principles. From the firm's perspective, this HR practice can be perceived as unfair and unethical, particularly if the overtime is not distributed evenly among employees (Easton & Goodale, 2005) or if it breaches local norms, industry regulations, and labor rights and laws (Locke et al., 2013; Simpson et al., 2021; Win et al., 2024). Consequently, firms may hesitate to encourage overtime working approaches.

From the individual perspective, excessive overtime can lead to burnout, stress, and negative impacts on employees' physical and mental health (Avgoustaki & Cañibano, 2020; Dembe, 2009; Golden & Wiens-Tuers, 2006; Hollman, 1980; Peccei, 2004). It becomes an ethical issue if employees feel coerced to work overtime out of fear of losing their job or facing other penalties (Hatton, 2020; Peng, 2020). This situation is often observed in emerging economies or in contexts with a surplus of job supply. Additionally, long working hours can disrupt work-life balance, affecting personal and family life (White et al., 2003). Such imbalanced work-life arrangements are neither sustainable nor conducive to enhancing worker productivity (Leslie, 1976) and creativity (Zhao & Wu, 2023).

Furthermore, scholars have identified a non-linear relationship between employee overtime and innovation outcomes (Nohria & Gulati, 1996). In the similar vein, Ko and Choi (2019), in an analysis of time-lagged data from 273 Korean firms, find that HR slack via employee overtime is curvilinearly (inverted U-shaped) related to innovation. Given these conflicting perspectives and inconsistent findings, a key research question arises: can employee overtime as HR slack promote or reduce firm innovation?

Embracing the organizational slack perspective, this study addresses this research question by constructing a one-year lagged and multiple sources matched panel dataset with 3934 Chinese list firms between 2009 and 2021. To capture firms' HR slack via employee overtime, we employ an innovative approach—textual analysis—to estimate overtime levels. Our empirical findings suggest, although overtime can increase innovation quantity, it does not improve innovation quality. Furthermore, the influence of employee overtime on innovation output is less pronounced in the manufacturing industry and labor-intensive firms. One of the key practical implications is that governmental leniency towards corporate infringement of employee rights does not adequately enhance firm competitiveness.

Based on these findings, the study endeavors to make three contributions to the literature. First, the literature has intensive debates on the effects of organizational slack on innovation. Nohria and Gulati (1996, p. 1245) call for future research on testing whether slack is “good or bad for innovation”. Our findings contribute to the realm of research and demonstrate the impact of HR slack (especially obtained from employee overtime) on innovation. This paper fills a gap in the literature by shedding light on the innovation outcomes of overtime work at the firm level. Second, the study provides timely economic evidence to support the common good HRM approach (Aust et al., 2020) that advocates the limited use of overtime in managing employees. Our findings enrich the ethical debate of humanistic management (Frémeaux & Michelson, 2017). Third, we construct a measure of HR slack (the degree of overtime work) at the firm level using textual analysis. Compared with the traditional approach (e.g. using headcounts or employees-to-sales ratio), the new approach enables deeper understanding on a context-sensitive social phenomenon (Bellstam et al., 2021). In contrast to the annual reports of listed firms, public media reports are generally considered more objective and comprehensive. The employee overtime insights from textual analysis are used to precisely represent the overtime features of listed firms and offer a reference for subsequent studies.

## Research Background

### Employee Overtime in China

Employee overtime has become a pivotal topic in HR management and business ethics, attracting significant attention due to its implications for worker well-being, organizational productivity, and labor rights. Overtime is typically defined as the working hours performed in addition to the standard (legislated) working hours during a day or week (Golden & Wiens-Tuers, 2006; Hollman, 1980). Katz and Krueger (2016) extend this definition by emphasizing the voluntary or compulsory nature of overtime, thereby reflecting the complex dynamics between economic necessity and ethical considerations.

Despite decades of globalization efforts aimed at harmonizing economic practices, both formal and informal institutions continue to shape the heterogeneity in workplace and workforce (Hall & Soskice, 2001). In many developed nations, there has been a noticeable reduction in working hours (Lautsch & Scully, 2007). This trend is linked to improvements in productivity that accompany industrial development or to efforts by labor movements (Takami, 2019). In OECD countries, the average number of hours worked per worker declined from 34.6 h per week (totaling 1799 h per year) in 2010 to 34.35 h per week (totaling 1752 h per year) by 2022. $ ^{1} $ Even in Japan and South Korea, where workaholism predominates, the average working

hours of workers have significantly decreased over the past decade, coinciding with a rapid increase in productivity. It is worth noting $ ^{2} $ that the reduction in employee working hours in these countries may also be influenced by other factors, such as a slowdown in productivity growth following the completion of industrial catch-up. Additionally, shifts in employee attitudes toward their investment in work, a trend observed globally, have played a role. The “sacrificed generation” has been succeeded by a generation more focused on achieving a balance between work and personal life in many regions (Twenge et al., 2010).

In contrast, prolonged working hours continue to be prevalent in emerging economies. In the last decade, a culture of overtime work has prevailed among Chinese firms, with some firms implementing a “996” working system in which employees may involuntarily work from 9:00 AM to 9:00 PM, six days a week. It is commonly believed that in China, individuals should be willing to prioritize collective interests over their own and make additional efforts for the benefit of the collective (Kang et al., 2017). In this context, working long hours for the firm’s interests (i.e., collective interests) has become a type of virtue (that is, moral merit). Moreover, overtime is not limited to entry-level employees; it encompasses most employees, including blue-collar workers, white-collar workers, administrators, managers, and professionals, who are subject to long working hours and high work intensity in China (Chen et al., 2020; Zhou, 2015).

The classification of employee overtime as a form of HR slack remains a subject of scholarly debate. In the literature, two key criteria—availability (Iyer & Miller, 2008) and boundary (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020)—are commonly employed to define HR slack. Specifically, HR slack requires the presence of excess employees within the organization (i.e., within the boundaries of business requirements) who are readily available for immediate redeployment (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020; Mishina et al., 2004). These criteria are designed to exclude instances of HR planning strategies, such as future staffing initiatives.

We propose an additional criterion for defining HR slack—perceived ownership. In Western-dominated literature, employees' off-job time (beyond regular working hours) is generally regarded as their personal time, safeguarded by established institutions that protect this basic human right. However, this perspective is not universally upheld. In many emerging markets, employers often fail to respect employees' personal time, with some assuming that off-job hours are implicitly included in labor contracts. This perceived "ownership" of employees' time manifests in the widespread disregard for overtime regulations in these economies. For instance, certain business leaders actively endorse excessive overtime as a workplace norm. A notable example is Jack Ma, founder of Alibaba, who publicly stated on April 12, 2019, $ ^{3} $ that: "...In my opinion, being able to work 996 is a great blessing. Many companies and people wish they had the opportunity to work 996 but don't. If you don't work 996 when you're young, when else will you be able to?" This unethical appropriation of employees' personal time may serve as a potential indicator of forced labor or modern slavery (Simpson et al., 2021; Win et al., 2024). Thus, we argue that employee overtime meets all three criteria for HR slack. Overtime represents a slack resource that is immediately available to the organization, remains within the boundaries of organizational demands, and involves a portion of employees' personal time that is improperly "owned" by the organization.

Given this context, employers in China often assert ownership over extra working hours, obtained through both ethical and unethical practices. Building on prior research (e.g., Bourgeois, 1981; Mishina et al., 2004), we conceptualize employee overtime as a form of HR slack. However, unlike conventional slack, which is typically seen as a positive resource for organizational adaptability, employee overtime more likely represents a negative signal of slack, highlighting its exploitative dimensions and potential harm to the workforce.

Accordingly, we contend that employee overtime should be recognized as a distinct form of HR slack. Overtime culture, as a means of obtaining HR slack and competitive advantages, is prevalent in domestic firms operating in China, both state-owned and private firms (Chan, 2015; Zheng & Qiu, 2023). This phenomenon suggests that China's government has been tolerating the overtime culture of domestic firms and has failed to strictly enforce labor laws. Mandatory overtime and the political underpinnings of the governmental regulation of working hours raise ethical issues (Dembe, 2009), yet there has been little research on the economic effect of overtime. The prominence of overtime work and the vast labor population make China an appropriate subject for studying overtime.

### Linking HR Slack with the Innovation Outcome

Employee overtime has been linked to various organizational outcomes, such as productivity. Longer working hours may initially lead to increased output; but, the law of diminishing returns quickly sets in (Kahneman & Krueger, 2006). Moderate levels of overtime are associated with increased productivity, as employees have more time to complete tasks.

(Brown & Smith, 2017). However, excessive overtime is found to yield diminished returns, leading to decreased productivity. Notably, the relationship between overtime and productivity is contingent on individual factors such as job complexity and task interdependence (Lee et al., 2020). Furthermore, organizations with high levels of overtime have lower employee retention rates, indicating that excessive overtime may contribute to turnover (Brown et al., 2018). High employee turnover usually has a negative impact on organizational performance and productivity due to the costs associated with recruiting, training, and the loss of experienced personnel (Mobley, 1982).

Despite extensive research on the relationship between organizational slack (e.g., financial slack, operational slack, and HR slack) and innovation performance, direct evidence linking HR slack obtained from employee overtime to firm innovation is limited. HR slack has the potential to generate unique internal resources, such as skilled personnel and ‘sufficient’ working time, which are fundamental sources of competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). Also, human slack is considered an endogenous input in R&D (Herold et al., 2006) and an essential element of innovation (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020; Kim & Rhee, 2022).

However, scholars also suggest that working overtime is a double-edged sword, with both positive and negative consequences for employee performance (Guo et al., 2021; Kang et al., 2017). On the one hand, working beyond regular hours allows employees to complete more tasks, potentially enhancing employee performance and the overall success of the firm (Miles & Goo, 2013; Molinsky & Miles, 2005; Ng & Feldman, 2008). On the other hand, increased overtime is also associated with higher levels of stress and burnout among employees, along with higher absenteeism rates (Johnson & Smith, 2016; Johnson et al., 2018). Therefore, due to moral deficits and a lack of a solid ethical foundation, the extent to which HR slack accumulated from employee overtime, particularly mandatory overtime, can sustainably generate positive economic returns, including high-quality innovative products, remains questionable.

In this study, we examine how HR slack accumulated through employee overtime affects firm innovation in the Chinese context. Patents are a direct outcome of innovation efforts and are frequently employed as indicators of firm innovation (Albert et al., 1991; Chi et al., 2019). In recent years, there has been a surge in patenting activity in China. There are two distinct characteristics of innovation in China compared with developed nations—low efficiency and low quality (Guan et al., 2009). On the one hand, R&D-patenting elasticities in China are lower than those in developed countries, implying low efficiency in R&D spending (Wu et al., 2022). On the other hand, improvements in the quality of patents have not kept pace with increases in the quantity of patents. Rather than pursuing higher-quality invention patents based on actual research results, some Chinese firms apply for low-quality patents, including a large number of utility model patents and invalid patents, to strengthen their strategic competitiveness and receive more government patent subsidies (Chang et al., 2023; Dang & Motohashi, 2015). According to data from the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), China registered the largest number of patent applications in 2023, yet its overall innovation performance ranked only 12th globally. $ ^{4} $ This evidence indicates that the innovation of Chinese firms is facing a dilemma of “emphasizing quantity over quality”.

## Hypothesis Development

## Employee Overtime and the Quantity of Innovation

The relationship between HR slack and innovation can be viewed as a special case of the relationship between organizational slack and firm performance (Herold et al., 2006). Embracing the organizational slack perspective, March and Simon (1958, p.187) explicitly address the slack-innovation linkage, and point out that "when an organization has slack money or manpower not committed to going programs, various specializations of function may arise with respect to commitment to new programs and program elaboration". The literature generally reaches a consensus on the processes and dynamics through which HR slack—excess human resources available to a firm—affects various organizational outcomes, particularly innovation and adaptability (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020; Kim & Rhee, 2022).

From the organizational slack perspective, HR slack via overtime can enhance a company's innovation outputs through several pathways. First, HR slack via overtime promotes knowledge sharing among employees and the enhancement of their job proficiency. Employee overtime not only offers more common working time and opportunities for interaction among employees, thereby strengthening their communication, knowledge sharing, and teamwork (Wu, 2013), but also provides ample working hours that allow employees to fully leverage their own knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs), which aids in boosting job effectiveness and spurring innovation (Ko & Choi, 2019).

Second, Kim and Rhee (2022) propose the slack-driven mechanism, which refers to the combination of slack with current resources or its diversion into other forms to create new opportunities. In this sense, employee overtime as HR slack acts on slack-driven searching, which can be regarded as a crucial input in R&D (Wang & Yu, 2023). Due to the nature of overtime, firms not only combine the ‘extra’ HR

slack with existing human resources (i.e., employees' routine working hours) but also optimize idle machinery or spare production capability to form slack-driven searching.

Third, in workplaces dominated by workaholism, employee overtime is often coerced, though not ethical (Hatton, 2020). In such situations, employees are likely to feel pressured to complete required tasks more efficiently in order to finish daily routines sooner. Consequently, employees may become more strategic in focusing on core activities, reducing multitasking, and pursuing advanced skill development, which, in turn, enhances efficiency in innovation that requires deep concentration (Ko & Choi, 2019). In summary, for these reasons, we propose the first hypothesis of this study:

Hypothesis 1 Employee overtime can significantly increase the quantity of firm innovation.

### Employee Overtime and the Quality of Innovation

Next, we shift our focus to the quality of innovation. Innovation quality refers to the impact of innovation output on the realization of innovation performance (Lahiri, 2010; Liu & Buck, 2007). The well-established organizational slack perspective posits that discretionary slack is positively related to high-quality and impactful innovation (Bourgeois, 1981; Lawson, 2001; Troilo et al., 2014). The underlying rationales can be explained at both the firm and employee levels. At the firm level, discretionary slack enables the creation of necessary conditions for innovativeness, including but not limited to transformative business routines, adaptive business processes, tolerance toward innovation failures, perceived sufficient resources, free information channels, and protected job security and employee voice (Chen & Huang, 2010; Du et al., 2022; Mishina et al., 2004; Nohria & Gulati, 1996; Sharfman et al., 1988). At the employee level, discretionary slack allows employees to cope with resource constraints, especially when time is the essential resource (Lawson, 2001). Also, discretionary slack reduces organizational infighting (Cheng & Kesner, 1997) and enables employees to devote their attention and efforts to specific R&D projects (Troilo et al., 2014).

Despite these well-received arguments, the key consideration is whether HR slack obtained via employee overtime can be regarded as discretionary in this study context. At the firm level, HR slack from employee overtime is often purposefully and strategically utilized, such as to fill short-term labor shortages (e.g., caused by absenteeism or strong market labor demand) and to handle product demand fluctuations (e.g., sudden increases in demand without hiring additional staff). In this sense, HR slack accumulated through employee overtime is typically planned in advance and is more likely to be used for routine business operations rather than as “unplanned slack” for discretionary goals.

Likewise, at the employee level, HR slack obtained from overtime may infringe upon employees' willingness and right to enjoy personal or family life, which is inherently unethical as previously mentioned (Avgoustaki & Cañibano, 2020; Hatton, 2020). While employee overtime increases the firm's HR slack, it may, as a tradeoff, reduce the time available for employees to acquire new skills through advanced training, thereby hindering the updating and deepening of their expertise. In other words, since the total amount of resources is fixed within a specific period, prolonged working hours can lead to employee self-depletion (Prem et al., 2016). Thus, we argue that HR slack accumulated through employee overtime is not discretionary at either the firm or employee level.

Compared with the quantity of innovation, the organizational slack perspective posits that the quality of innovation is more dependent on discretionary slack, such as the depth of expertise and an environment that nurtures advanced developmental practices and collaboration (Mishina et al., 2004; Nohria & Gulati, 1996). In this study context, HR slack obtained from employee overtime cannot be discretionarily utilized. Consequently, it violates the fundamental assumptions of the slack—quality innovation linkage.

In addition, employees who consistently work extended hours over prolonged periods are more likely to experience cognitive decline, heightened stress levels, and burnout, leading to reduced attention to detail and impaired decision-making abilities. These factors ultimately diminish the quality of their work (Sonnentag et al., 2010). Cognitive fatigue, a common consequence of overwork, impairs abstract thinking, which is essential for tackling complex problems and fostering creative solutions—both critical components of high-quality innovation. For instance, Sonnentag and Fritz (2015) emphasize that recovery time is vital for replenishing cognitive resources, and its absence, due to extended working hours, significantly degrades creative output. Also, Bakker and Demerouti's (2007) job demands-resources (JD-R) model illustrates how excessive job demands, such as long hours, negatively impact employee well-being and performance quality. Prolonged exposure to high demands without adequate recovery not only erodes mental and physical health but also hampers the capacity for sustained innovative contributions. Therefore, while overtime may enhance the quantity of a firm's innovation by leveraging HR slack, its impact on the quality of innovation remains ambiguous, as it often compromises the conditions necessary for creativity and high-level problem-solving. We propose the second hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 2 HR slack via employee overtime is not associated with improvements in the quality of firm innovation.

## Research Methods

### Sample Selection and Research Design

We select China's A-share listed firms from 2009 to 2021 as our research sample and exclude special treatment (ST) and particular treatment (PT) firms and those with missing data. Data for the variable representing employee overtime are derived from media reports using textual analysis. Media reports are obtained from the China Economic and Financial Research Database (CSMAR). The CSMAR encompasses all national and major local media outlets, including internet-based media as well as traditional newspapers and magazines. Data on R&D expenditures, patent applications, and patent citations are obtained from the China Research Data Service Platform (CNRDS). Other data are collected from the CSMAR. All continuous variables are winsorized at the top and bottom one percentile. Our final sample consists of 31,726 firm-year observations from 3934 listed firms.

To test the impact of overtime on firm innovation, we construct the following regression model:

 $$ \mathrm{Innovation}_{i,t+1}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\mathrm{Overtime}_{i,t}+\sum\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Controls}_{i,t}+\mathrm{Year}+\mathrm{Industry}+\varepsilon_{i,t} $$ 

where Innovation $ _{i,t+1} $ represents the innovation of the listed company i in year  $ t+1 $. $ ^{5} $

### Variables and Measures

Dependent variable—Innovation. The study employs two innovation measures, namely, the quantity of innovation output, and the quality of innovation output. Using patent-based metrics as proxies for innovation output is a standard practice in the literature (e.g., Cornaggia et al., 2015; Lai et al., 2023; Li et al., 2021; Shu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). According to the China National Intellectual Property Administration, there are three categories of patents in China: invention patents, utility patents, and design patents. Invention patents are the most innovative type of patents granted for new technical solutions related to products, processes, or improvements. Utility patents are granted for new and practical technical solutions related to the shape or structure of the product. Design patents are granted for new designs related to a product's shape, pattern, or color. We first employ the total number of patent applications (Pat_App) in three categories to gauge the quantity of innovation output. Among the three categories of patents, invention patents usually involve a higher technological intensity and are deemed the most novel among the three types of patents in China (Jia et al., 2019; Ke & Wang, 2021; Wang et al.,

2021). Therefore, we also use the number of invention patent applications (Inv_App) as alternative measures of the quantity of innovation output.

There are several frequently used measures for patent quality, including the number of patent citations, the patent's originality, and the patent's generality (Alon et al., 2018). Thus, we use patent knowledge breadth (Pat_Knowledge) and patent citation (Pat_Citation) to measure the quality of innovation output. Pat_Knowledge is calculated based on the breadth of patent knowledge (Akcigit et al., 2016; Li et al., 2021), as high-quality or radical innovation typically involves a broader search that extends beyond a firm's existing knowledge base (Harrison et al., 2024). As the format of the international patent classification is “Section-Main Class-Subclass-Main Group-Subgroup”, we choose the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) at the level of patent classification main groups to calculate patent knowledge breadth with the following formula:

 $ Pat\_Knowledge_{i,t} = 1 + \sum_{Z_{i,t}} \left( \frac{Z_{i,m,t}}{Z_{i,t}} \right)^2 $.  $ Z_{(i, m, t)} $ represents the cumulative number of patents up to year t under the main group m for company i, while  $ Z_{(i, t)} $ is the cumulative number of patent applications submitted by company i up to year t under all main groups. The greater the value of Pat_Knowledge is, the larger the knowledge breadth of the company's patents is and the higher the quality of innovation is. Furthermore, the number of patent citations (Pat_Citation) reflects the value and influence of a patent and is the most used proxy to measure the quality of firm innovation (e.g., Hsu et al., 2014; Kong et al., 2022; Shu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). Pat_Citation is calculated as the natural logarithm of one plus the average number of citations received by all patents.

Overtime refers to the extent of a firm's employee overtime, measured by the natural logarithm of one plus the number of mentions of overtime-related keywords in media reports. Quantitatively measuring employee overtime is challenging because firms are not required to disclose information on employee working hours in most countries. However, media worldwide have focused on employee rights, providing a valuable data source. In China, the pervasive overtime culture in firms has garnered significant media attention, including reports on state-owned enterprises (SOEs) controlled by the central government enforcing overtime in recent years (Chan, 2015; Zheng & Qiu, 2023). This broad media coverage offers an opportunity to quantitatively measure employee overtime through textual analysis.

We employ a textual analysis approach to measure overtime. Based on the textual information of listed company media reports, we capture keywords related to “overtime” and construct the overtime variable based on keyword frequency. We select keywords related to overtime, including 996, single-off, $ ^{6} $ involution, $ ^{7} $ overtime, overwork, work overload, workaholic, work exceeding the time limit, extended working hours, occupy off-duty time, encroach on rest time, stay up late, all night, early morning, day and night, long working hours, weekend, high-intensity work, high work intensity, large workloads, rushed work, and excessive fatigue. The corresponding Chinese keywords are presented in Appendix 1. We exclude from our analysis expressions with negative prefixes such as “no”, “without”, “not”, etc., around the keywords. Through manual coding, we exclude texts that include related keywords but do not express the meaning of overtime. For instance, “996 shops” and “weekend weather”, despite the presence of the keywords “996” and “weekend”, are not related to employee overtime. $ ^{8} $ The higher the value of the overtime variable is, the more attention a given company’s employee overtime receives from the media, indicating that the company’s overtime situation is more severe.

Notably, some firms may attract more media attention than others. Accordingly, famous firms where employee overtime exists may be reported by the media more frequently. Therefore, we use another proxy to measure whether any given enterprise is reported at least once to leverage employee overtime. Details are reported in our robustness analysis section (Sect. "Robustness Checks") below.

The control variables include R&D input, company size (Size), return on total assets (Roa), cash flow (Cash), asset-liability ratio (Lev), proportion of fixed assets to total assets (Fixedassets), company age (Age), SOE, proportion of shares held by the largest shareholder (Largest), board size (Board) and staff salary. R&D expenditure is the primary determinant of innovation output (Chang et al., 2015), while firm size significantly influences innovation (Tindall et al., 2024). Firms with better operating performance and lower leverage have more internal resources to invest in innovation (Atanassov, 2013; Chang et al., 2015). ### Robustness Checks

### Addressing Endogeneity

There is potential endogeneity between overtime and innovation, as firms with more innovation may be more inclined to promote an overtime culture. To control for potential endogeneity, this study selects the brightness of urban nighttime lights as an instrumental variable and validates the baseline regression using the two-stage least squares (2SLS) method. Work is usually completed during the day, and hence, the brightness of nighttime lights can reflect the degree of overtime to some extent. However, the brightness of nighttime lights does not directly affect the innovative behavior of firms. Rather, this variable influences firm innovation through employee overtime. Thus, we use rectified images of nighttime light (DMSP-OLS) data to measure the brightness of nighttime lights. The brightness of the city's nighttime lights (DN) is obtained by dividing the total intensity of the nighttime lights by the number of grid cells in each city. Table 4 reports the results of the two-stage least squares regressions. The results of the regression in the first stage show a significant positive correlation between the brightness of urban nighttime lights and employee overtime. According to the estimation results in the second stage, after using the instrumental variable to alleviate endogeneity, the coefficients of the fitted value of overtime are significantly positive when the quantity of firm innovation is used as the dependent variable (i.e., Pat_App and Inv_App). However, the coefficients of overtime are insignificant when the quality of firm innovation is used as the dependent variable (i.e., Pat_Knowledge and Pat_Citation). These findings are consistent with the findings reported above and suggest that the effect of employee overtime on firm innovation outputs still holds after controlling for endogeneity.

#### Addressing the Sample Selection Bias Concerns

The empirical results reported in Table 3 are subject to sample selection bias, as our sample is not randomly selected. Therefore, we re-estimate the regressions of Eq. (1) using the propensity score matching (PSM) approach. First, the research samples are divided into two groups based on whether a company has been reported by the media to have employee overtime (overtime > 0) or not (overtime = 0). Firms with reports related to overtime are considered the treated group, and firms without reports related to overtime are treated as the matched sample of the control group. Second, the logit model is used to estimate the probability of employee overtime, and propensity scores are calculated. The variables used for prediction are control variables in the baseline regression model, controlling for year and industry fixed effects. #### Additional Robustness Tests

Considering that the proxies for the quantity of firm innovations are truncated variables with a lower limit of zero, we use the Tobit regression model to re-estimate Eq. (1). The regression results, shown in Appendix B1, indicate that the empirical results remain unchanged. Although we scrutinize the credibility of overtime data gleaned from textual analysis, there is still potential noise. This noise may come from the word frequency derived from media reports. Since we cannot control for the length of media reports in the textual analysis, some media outlets may repeatedly use keywords related to employee overtime in relatively long articles. We construct a dummy variable for employee overtime (Overtime_dummy) to confirm the robustness of the empirical results reported in Table 3. Overtime_dummy takes the value of 1 when any overtime-related keyword appears in the media reports of firm i for year t and 0 otherwise. The regression results shown in Appendix B2 still indicate that while overtime can increase the quantity of firm innovation, it fails to improve the quality of firm innovation. To address the omitted variable problem due to firm characteristics that are difficult to observe and that remain constant over time, we employ firm fixed effects instead of industry fixed effects in our regression model. Appendix B3 presents the regression results that control for firm fixed effects. The results are consistent with those reported previously.

Second, the quality of overtime work may be influenced by whether it is voluntary for employees. A competitive salary can help prevent employee complaints and foster a culture of voluntary overtime (Tan et al., 2023). In situations where employees receive proper compensation for overtime work, employee productivity and efficiency may be sustained. Furthermore, high-quality overtime work can enhance employees' job satisfaction and happiness (Beckers et al., 2008). Conversely, low-income employees have fewer resources, and working overtime often leads to resource depletion, making them more susceptible to the adverse effects of prolonged working hours (Choi et al., 2021). Therefore, we explore the moderating mechanisms of how overtime affects firm innovation from the perspective of employee compensation. We measure employee salary levels (Staff_salary) by calculating the ratio of the natural logarithm of total employee compensation to the total number of employees. As shown in Table 7, Columns (1) and (2) display the regression results with the dependent variables being the quantity of innovations (Pat_App and Pat_AppInv). The interaction term between overtime and salary levels (Overtime* Staff_salary) is significantly positive, indicating that the positive correlation between overtime and the number of firm innovations is influenced by the company's salary level. When employee salary levels are higher, the positive impact of overtime on the quantity of firm innovation is stronger. Columns (3) and (4) show regression results with dependent variables representing the quality of firm innovations (Pat_Knowledge and Pat_Cite). The interaction term between overtime and salary levels (Overtime*Staff_salary) remains insignificant, suggesting that even with relatively higher salary levels, overtime cannot produce higher-quality innovation outputs.

### Exploring the Industry-Level Interaction Effects

The labor-intensive industry provides considerable employment opportunities and contributes to economic growth globally. However, employee overtime in labor-intensive jobs is more likely to result in employee fatigue and thus diminishing productivity over time. The law of diminishing returns suggests that beyond a certain point, the productivity derived from an additional hour of work decreases as employees become mentally and physically exhausted, potentially leading to errors and accidents and counteracting the perceived productivity gains from extended working hours. Under these circumstances, the effect of employee overtime on firm innovation may be weaker in labor-intensive firms. Accordingly, we explore the moderating effect of labor intensity on the relationship between employee overtime and firm innovation. Labor intensity (Labor_intensity) is defined as the ratio of the total number of employees multiplied by 1000 to sales revenue. As shown in Table 9, the interaction term between employee overtime and Labor_intensity (Overtime*Labor_intensity) is negative and significant at the 1% level when patent applications and invention patent applications are used as dependent variables. This finding suggests that the effect of employee overtime on firm innovation is weaker in labor-intensive firms.

In 2020, China accounted for an impressive 35% of the world's total manufacturing output, and its share was greater than the combined output of the next nine largest manufacturers. $ ^{[10]} $ This highlights the significant role of China's industrial system globally and makes it compelling to explore the impact of overtime work on innovation in China's manufacturing firms. Manufacturing is also the industry with the highest concentration of innovation in the country. In recent years, China's government has aimed at promoting industrial transformation and upgrades and has encouraged commercial banks to provide significant capital to domestic manufacturing firms. However, China's manufacturing industry still exhibits the characteristic of being "big but not strong", with issues such as the absence of key core technologies (Huang, 2022). The industry's pattern of relying heavily on labor input has not truly changed. Overtime work is often adopted to promote output and reduce labor costs in the manufacturing industry (Zheng & Qiu, 2023). The severity of overtime in manufacturing is greater than that in most industries, especially in labor-intensive manufacturing firms, where overtime is more common. On the other hand, compared with nonmanufacturing industries such as education, information technology, leasing and business services, scientific research and technical services, culture, sports, and entertainment, manufacturing firms offer less flexible working hours. When employees cannot adjust their working hours, an increase in working hours can easily lead to employee fatigue. To examine whether the relationship between overtime and firm innovation is affected by the type of industry, this study designates whether the company is in the manufacturing industry (MF) as an additional moderating variable. MF takes the value of 1 if the company belongs to the manufacturing industry and 0 otherwise.

The regression results are shown in Table 10. The interaction term between overtime and manufacturing industry ( $ Overtime*MF $) is significantly negative, which indicates that the impact of overtime on firm innovation is stronger for nonmanufacturing firms than for manufacturing firms. In addition, the regression results suggest that employee overtime minimally improves the quantity or quality of innovation in manufacturing firms. Thus, to promote the industrial transformation and upgrading of the manufacturing industry, the regulatory bodies of China should focus on protecting employee rights by strengthening the enforcement of labor laws.

## Discussion

This study empirically analyzes the impact of accumulating HR slack via employee overtime on the quantity and quality of firm innovation. The results indicate that although overtime can promote the quantity of firm innovation, it does not enhance the quality of innovation. Further analysis reveals consistent results in firms with R&D personnel slack or those offering higher salaries. Notably, the impact of overtime on innovation does not significantly vary between state-owned firms and private firms. In addition, the effect of employee overtime on innovation output is less pronounced in the manufacturing and labor-intensive industries. These findings can contribute to both theories and practices, as discussed below.

A foundational assumption underpinning these arguments is that surplus human resources, when not occupied with urgent or routine tasks, can focus their attention on creative endeavors, such as generating new ideas and developing innovative products (March & Simon, 1958). Our findings partially align with this perspective, revealing a positive relationship between HR slack and organizational innovation performance. However, when distinguishing between the quantity and quality of innovation, our results show support only for the former. This differentiation provides a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between HR slack and innovation outcomes.

On one hand, the findings are consistent with prior research suggesting that extended working hours may enhance knowledge sharing and teamwork (Wu, 2013), allocate additional resources for slack-driven exploratory activities (Kim & Rhee, 2022), and improve innovation efficiency by fostering a task-oriented focus (Ko & Choi, 2019). These mechanisms underline how HR slack, particularly in the form of additional working hours, can boost innovation quantity. On the other hand, our findings reveal that not all types of HR slack support the quality of innovation. Specifically, HR slack generated through employee overtime fails to improve innovation quality. We argue that this is due to the ethical and discretionary nature of the slack itself. Employee overtime often lacks voluntary approval and support from employees, making it a coercive form of slack rather than a discretionary one. Furthermore, extended working hours frequently lead to cognitive fatigue, heightened stress levels, and burnout, which collectively undermine employees' ability to engage in abstract thinking, tackle complex problems, and generate creative solutions (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2015; Sonnentag et al., 2010).

To further examine this finding, we explored the interaction between employee overtime and R&D-specific HR slack (i.e., R&D personnel). Our supplementary analyses (Table 6) reveal that this interaction positively influences innovation quantity but does not enhance innovation quality. These results reinforce a key argument in the HR slack literature, that is, discretionary slack is more likely to foster high-quality, impactful innovation outcomes (e.g., Bourgeois, 1981; Lawson, 2001; Troilo et al., 2014). By distinguishing between the types and sources of HR slack, our study underscores the importance of ethical and sustainable practices in leveraging HR slack for corporate innovation. It also highlights the critical need to balance the quantity and quality dimensions of innovation, providing new insights for both academic research and managerial practice.

Second, our study contributes to the literature by exploring the business ethical implications of employee overtime and expanding the understanding of the relationship between common good HRM and firm innovation. Recent trends among HRM scholars have highlighted a shift from profit-driven HRM systems to those centered on the common good (Frémeaux & Michelson, 2017; Ren et al., 2023). The economy of common good (ECG) movement emphasizes employee well-being and job creation as integrated aims, particularly advocating for the limited use of overtime and extra hours in managing employees (Aust et al., 2020). Our findings provide timely economic evidence supporting the ECG movement's call to reduce employee overtime. It is challenging to derive a precise conclusion regarding the reduction of working hours through empirical analysis. Moreover, improving social governance and safeguarding citizens' rights is a lengthy process. At this stage, emerging countries, including China, should prioritize achieving or reinforcing the implementation of the legally mandated working hours. This can be accomplished through sustained efforts to enforce labor laws to gradually curtail excessive overtime.

While it is true that some employees work longer hours to meet sudden and non-routine demands, employee overtime in many developing and emerging economies has often become culturalized or institutionalized, as exemplified by the "996" work system in China (Zheng & Qiu, 2023). In such contexts, overtime is less likely to be driven by urgent job demands and more likely serves as a routine means of generating HR slack. However, our study suggests that this approach is both unethical and unsustainable. Although employee overtime may increase the quantity of innovation outputs, it does not enhance the quality of innovation. The primary reasons are: (1) HR slack obtained through employee overtime is not discretionary at the employee level; (2) it comes at the cost of employees' health, work-life balance, and well-being. Therefore, employee overtime violates the principles of common good HRM systems, rendering the outcomes of such unethical HR slack unsustainable in the long term.

Third, this study pioneers the use of textual analysis to construct a unique measure of HR slack, specifically through the degree of overtime work. Traditional measures of HR slack have often relied on quantitative indicators such as headcount or financial metrics (e.g., employees-to-sales ratio) (Bentley & Kehoe, 2020; Bourgeois, 1981), which can overlook the nuanced and dynamic nature of human resources within organizations. By utilizing textual analysis, this study captures a more granular and context-sensitive measure of HR slack, reflecting how overtime work is embedded in organizational practices and cultures, particularly in less explored contexts such as China. Furthermore, the use of textual analysis allows for a broader sample

of firms to be included in empirical research (Loughran & McDonald, 2016). Information on employee overtime is gleaned from China’s national and major local media reports, rather than focus on the annual reports of listed firms as a “self-reported” source.

This approach deepens our understanding of the relationship between HR slack and innovation performance, especially in differentiating between the sources and types of slack (cf., Bellstam et al., 2021). The innovative application of textual analysis opens new avenues for exploring various aspects of HR slack, providing a richer theoretical framework that accounts for the complexity of HRM across diverse organizational settings. Additionally, this method contributes to the broader literature by offering a replicable and adaptable tool for analyzing HR practices in different contexts, thereby enhancing the generalizability and applicability of research on HR slack and business ethics across industries and regions.

### Practical Implications

Our research offers valuable practical insights for both managers and policymakers. Our empirical findings provide crucial economic evidence that the widespread corporate culture of using employee overtime to create HR slack has minimal impact on generating breakthrough technologies. Entrepreneurs and managers who promote a “long working hours” culture should reconsider their HRM policies and practices. In both developed and emerging markets, over-reliance on excessive working hours not only fails to drive innovation and enhance a firm’s core competitiveness, particularly in labor-intensive industries, but also raises ethical concerns. Even when employees receive favorable compensation, overtime is unlikely to foster high-quality innovation. Instead, it can erode employees’ rights, breach business ethics, and contribute to broader social issues.

Our findings highlight critical policy implications for the Chinese government and global regulatory bodies, such as the International Labor Organization. While raising moral standards remains a long-term goal, it is more pressing and practical to strengthen legal enforcement to create a safer work environment for employees and ensure fair competition among firms. For example, Chapter 4 of the Labor Law of China stipulates that employees' daily working hours should not exceed eight hours, with an average weekly maximum of forty-four hours (NPC, 1995). The law allows for limited extensions under specific conditions, but these are tightly regulated to protect workers' health.

### Limitations and future research agenda

We identify several limitations in our research that raise additional questions to be investigated in future studies. First, the scope of our research, which is contextualized within China, inherently carries limitations regarding its generalizability (Chan, 2015; Zheng & Qiu, 2023). The findings may not be directly applicable to other national contexts, where cultural aspects, economic conditions, and regulatory environments can significantly vary (Hall & Soskice, 2001). Future studies can take a step to check the robustness of our findings in other countries. For instance, investigating the difference in the effect of overtime on innovation between countries with low and high-power distance is worthy of consideration.

Second, the insights drawn from listed firms may not extend to small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), given that these entities have distinctive organizing mechanisms and competitive pressures. Future research should endeavor to address these disparities by conducting a comparative study of SMEs to enhance the universality of our findings. Also, this study only centers on innovation performance, which may not be a major concern for different types of firms. Future research may also analyze the impact of overtime on other aspects of company operations, such as the cost of capital, and strategic outcomes (e.g., mergers and acquisitions).

Third, although this study addresses both the quantity and quality dimensions of innovation, it does not examine the economic consequences of the innovation dilemma arising from employee overtime. Innovation is widely regarded as a key driver of competitive advantage and corporate growth, which ultimately impacts firm valuation. Future research may refine the study design to further investigate the economic outcomes of HR slack achieved through employee overtime or other economically beneficial but ethically contentious corporate practices. Alternatively, additional analysis may explore whether the incremental innovations generated by overtime (at the expense of more radical innovation opportunities) translate into increased market value, such as higher stock prices.

Last but not least, the main limitation of this study arises from the inability to accurately differentiate the categories of employees through textual analysis. Thus, we were unable

to determine whether there is a difference in the impact of overtime work on firm innovation between executives and regular employees. Future research should further examine the effect of overtime work on firm innovation with respect to different categories of employees using survey methods. Meta-analysis is another suggested method that can be used to analyze employee overtime behavior.

## Conclusion

The study focuses on how employee overtime, as a means of accumulating HR slack, affects the quantity and quality of firm innovation. We find that although overtime increases the quantity of firm innovation, the quality of innovation does not improve. The results of the supplementary analysis show that the effect of overtime on innovation outputs is relatively weak in the manufacturing industry, yet there is no significant difference between state-owned firms and nonstate-owned firms. Therefore, our findings suggest that the value of firm innovation, gained at the expense of HR slack accumulated via employee overtime, is quite limited in China. An excessive focus on collective interests at the expense of individual interests may not contribute to the sustained progress of human civilization.
