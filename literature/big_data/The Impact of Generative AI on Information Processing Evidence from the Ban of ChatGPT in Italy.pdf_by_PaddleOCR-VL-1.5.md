## Abstract

January 26, 2025

This paper explores how the emergence of generative artificial intelligence is reshaping the information environment in capital markets. Leveraging an unexpected ban on ChatGPT in Italy, we examine its impact on the information processing capabilities of market participants. We employ metrics for AI-generated text detection to show that the ban coincides with decreased AI usage by domestic financial analysts and fewer earnings forecasts issued relative to foreign analysts covering the same firm. The negative effects are more pronounced among analysts whose pre-ban reports are more consistent with AI use or analysts with a technical background. The ban also diminishes forecast accuracy, increases reliance on industry-specific information, and reduces information efficiency. Furthermore, investor reactions to earnings announcements become more pronounced, and bid-ask spreads widen, reflecting lower market efficiency.

Keywords: AI, generative, ChatGPT, analyst, information processing, market efficiency.

## I. Introduction

The advent of generative AI has transformed access to computer-assisted productivity tools. Leading technologies, such as Google's Gemini and OpenAI's ChatGPT, have equipped individuals worldwide with unprecedented information processing capabilities. As Lawrence H. Summers, former US Treasury Secretary, stated on Bloomberg TV on December 9, 2022, "ChatGPT is a development on par with the printing press, electricity, and even the wheel and fire." Providing capital market participants with advanced information processing tools can have profound implications for the informational efficiency of the capital market (Sims, 2003; Li, 2008; Dong et al., 2016; Blankespoor et al., 2019; Blankespoor et al., 2020). Within the rational expectations framework, reducing the cost of accessing and processing information should enhance market efficiency (see Verrecchia, 1982, p. 1426).

This paper examines whether adopting new tools that reduce informational friction actually improves information flows and the capital market’s informational efficiency. However, the empirical challenge is that the adoption of generative AI varies in intensity and timing across firms and individuals, and utilization correlates with many other economic characteristics. To overcome this challenge, we leverage a setting in which, on March 31, 2023, Italian regulators banned ChatGPT within its geographic borders due to concerns about consent and personal data protection, and access to servers from IP addresses from Italy was blocked. After nearly a month of discussions and improvements, Italian authorities re-authorized the service on April 28, 2023. $ ^{1} $

The ban’s abrupt timing and geographic scope have allowed us to better control for trends in adopting related technologies correlated with generative AI use. More importantly, the ban took effect after ChatGPT was already widely used in Italy, but before other AI options were available.

We focus on the effect of the ban on the work of financial analysts for several reasons. First, analysts play a well-documented role in information intermediation and in shaping the capital market’s informational efficiency (Womack, 1996; Healy and Palepu, 2001; Bradshaw, 2011; Bradshaw et al., 2016; Kothari et al., 2016). $ ^{2} $ Analysts can use prompts to guide ChatGPT in efficiently preparing and analyzing information. Second, analysts’ job performance and outputs are empirically measurable, as they provide metrics that capture technological adoption and information processing activities (Li et al., 2024). Third, the adoption of ChatGPT is influenced by technological savviness, with financial analysts with technical backgrounds being more likely to adopt it (e.g., Bick et al., 2024). By collecting demographic information on individual analysts, such as education and location, we are able to assess the differential impacts of ChatGPT adoption across various groups.

We investigate analysts' company reports to explore whether the ban might have reduced their use of the tool. Since not all analysts use AI technologies, and some may have found workarounds, such as personal virtual private networks (VPNs), the ban was unlikely to impact everyone equally. Nevertheless, the linguistic cues within the reports may indicate the likelihood of ChatGPT usage. This is particularly relevant in our setting, where most domestic analysts are non-native English speakers writing in English. Thus, reports edited with ChatGPT (pre-ban) could differ from those

composed without ChatGPT (during the ban). In light of its focus on reports, this analysis represents an initial step toward measuring the adoption of new technologies among financial analysts, which is typically unobservable and rarely examined in prior studies.

To conduct our analysis, we rely on widely used text-based metrics to aid in AI-generated text detection (see Appendix B). To assess differences in ChatGPT usage between domestic and foreign analysts, we use analysts' office addresses and email domains to determine their locations. Reports authored by domestic analysts exhibit a lower likelihood of AI-generated content during the ban. However, after the restoration of ChatGPT, we do not observe significant differences in AI-generated content between domestic and foreign analysts. To refine our analysis, we collect data from resumes to identify analysts majoring in technical subjects (e.g., STEM majors) during undergraduate or postgraduate studies (Frank et al., 2023). These "tech analysts" are presumably more inclined to use new technology and are therefore more likely to be impacted by the ban. Indeed, the change in text-based metrics indicating AI use in the reports of domestic analysts was more pronounced among "tech analysts." Overall, these findings collectively indicate that the ban diminished financial analysts' use of AI.

Next, we examine how reduced ChatGPT usage affects the production of information by financial analysts. To do this, we compare domestic analysts' intermediation activities with those of foreign analysts covering the same firms. Our results show that during the ban, the number of forecasts by domestic analysts decreases by 21% of the sample standard deviation compared to those produced by foreign analysts, suggesting that the ban impairs the analysts' information intermediation. We also find that the reduction in information production was reversed in the months after ChatGPT was reactivated. We perform two cross-sectional analyses examining the ban's impact on different groups of analysts, differentiated by their educational background and

likely prior usage of ChatGPT. The observed decrease in the number of forecasts during the ban is predominantly driven by domestic analysts with a technical major or whose reports are more consistent with AI-generated content.
Enhancing analysts' processing capacity can increase both the quantity and quality of their forecasts. Documenting the significant impact of the ChatGPT ban on forecast accuracy suggests that analysts use ChatGPT for complex tasks, such as data input and prompt-based information processing, rather than merely for writing (Kim et al., 2024). We find consistent evidence that the ban reduced forecast accuracy, especially among analysts with high technological expertise or those for whom prior AI usage was detected.
We additionally analyze industry-level information in analyst reports, as this type of information typically has lower processing costs and is prioritized when information processing capacity is limited (Peng, 2005; Peng and Xiong, 2006). Using machine learning to identify industry-related keywords (Li et al., 2021), we measure the proportion of such information in the analyst reports. Our analysis shows a significant increase in reliance on industry-level information among domestic analysts during the ban, indicating that reduced processing capacity may lead to a greater focus on easily processed information.
We also examine how reduced activity by financial analysts affects the informational efficiency of capital markets (Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980; Verrecchia, 1982; Blankespoor et al., 2020). In this case, we anticipate that the ban would be found to have limited the analysts' information production, leading investors to depend more on public information from earnings announcements and thereby increasing their reaction to these announcements (Kyle, 1985; Back, 1992; Ball and Shivakumar, 2008). We find consistent evidence for this hypothesis.
Furthermore, our results show increased bid–ask spreads for Italian stocks during the ban,

which suggests that a widely used new technology, such as ChatGPT, may “level the playing field” by facilitating dissemination to investors with an informational disadvantage (Verrecchia, 1982; Kyle, 1985). Further analyses reveal that firms with lower analyst coverage exhibit a more pronounced increase in bid–ask spreads, which highlights the crucial role of analysts in information intermediation. Our results suggest that the ban led to higher information processing costs and, consequently, a less informationally efficient market.

Our research contributes to multiple strands of the literature. First, our work is tied to the recent studies in finance and accounting demonstrating the capacity of AI in extracting information from unstructured sources, such as determining the sentiment of news headlines (Lopez-Lira and Tang, 2023), predicting future earnings per share (Li et al., 2023), forming economic expectations based on news articles (Bybee, 2023), improving the readability of management disclosures and conference calls (Frankel et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2023), quantifying new information conveyed by managers in conference calls (Bai et al., 2023), measuring managers’ anticipated capital expenditure (Jha et al., 2023), and determining business complexity (Bernard et al., 2023). $ ^{3} $ Our study contributes to this stream of research by presenting timely evidence of the impact of generative AI technologies on the information processing capabilities of capital market participants. $ ^{4} $

Second, our study adds to the large body of work examining individual analyst characteristics (Womack, 1996; Healy and Palepu, 2001; Jegadeesh et al., 2004; Bradshaw, 2011; Bradshaw et al., 2016; Li et al., 2024). In this regard, we investigate the information processing capabilities of financial analysts, providing causal evidence of how modern information technologies, particularly generative AI, affect intermediation activities. By analyzing the content of analyst reports, we identify the use of generative AI technology and evaluate its impact on report characteristics, thereby shedding light on analysts' information production.

Third, our study contributes to the information processing literature reviewed by Blankespoor et al. (2020). Specifically, this body of theoretical work suggests that limited information processing capacity hampers market participants' ability to process a comprehensive set of information, leading to informationally inefficient asset prices (Sims, 2003; Peng, 2005). We add to this literature by introducing a novel setting, the ChatGPT ban in Italy, and testing the effect of processing costs on informational efficiency.

## II. Institutional Background and Conceptual Framework

### 2.1. Institutional Background of the ChatGPT Ban and Restoration in Italy

OpenAI is a non-profit laboratory founded in 2015 to promote AI development. The organization first released GPT-1 as open source on June 11, 2018, and GPT-2 followed on February 14, 2019 (Radford et al., 2018, 2019), to general acclaim. GPT-1 and GPT-2 were primarily designed as chatbots with capabilities for text generation rather than reliable productivity tools. On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT, built on the GPT-3 architecture, first to select beta testers and then to the general public on January 30, 2023. The increased size of GPT-3, approximately 100 times the size of GPT-2, made GPT-3 versatile for performing a wide range

of complex natural language tasks. GPT-4 was released on March 14, 2023, with about 1,000 times the number of parameters of GPT-3 and vastly improved capabilities to perform complex tasks (OpenAI, 2023).

By December 4, 2022, ChatGPT had already attracted over one million users. By January 2023, it had reached over 100 million users, becoming the fastest-growing consumer application ever. $ ^{5} $ A March 2023 survey by Pew Research found that 14% of American adults had used ChatGPT, which rose to 18% by July. In addition, the propensity to use ChatGPT increased with the user's level of education. $ ^{6} $ A global survey published by US trading platform eToro on July 11, 2023, revealed that 41% of retail investors from developed countries—including the UK, US, Germany, France, Australia, Italy, and Spain—are open to or already using AI to aid in their investment decisions. $ ^{7} $

On March 20, 2023, OpenAI paused the service for four days after discovering a security vulnerability, which allowed access to other users' chat histories and personal information over a nine-hour window. Although OpenAI patched the vulnerability shortly after the breach, the Italian data protection authority (Garante per la protezione dei dati personali) determined that OpenAI had violated EU privacy laws. On March 30, Italian regulators issued an initial decision (Registro dei provvedimenti n. 112) requiring a "temporary limitation of the processing of personal data of

data subjects established in the Italian territory” (translated from Italian). They clarified this initial decision in a statement dated March 31, noting that “it [OpenAI] will have to notify the Italian SA within 20 days of the measures implemented to comply with the order, [or] a fine of up to EUR 20 million EUR or 4% of worldwide sales may be imposed.” $ ^{8} $

The decision was largely unexpected. There were no enforcement actions because, under this threat, OpenAI quickly discontinued its service for IP addresses originating from Italy on March 31, 2023. The news received mixed reactions, with Deputy Prime Minister Matteo Salvini stating, "This is a work tool that many young people, many companies, many start-ups were using, and I hope that they can use it again as soon as possible, because otherwise Italy will have a gap compared to all other European countries" (Reuters, April 4, 2023). As OpenAI met with Italian regulators, it was widely expected that a reactivation would occur, but no timetable was set (AP News, "OpenAI to offer remedies to resolve Italy's ChatGPT ban," April 6, 2023).

On April 28, the service was reactivated after OpenAI introduced age verification to exclude users below 13 years of age and allow users to exercise their right to object to the training of models with their personal data (“ChatGPT: OpenAI reopens the platform in Italy, guaranteeing more transparency and more rights for European users and non-users,” press release, April 28, 2023). However, while the service has been uninterrupted since its reactivation, there are still concerns about the long-term regulation of AI in the European Union. $ ^{9} $

### 2.2. Conceptual Framework

#### 2.2.1 Information Production by Financial Analysts

An extensive amount of prior literature relies on noisy rational expectations theories (Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980; Verrecchia, 1982) to point out limits to the efficiency of capital markets. When acquiring information is costly, market prices cannot fully reflect the knowledge of well-informed investors, because investors must make trading profits to cover their information acquisition costs. Sims (2003, 2006) and Veldkamp (2006) have explored how individuals and institutions, constrained by their capacity to process information, fail to analyze publicly available information, resulting in systematic noise that can skew or delay information incorporation into prices. The theory explains why, when information production is costly, rational investors tend to access only a subset of available information, thereby affecting the informational efficiency of prices. This perspective has inspired a growing body of empirical studies on the economic consequences of information processing limitations, as viewed through the lens of rational inattention (e.g., Blankespoor et al., 2014; Dong et al., 2016; Blankespoor et al., 2020).

In this vein, Blankespoor et al. (2020) suggest that investing in technology may help mitigate information processing constraints. However, the costs typically associated with technology adoption imply that only a select group of agents reaps the benefits (Blankespoor et al., 2014). The introduction of generative AI, led by OpenAI's ChatGPT, may have altered these dynamics. Known for its contextual awareness and coherent conversations, ChatGPT quickly gained popularity due to its minimal adoption costs, user-friendly interface, and integration into popular platforms and apps.

How does the adoption of ChatGPT affect information processing? We rely on the ChatGPT ban in Italy, which disrupts the usage of the technology, to assess how an increase in processing

costs affects information flows in the capital market. We posit that the ban’s impact may have been most evident in the behavior of financial analysts. First, analysts play a well-documented role in facilitating information flow within the capital market. They process macro- and micro-level data, distilling complex information into reports that provide value-relevant insights. These reports aid both sophisticated and less sophisticated investors in making informed decisions. Second, although ChatGPT is a widely used application, its adoption has been uneven and often depends on users’ technological proficiency. Therefore, we assume that financial analysts with strong technical backgrounds would be more likely to embrace such innovative tools.

During the ban, domestic financial analysts faced greater information processing constraints than foreign analysts. This restriction likely decreased their ability to process information quickly and effectively, leading to reduced or lower-quality information production. ChatGPT can assist financial analysts by streamlining routine, non-research tasks, thereby freeing up more time for critical activities, such as conducting in-depth firm-specific research. Furthermore, ChatGPT potentially aids in financial statement analysis and earnings predictions (Chen et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024). Without the aid of ChatGPT, analysts may incur higher costs in processing vast amounts of information. Furthermore, analysts with limited time and resources may prioritize easier-to-process industry-level information, which may result in important firm-specific insights being overlooked (e.g., Peng, 2005; Peng and Xiong, 2006; Blankespoor et al., 2020).

#### 2.2.2 Informational Efficiency

Given the important role of analyst forecasts in enhancing informational efficiency (Womack, 1996; Healy and Palepu, 2001; Bradshaw, 2011; Bradshaw et al., 2016), the ban may have reduced informational efficiency by decreasing the frequency and quality of analyst forecasts. If the ban limited the provision of forward-looking information by analysts, less information would be

integrated into stock prices ahead of events. In markets where traders rely on analyst forecasts to inform their trading strategies, the absence of such information suggests that fewer market participants are informed before earnings announcements. Since private information is gradually incorporated into prices through trading (Kyle, 1985; Back, 1992), the lack of information flow from analysts could result in a more pronounced price reaction to earnings announcements.

The reduction in both the quantity and quality of analyst forecasts due to the ban may also have affected bid-ask spreads (Glosten and Milgrom, 1985). A priori, the impact of increased processing costs on bid-ask spreads hinges on whether the ban affected traders with less information, which would be consistent with theories of information leveling the playing field, or predominantly affected traders with more information, which would align with theories of information complementarity. $ ^{10} $

In the context of studying the practices of financial analysts, prior empirical studies generally support the “leveling the playing field” and show that analyst information production helps to reduce information asymmetry (Mikhail et al., 2007; Kelly and Ljungqvist, 2012; McLean et al., 2024). This is because analyst reports are widely accessible and provide readily usable analyses. Hence, reduced analyst information intermediation may increase information asymmetry among different groups of investors. While both large and small investors rely on analyst reports for informed trading, larger and more sophisticated investors may be less affected by the ban due to their access to extensive resources. In contrast, smaller and less sophisticated investors might lack the means to compensate for reduced analyst information production, leaving them relatively less

informed. This disparity could intensify information asymmetry in the market, leading to wider bid-ask spreads.

## III. The Impact of the ChatGPT Ban on Analyst Information Production

This section investigates whether the ban affects financial analysts' information production. We perform multiple tests on the impact of the ban on analysts. First, we employ commonly used techniques from computer science to detect AI-generated content in the analyst reports. This test helps address two questions: 1) whether the analysts use generative AI in drafting their reports, and 2) whether the usage of generative AI in the analyst reports is disrupted during the ban. Second, we assess the impact of the ban on domestic analysts' information production, including forecast frequency and accuracy. Finally, we test whether foreign analysts take up the slack, given the potential disruptions in information production.

### 3.1. Sample and Data Sources

We employ three datasets. First, we construct an analyst-level dataset that captures each analyst's information production through analyst reports. Second, we build a dataset at the firm-analyst level, which measures analyst forecast frequency and accuracy for each public firm. Third, we compile a firm-level dataset comprising firm-level outcomes, such as bid-ask spreads and trading volumes. Collectively, these datasets allow us to assess the impact of the ban on analysts' information production and the associated capital market consequences.

To build the first dataset, we obtain analyst reports on listed firms in Italy through Thomson One's Investext. For each analyst report, Investext provides details on the Document Date (when the report was written), Contributor (the brokerage firm's name), Author, Language, and Pages. In addition, we extract firm names and tickers, as well as analyst email addresses, physical addresses,

and phone numbers. We match the analysts' reports with the firms' information from Refinitiv Eikon using firm names and tickers.

To identify domestic (i.e., Italy-based) analysts, we use office addresses, email domains, or phone numbers. $ ^{11} $ Our final sample comprises 2,090 reports authored by 677 analysts, 169 of whom were based in Italy. $ ^{12} $ We manually search each analyst in our sample on LinkedIn using their names and the names of their affiliated brokerage houses. We gather data on analysts' educational backgrounds, work experience, and other characteristics. After matching data on analyst reports with LinkedIn profiles, we obtain a sample of 1,208 analyst-month observations involving 602 analysts affiliated with 54 brokerage houses. $ ^{13} $

We construct the second dataset to examine analyst forecast frequency and accuracy. We obtain data on analyst forecasts with a one-year forecast horizon from I/B/E/S. $ ^{14} $ After matching the I/B/E/S forecast data with LinkedIn data on analyst characteristics, we compile a sample of 2,084 analyst-firm-month observations, involving 155 analysts issuing forecasts for 107 firms, of whom 49 were domestic analysts. Table 1 provides the summary statistics for the analyst-firm-level sample of analyst forecasts. On average, each analyst issued 0.33 forecasts per month for Italian firms, and 30% of analysts issued at least one forecast monthly. The analysts in this sample

had an average of 11.4 years of experience, and 50.5% held technical degrees.

To construct the third firm-level dataset, we obtain stock returns, trading volumes, and ownership data from the Refinitiv Eikon and Datastream databases. This dataset includes firms from the European Union and the United Kingdom. We employ propensity score matching to create a group of control firms to alleviate concerns about the differences between Italian and non-Italian firms. We match each Italian firm to a foreign firm based on industry (classified by a two-digit NAICS code), market capitalization, and profitability (measured by return on assets (ROA)) without replacement. Market capitalization is calculated as of March 24, 2023, one week before the ban. For our firm-level analysis, we use a control sample of firms in the European Union (excluding Italy) and the United Kingdom, resulting in a sample of 228 Italian firms and 228 matched European firms. $ ^{15} $ Table 1 presents the summary statistics. To mitigate the influence of outliers, we winsorize all continuous variables at the first and 99th percentiles, excluding stock returns and unexpected earnings. The median firm in our sample has a market capitalization of EUR 315 million and an ROA of 0.05.

We define the ban period as March 31 (inclusive) to April 28 (inclusive), totaling 21 working days. For comparability, each monthly time unit in the pre-, during-, and post-ban periods included exactly 21 working days. The pre-ban period consists of three consecutive subperiods with 21 working days (January 2 to March 30), referred to as January, February, and March for simplicity. The post-ban period similarly consists of two consecutive 21-working-day subperiods (April 29 to June 27), referred to as May and June.

### 3.2. Evidence of the Likelihood of AI-Generated Content in Analyst Reports

We investigate whether the ban affects analysts' utilization of AI in writing their reports. We use two established metrics from the computer science literature that help with AI-generated text detection (AGTD): perplexity and burstiness. Perplexity and burstiness are broadly used in the natural language processing (NLP) literature as benchmark measures for AGTD (Mitchell et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Chakraborty et al., 2023). $ ^{16} $ A text displaying higher levels of perplexity and burstiness is more likely to be authored by a human.

One caveat is that more recent large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated the utilization of a wide range of vocabulary (OpenAI, 2023), which may potentially lower the detection power of perplexity- and burstiness-based AGTD algorithms. We conduct a simple experiment to alleviate this concern. $ ^{17} $ We find that perplexity and burstiness scores are significantly higher in human-only written reports compared to AI-polished and AI-only written reports. Specifically, perplexity and burstiness decrease by 14.2% and 25.5%, respectively, in AI-polished reports and by 16.7% and 35.0% in AI-only written reports, which supports the

effectiveness of AGTD measures in detecting AI-generated content.

Next, we utilize AGTD measures to identify ChatGPT’s footprint on analyst reports. In particular, we compute the perplexity and burstiness levels for each analyst report developed by professional analysts. Then, we create a composite score of perplexity and burstiness (Composite Score) by averaging the scores of the two measures. We aggregate the data into a panel at the analyst-month level to investigate how analysts’ utilization of AI changes from the pre-ban period to the ban period. Our final sample comprises 1,208 analyst-month observations, including 893 observations from the pre-ban months and 315 observations during the ban.

### 3.3. Impact of the ChatGPT Ban on Analysts' Forecast Frequency

Having documented preliminary evidence of analysts' use of ChatGPT, we explore ChatGPT's impact on information production. The empirical challenge is that the ban may have influenced both analysts' information processing capabilities and capital market participants' expectations of fundamental firm changes. As a result, observed changes in analysts' behavior could have stemmed from changes in information processing costs or firm fundamentals.

To address this challenge, we employ an empirical strategy that involves conducting comparisons within the set of analysts covering the same Italian firm. This research design enables controlling for variations in firm fundamentals. Consequently, any documented effect should have come from the ban’s impact on analysts’ information processing capacities. Both channels are consistent with a reduction in the information available to investors.

variable, Ex-Ante Prob (AI-Generated Text), which is set to one if the analyst's reports in the pre-ban period were more likely to be written by AI (i.e., the composite score of perplexity and burstiness (Composite Score) was lower than the sample median). Second, we use Tech Major, which is based on whether analysts majored in technical subjects. Table 4 presents our findings. The decline in information intermediation during the ban was concentrated among domestic analysts with a technical degree or with a higher chance of using ChatGPT to prepare reports in the pre-ban period. $ ^{20} $

### 3.4. Do Foreign Analysts Take Up the Slack?

Given analysts' reduced information production, a crucial empirical question is whether foreign analysts compensate to fill the gap in the information environment. A substitution effect would imply that the provision of information by domestic (foreign) analysts decreases (increased) during the ban. $ ^{21} $ To examine this question empirically, we revisit the provision of information by domestic and foreign analysts during the ban, which is shown in Table 5. We estimate the effect of the ban on the number of forecasts by domestic analysts in Column 2, the corresponding effect on foreign analysts in Column 5, and the combined amount of forecasts by domestic and foreign analysts in Column 1. Columns 3 and 4 (6 and 7) further partition the domestic (foreign) analysts by the ex-ante probabilities of AI-generated content in their reports before the ban and their educational backgrounds (i.e., technical analysts).

We note several findings. First, we find a significant decrease in the number of forecasts by domestic analysts (Column 2), and the effect was stronger for analysts with a higher propensity to use ChatGPT (Columns 3 and 4). Second, we do not find any significant changes in information production by foreign analysts from the period before the ban to during the ban (Column 5), and there are no significant differential impacts across foreign analysts with different propensities for using ChatGPT (Columns 6 and 7). Finally, turning to the total number of forecasts by domestic and foreign analysts for Italian firms in Column 1, our evidence suggests that net information production was likely to have decreased, though the coefficient is statistically insignificant, potentially due to noise and testing power. Overall, unless there was an unrelated decrease in the propensity to issue forecasts by foreign analysts during the ban, our evidence is inconsistent with foreign analysts substituting for the reduced information provision by domestic analysts.

### 3.5. Impact of the ChatGPT Ban on Analysts' Forecast Accuracy

In the previous sections, we demonstrated that the ban reduces the quantity of forecasts produced by domestic analysts. However, informational efficiency depends not only on the quantity of information, but also on its quality. Although we present evidence in Table 2, suggesting that the analysts may have used ChatGPT to assist in drafting reports, greater use need not translate into higher-quality information provision, since the latter requires analysts to have the ability to productively employ AI to search and process value-relevant information.

We rely on forecast accuracy as a proxy for information intermediation quality. If the analysts had reached an advanced level of utilization, the ban may have adversely impacted this capability, thereby potentially reducing the precision of their forecasts. Therefore, we assess how forecast accuracy changes from before to during the ban for domestic versus foreign analysts using the following specifications:

 $$ \begin{aligned}Forecast Accuracy_{i,a,t}=&\beta_{l}Italy Analyst_{a}\times Ban_{t}+Controls+a_{t}+\gamma_{i,a}+\epsilon_{i,a,t},\end{aligned} $$ 

where  $ Forecast\ Accuracy_{i,a,t} $ is the absolute forecast error for analyst a covering firm i in month t, calculated as the absolute value of the difference between the actual and forecasted values of earnings per share deflated by the stock price two days before the earnings forecasts:

 $$ A F E_{i,a,t}=|\frac{A n a l y s t\;F o r e c a s t e d\;E P S_{i,a,t}-R e a l\;E P S_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-2}}|. $$ 

We control for a vector of lagged firm-level characteristics, which consists of the book-to-market ratio, idiosyncratic volatility, market capitalization, and stock return over the last month. We further include analyst-by-firm (i.e.,  $ \gamma_{i,a} $) and month fixed effects (i.e.,  $ \alpha_{t} $). $ ^{22} $ Standard errors are clustered by industry.

Table 6 reports the effects of the ban on analyst forecast accuracy. Column 1 shows a significant increase of 0.95% (8% of the sample standard deviation) in the absolute forecast error by domestic analysts compared to their foreign peers during the ban. We further interact the Italy × Ban term with two proxies for usage: Ex-Ante Prob (AI-Generated Text) and Tech Major in Columns 2 and 3, respectively. We find that the decline in forecast accuracy during the ban was more pronounced for domestic analysts with a technical degree or higher ex-ante utilization in the pre-ban period. Moreover, the negative coefficient of the two-way interaction term Ban × D suggests that AI-savvy foreign analysts improved their forecasting in the post-ban period, which could be due to the broader trend of increased use of ChatGPT and related technological tools over time. Collectively, our evidence indicates that the ban potentially diminished analysts' capacity

for information processing and impaired their ability to provide accurate information to capital market participants.

### 3.6. Industry/Macro-Level Information in Analyst Reports

Another approach to assessing the impact of the ban on the quality of analysts' information intermediation is to analyze the specific types of information the analysts discussed in their reports. This approach also helps to illuminate how the analysts utilized ChatGPT during the information processing stage, and how effectively it could be integrated into the analytical process. Thus, we examine the extent to which ChatGPT aided the analysts in processing industry- or macro-level information. We focus on this type of information because it is plausibly relatively less idiosyncratic, easier to process, and more frequently utilized when information acquisition is costly (Peng, 2005; Peng and Xiong, 2006).

Following Li et al. (2021), to identify the fraction of industry information discussed in analyst reports, we rely on the frequency of industry-related words and construct a variable named Industry Information. We select “industry,” “macroeconomy,” and “market” as seed words and employ the word embedding model, “word2vec,” to learn all similar words in the analyst reports. Then, we select a comprehensive industry-related word list based on the output of the word embedding model (see Appendix C). Based on this list, we define Industry Information for all reports issued by analyst a in month t as

 $$  Industry Information_{a,t}=\sum_{r=1}^{N_{a,t}}\frac{Industry Words_{r,a,t}}{Length_{r,a,t}}\ /N_{a,t}, $$ 

where Industry Words $ _{r,a,t} $ is the number of all industry-related words in report r issued by analyst

a in month t, and  $ Length_{r,a,t} $ is the number of words in report r by analyst a in month t.  $ N_{a,t} $ is the

number of reports issued by analyst a in month t. We multiply Industry Information by 100 to express it as a percentage. We follow the regression specification in Equation (1).

Table 7, Column 1, indicates a significant increase of 8.43% (relative to the sample mean) in Industry Information for domestic analysts compared to their foreign peers during the ban. The effect is stronger for domestic analysts with higher ex-ante ChatGPT utilization in the pre-ban period or a technical degree (Columns 2 and 3). $ ^{23} $ The negative coefficient of the  $ B_{an} \times D $ interaction term suggests that AI-savvy foreign analysts incorporated relatively more firm-specific information (or less industry-related information) in the ban period. This provides suggestive evidence of the enhanced information-gathering capacities facilitated by ChatGPT over time. Together with the findings on forecast accuracy, our interpretation is that the ban discouraged analysts from effectively processing firm-specific information, leading them to rely more on industry- and macro-level information. This shift could have contributed to lower forecast accuracy if industry-level information was coarser than firm-specific information.

## IV. The ChatGPT Ban and the Information Environment

This section investigates the ban’s effect on the information environment. First, we study the market response to earnings announcements during the ban to shed light on investors’ overall reaction to public information. Second, we examine bid-ask spreads to assess whether the ban harms investors’ information processing capabilities (especially smaller investors) and increases information asymmetry.

### 4.1. Impact of the Ban on Market Reactions to Earnings Announcements

As discussed above, if the ban limits analysts’ provisions of forward-looking information, less of this information would be integrated into stock prices before the earnings announcements. Hence, the market reaction to earnings announcements would likely be stronger, as earnings news is largely new to the market (Ball and Shivakumar, 2008). $ ^{24} $

Following Livnat and Mendenhall (2006) and Mian and Sankaraguruswamy (2012), we calculate the unexpected earnings (UE), which represent the unexpected part of the earnings announcement, as follows:

 $$ UE_{i,t}=\frac{Earnings_{i,t}-Earnings_{i,t-l}}{P_{i,t}}, $$ 

where  $ Earnings_{i,t} $ is the EPS of firm i in year t, and  $ P_{i,t} $ is the stock price two days before the earnings announcement date in year t (to avoid a contamination of announcements on prices). We use the prior year's earnings ( $ Earnings_{i,t-1} $) as investors' expected earnings for year t. For robustness, we follow Chiang et al. (2019) in calculating robust earnings surprises and find that our results are qualitatively unaffected. $ ^{25} $

We then measured the stock market’s reaction to earnings announcements by regressing stock returns on unexpected earnings:

 $$ Ret_{i,t}=\beta_{1}Italy_{i}\times Ban_{t}\times UE_{i,t}+\beta_{2}Italy_{i}\times Ban_{t}+\beta_{3}Italy_{i}\times UE_{i,t}+\beta_{4}Ban_{t}\times UE_{i,t}+\beta_{5}UE_{i,t}+ $$ 

 $$ \beta_{6}Italy_{i}+\beta_{7}Controls\times UE_{i,t}+Controls+\alpha_{t}+\gamma_{j}+\epsilon_{i,t}, $$ 

where  $ Ret_{i,t} $ is the cumulative abnormal return for firm i in four different time windows: one, three, five, or seven days centered around the earnings announcement dates. $ ^{26} $ Following Gipper et al. (2020), we included a set of firm-level controls: Loss, Size, Market-to-book, Leverage, Earnings Persistence, and Beta. We further controlled for industry (i.e.,  $ \gamma_{j} $) and month fixed effects (i.e.,  $ \alpha_{t} $), as well as the interaction between the control variables and UE, and clustered standard errors by industry. We are unable to include firm fixed effects due to the small number of observations (192).

Table 8 shows that the market reaction to earnings announcements is stronger for domestic firms than for comparable foreign firms during the ban period compared to the pre-ban period. With a one-standard-deviation (0.045) increase in unexpected earnings, Italian firms experience an additional positive market-adjusted return of 3.73 percentage points during the ban relative to their peers on the earnings announcement day. The additional cumulative abnormal return is 2.51, 2.75, and 3.72 percentage points for the three-day, five-day, and seven-day windows around the announcement dates, respectively.

We shift our focus to other coefficients in Table 8. The main effect (UE) is positive and statistically significant. The economic magnitude is also broadly consistent with the findings in the prior literature. The estimated coefficient of  $ B_{an} \times UE $ is significantly negative, which captures

the change in market responsiveness to unexpected earnings for non-Italian firms between the pre-and during-ban periods. The negative coefficient suggests that earnings contain less news content over time (i.e., when comparing the ban period with earlier periods) in countries with ChatGPT due to the faster incorporation of the firms' information into prices before the earnings announcement. The coefficient of  $ Italy \times UE $ is negative and significant, implying that, relative to other countries, domestic firms experience weaker market reactions to earnings announcements in the pre-ban period. Finally, the coefficient of  $ Italy \times Ban $ is significantly positive, indicating that domestic firms experienced more positive returns during the earnings announcement windows compared with firms from foreign countries in the pre-ban period. Overall, our results suggest that the ban negatively affects investors' information processing prior to earnings announcements.

### 4.2. Impact of the ChatGPT Ban on Bid-Ask Spreads

Next, we examine the impact of the ChatGPT ban on bid-ask spreads by using a difference-in-differences design:

 $$ Bid-ask_{i,t}=\beta_{l}Italy_{i}\times Ban_{t}+Controls+\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{t}+\epsilon_{i,t}. $$ 

We focus on the bid–ask spreads and the bid–ask spreads adjusted for the trading volume. We control for firm and month fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the industry level. Table 9 shows that both the raw (Column 1) and adjusted (Column 2) bid–ask spreads increased significantly by about 0.03, which is about 1.5 times the sample standard deviation. $ ^{27} $ This result is consistent with the ban hindering information processing and increasing information asymmetry across investors.

We also conduct several cross-sectional tests on the adjusted bid-ask spreads (Table 10) to shed light on the mechanisms driving the increased information asymmetry. Firms with limited analyst coverage (Column 1) are more prone to information processing constraints and experience decreased liquidity during the ban. Firms with higher foreign ownership (Column 2) exhibit a smaller increase in information asymmetry, because the ban applies only to Italian geographic boundaries and does not directly affect foreign investors. Consistent with smaller and unsophisticated investors relying more on technology to parse through financial information, firms with higher institutional ownership (High Institution, Column 3) tend to exhibit a smaller increase in bid-ask spreads. Overall, our results point to the role of generative AI as a productivity tool that facilitates investors' information processing.

## V. Additional Analyses

### 5.1. Dynamic Treatment Effects of the ChatGPT Ban

We estimated the dynamic treatment effects of the ban on major outcomes from periods before the ban to periods after the restoration. Our tests serve two main purposes. First, we assess the parallel trend assumptions by examining the pre-trends for treated and control analysts or firms in the periods leading up to the ChatGPT ban. Second, examining the periods after the restoration of ChatGPT allows us to assess whether the treatment effect reverses after the ban is lifted.

We extend our sample to June 2023 and estimate the dynamic treatment effects for three sets of major outcomes. First, we examine the three different measures of the probability of AI-generated content in analyst reports. Empirically, we estimate the following specification:

Text Char•a,t =  $ \beta_s \sum_{s=-3 \sim +2} \text{Italy Analyst}_a \times D_s(a,t) + \beta_3 \text{Italy Analyst}_a + \text{Controls} + \alpha_t + \gamma_n + \epsilon_{a,t} $, (8)

where Text Char•a,t is the score of AI-generated texts proxied by perplexity, burstiness, and a

composite score of perplexity and burstiness (Composite Score) for analyst a in month t.  $ D_{s(i,t)} $ is a set of indicator variables that equals one if the time period is s months away from April 2023. Our sample period ranges from three months before the ban (i.e., January 2023) to two months after the ban (i.e., June 2023), with January 2023 (s = -3) omitted and used as the benchmark period.

Figure 1 presents our results. We find no significant disparities in AI-generated content in the analysts' reports in the periods leading up to the ban, which is consistent with the parallel trends assumption. There is a spike in the probability of human-generated content for domestic analysts during the ban (due to the ChatGPT ban) compared to their international counterparts. After ChatGPT was restored, the differences between domestic and foreign analysts' AI-generated contents disappeared in the two months post-periods.

Second, given the evidence on AI-generated content in analyst reports, we study the dynamic treatment effect on analysts' forecast frequency. We plot the estimated coefficients in Figure 2. We find that domestic analysts decreased their forecast frequency during the ban period  $ (s = 0) $, and the effect reverses in the two months after ChatGPT was restored in Italy  $ (s = +1 \text{ to } +2) $. We note that the estimated coefficient for the first month after the restoration (i.e.,  $ s = +1 $) is positive but insignificant due to large standard errors. One possible explanation is that after ChatGPT was restored, the re-adoption among analysts is unbalanced, with some analysts being slow to return to prior routines due to the disruptions of the ban. This heterogeneity leads to greater standard errors in the estimates, making the coefficients statistically insignificant shortly after the reactivation. The reversal is statistically significant in the second month after the ban was lifted  $ (s = +2) $.

In addition to the effects on analyst information production, we plot the dynamic impact of the ban on information asymmetry among investors (i.e., bid–ask spreads) in Figure 3. We find no

significant disparities in bid–ask spreads (adjusted bid–ask spreads) across domestic and foreign firms during the periods leading up to the ban in Panel A (Panel B). There is a spike in the bid-ask spreads during the ban, consistent with greater information asymmetry among investors. After ChatGPT is restored, the differences in bid–ask spreads between Italy and matched control firms reverse back to zero over the subsequent two months.

## VI. Conclusion

In this study, we examine the effect of a major technological change on investor information processing by examining the ChatGPT ban in Italy in April 2023 and developing new insights into whether changes in processing affect information flows and asymmetric information. We demonstrate that the ban reduces the likelihood of usage by domestic analysts, as suggested by an increase in the perplexity and burstiness of their reports. The increase in perplexity and burstiness is more pronounced among analysts with technological backgrounds. Then, we show that a reduction in usage discourages domestic analysts from issuing forecasts compared with foreign analysts covering the same firm. The reports written by domestic analysts also exhibit declines in forecast accuracy and a greater reliance on industry- and macro-level information provision. These negative effects are more pronounced for analysts relying more on ChatGPT before the ban or with a background in technology. These findings collectively suggest that the ban reduces analysts' information processing capacities and, therefore, their function as information intermediaries.

Next, we analyze the consequences of the ban on the informational efficiency of the capital market. As a result of reduced analyst forecasts, investors increasingly depend on publicly announced earnings to assess share values. In this case, this shift is accompanied by a widening of bid-ask spreads following the ban, suggesting a decrease in market liquidity and an increase in

information asymmetry. The negative impact on capital market informational efficiency supports the meaningful role of generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT, in enhancing the information processing capacity of capital market participants.

Our results are also subject to several caveats. First, domestic analysts could circumvent such a ban by using proxy servers with foreign IP addresses. As far as we know, OpenAI does not have a policy to block IP addresses used by proxy servers, and sophisticated users, such as coding engineers, who likely benefited the most from ChatGPT’s coding capabilities, used proxy servers more extensively and recovered quickly within a few days of the ban being implemented (Kreitmeir and Raschky, 2023). In a counterfactual case, where the ban had been prolonged and extended to other countries, it is possible that a greater share of the user base would have switched to such services. ChatGPT users may also have switched to alternative services during the ban, especially considering that Google’s Bard was launched shortly before the ban. However, Bard was initially limited to selecting users on a waitlist in the US and the UK. It was made available to the general public on May 10 and launched in the European Union on July 13, after the ban.

Second, other important events may provide complementary evidence to better measure the economic consequences of AI. For example, OpenAI did not permit individuals to register in China, although it was not fully blocked and was compatible with Mandarin. Throughout February 2023, Chinese authorities took steps to restrict access by ordering social media platforms to remove workarounds and enforce stricter controls on VPN access. The banning of ChatGPT in China is a significant event that could further expand our understanding of the benefits of AI.

## Figure 1: Dynamic Treatment Effects of the ChatGPT Ban on AI-Generated Contents in Analyst Reports

We assess the dynamic treatment effects of the ChatGPT ban on analysts’ utilization of AI in drafting analyst reports. Specifically, we estimate the following specification:

 $$ Text Char\cdot_{a,t}=\beta_{s}\sum_{s=-3\sim+2}Italy Analyst_{a}*D_{s(a,t)}+\beta_{3}Italy Analyst_{a}+Controls+\alpha_{t}+\gamma_{n}+\epsilon_{a,t}, $$ 

where Text Char $ _{a,t} $ is the score of AI-generated texts proxied by perplexity, burstiness, and a composite score of perplexity and burstiness (Composite Score) for analyst a in month t. We report the results for perplexity, burstiness, and the Composite Score in Panels A, B, and C, respectively. Italy Analyst is a dummy variable indicating domestic analysts.  $ D_{s(i,t)} $ is a set of indicator variables that equals one if the time period was s months away from April 2023 (s = 0 when ChatGPT was banned), with January 2023 (s = -3) omitted and used as the benchmark. Control variables include a set of analysts' characteristics, including work experience, social connection, and education. We add brokerage-house and month fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the analyst level.

### Appendix B: Testing the Effectiveness of the Perplexity- and Burstiness-Based Algorithms

We conduct a laboratory experiment to test the effectiveness of the perplexity- and burstiness-based algorithms in detecting AI-generated texts. Approved by our institution's Institutional Review Board (IRB), the experiment takes place in a computer lab at a major research university, ensuring consistent technological and environmental conditions. We recruit 35 participants with backgrounds in accounting and finance through university email broadcasts targeting senior and graduate students in these departments. Eligibility requires the completion of at least two courses in financial analysis or related subjects to ensure content familiarity. Participants, most of whom are non-native English speakers, are instructed to write equity reports in English, which mirrors the scenario in which domestic analysts often produce such reports in a non-native language. We do not collect personal information, and each participant signs a consent form outlining the data usage policy, which ensures confidentiality and IRB compliance.

Each participant receives a packet containing the earnings call transcript, the 2022 performance statistics of Prada S.p.A. (PRDSY), a glossary of financial terms, and detailed instructions for the report requirements. They are required to write their reports on university-provided computers using standard text processing software. We provide the following instructions: "Please write a short report based on the earnings call transcript. Your report will answer two questions. First, what are Prada's 2022 performance and driving factors? You shall discuss at least three aspects: Prada products, Miu Miu products, and financial metrics, such as revenue, margin, etc. Second, you shall focus on the discussions about the Chinese market, including what happened in 2022 Q4 (COVID restrictions) and the outlook for 2023 (reopening)." These questions are open-ended yet specific enough to guide the analysis, while allowing for individual interpretation and insight. Reports are submitted electronically via a secure university platform that timestamped each submission to ensure adherence to deadlines. All reports are coded with participant IDs instead of names to maintain anonymity during the analysis phase. We utilize these human-written reports to conduct a verification test. First, we use ChatGPT 3.5 to polish the human-written reports. Second, we ask ChatGPT 3.5 to summarize the raw earnings call transcript using the following prompt: "You are a financial analyst. You will be given the Prada Group's earnings call transcript. Please summarize Prada's 2022 performance and key drivers. Please also summarize the discussions on China.
