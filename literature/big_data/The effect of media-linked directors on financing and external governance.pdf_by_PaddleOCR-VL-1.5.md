## A B S T R A C T

Firms sharing a board member with a media company receive more news coverage. This in turn affects those firms' financing choices: they issue more bonds, rely less on bank loans, and have lower blockholder ownership. These findings are consistent with media coverage acting as an external governance mechanism that substitutes for monitoring by banks and equity blockholders. The effect of media-linked directors on financing is evident in panel and time series analyses and using two different instrumental variable analyses, suggesting a causal relation.

### 1. Introduction

Corporate governance research has outlined effects of board members' expertise and networks on corporate finance policies and outcomes. $ ^{1} $ Studying the role of expertise, Gurun (2020) shows that members of boards of directors who also sit on the boards of major newspapers positively affect media coverage of their firms. $ ^{2} $ Media-linked directors, through their effect on news, could play a role in the financing of the firm, as news coverage has been shown to affect both firms' equity and debt. On the equity side, coverage "grabs" investors' attention and increases their awareness of a firm, and, consequently, positively influences the stock's attractiveness and reduces the firm's cost of capital. On the debt side, an active press has been

shown to be a monitoring force that decreases the cost of public bond finance. $ ^{3} $

Media coverage induced by media-linked board members could affect financing through at least two mechanisms. On the one hand, more and better media coverage could help firms access public equity and debt markets via greater visibility, which is key for equity investors, as noted above, and could be important for lenders too. Another possibility is that the increased information flow about the firm could serve as an external governance mechanism, $ ^{4} $ allowing firms to shift toward less governance-intensive forms of financing. On the debt side, a governance-pressure substitution effect could then impart a shift toward bond financing and away from more monitoring-intensive bank financing (Bharath and Hertzel, 2019). On the equity side, it could mean a lower fraction of the types of owners most likely to actively monitor the firm, such as blockholders. Such shifts would benefit the firm to the extent that monitoring costs are avoided while sufficient outside oversight is maintained. $ ^{5} $ Of course, both mechanisms, visibility and governance, could be at play. The two mechanisms share some predictions in common (e.g., for bond issues), but differ in others (e.g., for bank loans). Moreover, while the visibility idea has a long tradition in the finance literature, little is known about a media-financing link via increased governance.

In this paper, we study whether media-linked directors facilitate financing via news they generate, whether they have different effects on equity issues and ownership and on private versus public debt, and whether director-induced news substitutes for other governance mechanisms related to equity and debt financing.

Analyzing more than 32,000 firm-years over 2003–2017, we uncover extensive evidence that a governance effect is empirically important and dominates any visibility effect on a firm's financing mix. We find that the presence of media-linked directors has a strong effect on news flow about the firm, which in turn allows the firm to access financing sources that are relatively less governance-intensive. Firms with media-linked directors lean toward new financing in the public debt markets as opposed to bank debt, and lean toward fewer blockholders in the equity markets.

On the other hand, we do not uncover much evidence of links between media-linked directors and seasoned equity offerings. This could be due to the fact that other factors, including timing, stock price, pecking order, trade-off, manager-investor agreement, and other considerations, are known to be strong influences on the likelihood of seasoned equity offerings, $ ^{6} $ while news has been shown to affect mainly long-run returns in equity issues. $ ^{7} $ Further, from the point of view of a governance hypothesis, more equity does not necessarily mean more monitoring, as ownership structure matters also. In particular, it is ownership concentrated in the hands of equity blockholders that is generally thought to correspond to strong oversight. In any case, the evidence does not suggest that increased access to equity due to increased visibility is the main effect of media-linked directors on a board.

As a first step in our empirical analysis, we define a media-linked director as a board member who currently serves as a director of a firm in the media industry, broadly defined to include firms in print, video, and radio news. In doing this, we follow Gurun (2020), but also include non-newspaper media firms. We assess the connection between media-linked directors and financing outcomes using financing changes measured with data from a variety of sources, including SDC new issues data for stock and bond issuance, DealScan data for the establishment of new loans, Capital IQ data for changes in the debt mix on firms' balance sheets, and the Thomson Reuters 13F data for changes in blockholder ownership. We consider both dollar measures of the size of financing shifts, and also firm-year counts of financing changes.

On the debt side, our results show that one-year-lagged media-linked director presence is associated with (1) an increase in new bond financing, (2) a decrease of new secured loans being established, (3) a decrease in the balance sheet proportion of financing from bank loans overall (i.e., term loans and credit lines together) as well as from term loans specifically. $ ^{8} $ On the equity side, media-linked directors are associated with a decrease in the proportion of shares held by blockholders. The economic magnitude of these results is strong, with media-linked directors presence being associated with a change in financing that is from 5% to 41% of a standard deviation unit in absolute value. The economic impact is larger for measures of financing changes like bond issues and new loans that involve a specific decision, and smaller for measures like the balance sheet change in bank borrowing that could be incidental to the firm's overall balance sheet evolution. The common thread is a shift away from the most monitoring-intense forms of debt and equity—away from bank loans toward bonds, and away from equity blockholders.

As a second step in our empirical analysis, we study the channel through which media-linked directors could most obviously affect financing outcomes: news production about their firms. One key finding is that the presence of a media-linked director is positively associated with the number of news stories about the firm. For this analysis, we draw from the RavenPack data service, which covers all news stories and press releases reported by the Dow Jones Newswires, regional editions of the Wall Street Journal, Barron's, and MarketWatch for more than 40,000 companies around the world. RavenPack has previously been used in other studies to measure news production and slant (see Dang et al., 2015 among others). We exclude news that is related to equity and debt issues/changes so as to focus on broad news about the firm, rather than news related to financing events per se that could otherwise contaminate our measurement. We find that the number of news stories increases when a media-linked director is on the board, while the slant of news is only marginally

affected by the presence of a media-linked director. Our results are confirmatory of those in Gurun (2020), using different data and a broader definition of a media-linked director.

We also establish that the effect of media directors on financing is through a news coverage channel using two different techniques. First, we adapt instrumental variables (IV) regression methods to decompose the media-linked director effects into two parts: the effects of media-linked directors on news outcomes, and the effect of news outcomes driven by media-linked directors on financing changes. We also test with placebo versions of these regressions to ensure that we are not merely seeing the contaminating influences of cross-sectional regularities such as control variables or fixed effects. Second, we confirm and extend these results using a separate statistical method, path analysis, that is specifically designed to measure indirect effects of a driving variable (like our media-linked directors) on an outcome variable (like our financing change measures) via a "mediating variable" (such as news). The path analysis confirms that news is an important channel and reinforces the accuracy of the initial results.

Results like ours that rely on panel data are obviously suspect when it comes to claims of causality. We present several pieces of evidence suggesting a causal link from lagged media-linked directors to financing outcome changes. This evidence includes two separate instrumental variables investigations. Additionally, we provide Granger causality analyses of financing changes immediately subsequent to the appointment or departure of a media-linked director. We next briefly describe these analyses.

In the first instrumental variable analysis, we instrument media-linked directors with the distance from their firm's headquarter to the closest media firm. $ ^{9} $ In the second instrumental variable analysis, our instrument is an indicator variable as to whether the firm's CEO previously (before becoming CEO of the current firm) served on another board with a media-linked director. The idea behind the first instrument is that geographical proximity matters in the choice of hiring a new director (Knyazeva et al., 2013). The idea behind the second instrument is that a CEO who previously served on another board with a media-linked director would have an increased current likelihood of hiring this type of director, for at least one media-linked director is already in the network of such a CEO, and the CEO is already somewhat accustomed to working with directors with a media orientation. Both instruments have a reasonably strong first stage in statistical tests. Neither of these instruments should be correlated to financing outcomes other than through the media-linked director. The results of our instrumental variable analysis confirm our main findings.

Regarding the Granger causality analysis, we study arrivals and departures of media-linked directors on firms' boards to learn about the time series association with subsequent financing change events. In particular, we ask whether the arrivals or departures of media-linked directors Granger cause financing choices. For power and to avoid selection bias, we examine only firms with public market access in which a media-linked director is added or removed from the board at least once during the time of our sample. This restricts the sample size substantially, but we still find that arrivals and departures of media-linked directors Granger cause bond issuance, new loan taking, and blockholder changes. These findings are in line with the governance effects of media-linked directors.

Finally, we perform a variety of robustness checks and extensions. Among other things, we restrict the analysis to independent media-linked directors only, and we control for the presence of a banker on the board (Guner et al., 2008). Our results are largely unaffected.

Media-linked directors' ability to affect news coverage could have various sources, such as know-how about the way media works, ability to exploit their current position of power in a media company board, or having current or past media network connections. Therefore, as an additional step in our analysis, we try to understand if the effect of media-linked directors is more due to networking or to media experience. We analyze the importance of directors who were on the board of a media company before joining their current board, of directors previously employed as journalists, and of directors who previously worked in professions like public relations or investor relations. We find no significant effect of these types of directors on firm financing, suggesting that our results are driven more by the current networks of media-linked directors rather than by their accumulated expertise.

By establishing directors' influence on the news as a force in governance and financing outcomes, our results contribute to the literature on the role of corporate boards. Research often posits monitoring and advising as the mechanisms by which corporate boards influence corporate outcomes (Adams and Ferreira, 2007; Harris and Raviv, 2008). Manager expertise and experience are often suggested, especially as the basis for valuable advising outcomes (Fahlenbrach et al., 2010; Coles et al., 2012; Dass et al., 2014; Naveen et al., 2016; Gopalan et al., 2019). The importance of directors' backgrounds as they influence monitoring and outside governance outcomes is less often discussed. Thus, one contribution of our work is to show that board member background can be economically important for monitoring and outside governance, via an effect on financing.

We also contribute to the literature on media (which includes papers such as Engelberg and Parsons, 2011; Dougal et al., 2012; Liu and McConnell, 2013; Peress, 2014; Nguyen, 2015), and specifically on media as an external governance force (Dyck et al., 2010; Dyck et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2020). We contribute to this literature by showing

that the external governance force of media coverage that is driven by media-linked directors moves firms toward less governance-intensive form of financing.

### 2. Boards, media, visibility, governance, and financing

To study the effects on financing of media-linked directors via their influence on news coverage, we use a research design guided by two hypotheses. One hypothesis is rooted in ideas about company visibility and familiarity, and the other in ideas about governance and monitoring.

Most obviously, media coverage driven by media-linked directors could affect financing via enhanced visibility of the covered firms. Investors are not equally aware of or familiar with all securities (Merton, 1987; Huberman, 2001), and they buy shares that "grab the attention" of retail investors via increased media coverage (Barber and Odean, 2008). Kaniel and Parham (2017) show that retail investors disproportionately buy mutual funds that are highlighted in the Wall Street Journal Kings column feature. Similarly, Kaniel et al. (2007) and Solomon et al. (2014) find that media coverage of fund holdings affects fund flows. Institutional investors are also affected: Fang et al. (2014) show that mutual funds tend to buy highly covered stocks. Thus, if any increased media coverage related to media-linked directors has mainly a visibility effect on financing, we expect firms with such directors to be able to issue public equity more easily. The likelihood of an effect on public debt is less clear. However, as mutual funds are one of the most important buyers of corporate bonds and they are influenced by media coverage in their choice of stocks, the visibility reasoning might also apply for bonds.

In addition to the possibility of a visibility effect, we note the possibility of governance-related effects on financing. A firm's mix of financing implies, among other things, particular levels of monitoring intensity, capability, and effectiveness by providers of capital. For example, bank debt is considered more monitoring-intensive than bonds (Bharath and Hertzel, 2019), and blockholders monitor more than many other type of owners (Holderness and Sheehan, 1988). At the same time, external governance can substitute for internal mechanisms (Cremers et al., 2008; Giroud and Mueller, 2010; Misangyi and Acharya, 2014; Avedian et al., 2017); and media coverage has been shown to act as an external governance mechanism that could reduce the optimal amount of direct monitoring (Dyck and Zingales, 2002; 2004; Dyck, Volchkova and Zingales, 2008; Dyck, Morse and Zingales, 2010). Based on this logic, there could be a substitution between the monitoring intensity in a firm's financing mix and media coverage as a governance force. To the extent that media-linked directors influence news flow, there would then be a relation between directors and financing outcomes. We next consider what those connections would be.

Regarding equity financing, it is not clear that more equity per se is stronger governance; ownership structure also matters. In particular, concentrated ownership in the hands of equity blockholders is generally thought to correspond to stronger oversight and to minimal harm from principal-agent conflicts (e.g., Holderness and Sheehan, 1988; Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2001; Edmans, 2014; and many others). In contrast, extensive ownership by retail investors, index funds and quasi-indexers is thought to correspond to weaker oversight (e.g., Bushee, 1998; Bebchuk and Hurst, 2019; and many others). $ ^{10} $ Based on the nature of the implied monitoring, we would expect to observe a negative relation between a firm's reliance on blockholders in its equity financing and the presence of media-linked directors.

On the debt side, bank loans bring specialized and effective monitoring that has a corporate governance role (e.g., Ivashina et al., 2009). Even though a bank's higher priority in bankruptcy could attenuate its incentives somewhat, its special access to information dominates (Fama, 1985; James, 1987). Public bond financing arguably contributes least to monitoring, in that bonds have free-rider problems in monitoring, incentives for trustees to be passive, and coordination difficulties if distress occurs. $ ^{11} $ Based on the nature of the implied monitoring, we would expect firms with media-linked directors to rely more on bond financing and less on bank financing. Moreover, among the various forms of bank-provided financing, some likely impound more monitoring force than others. For example, with secured loans, banks obtain access to detailed information regarding the state of the collateral. With term loans, banks depend on the health of the borrower for a longer period than with revolving credit agreements that can be terminated fairly quickly. We might therefore expect to see especially prominent governance effects for these types of bank lending.

Summing up, we have two hypotheses as follow.

Visibility hypothesis: The presence of a media-linked director leads to increased use of forms of financing that benefit from increased visibility, i.e., public equity and possibly public bond issues. This hypothesis does not have strong predictions for bank debt or equity blockholders,

though it is possible that they could also be positively influenced by visibility. The effect of visibility and awareness for bank debt would be weak to the extent that banks' information relevant to lending comes from more direct sources than the news (loan applications, company documents, etc.). At least, the Visibility Hypothesis as it relates to bank debt does not predict a negative association of media-linked directors to bank debt usage.

Governance hypothesis: The presence of a media-linked director leads to less use of governance-intensive forms of financing. It positively affects public bond issues and negatively affects bank debt, especially certain types, such as secured debt and term loans. It also negatively affects the prevalence of blockholders. This hypothesis does not have any strong prediction for the quantity of equity financing per se.

The two hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, but while one prediction (the one regarding bond issues) is in the same direction for both hypotheses, other predictions (those regarding bank financing, equity issues, and the presence of blockholders) differ substantially. Based on these differences, it should be feasible to distinguish between the hypotheses empirically. To do so, we study several specific types of financing changes. They are listed below, along with the implication of a positive or negative association with media-linked director presence for the Visibility Hypothesis and the Governance Hypothesis.

• Public issues of bonds and notes: positive association reflects a decrease in monitoring intensity and also an increase in visibility.

- New secured bank loans: negative association reflects a decrease in monitoring intensity, while there is no strong prediction regarding visibility.

• Changes in the share of common equity owned by 5% blockholders: negative association reflects a decrease in monitoring, while there is no strong prediction regarding visibility.

• Seasoned equity offerings (SEOs): there is no strong prediction regarding governance, while a positive association reflects an increase in visibility.

• Changes in the balance sheet proportion of bank loans: a negative association reflects a decrease in monitoring intensity, while there is no strong prediction regarding visibility. $ ^{12} $

• Changes in the balance sheet proportion of term loans: negative association reflects a decrease in monitoring, while there is no strong prediction regarding visibility.

The reason we do not state any implication for seasoned equity issues under the Governance Hypothesis is that, as mentioned before, it is not clear that more equity ownership means stronger monitoring, considering that retail investors may not be strong monitors. Even if we focus on institutional investors, the issue is not clear, as passive institutions and indexers may not be strong monitors, and some evidence shows that only concentrated ownership is a strong governing force. Thus, we focus our attention on blockholders.

Finally, we note that a negative effect of media-linked directors on blockholders could also be interpreted in line with the Visibility Hypothesis if visibility is at work only for retail investors and institutional investors that are non-blockholders, thus reducing the overall fraction of blockholders. However, Fang et al. (2014) show that institutional investors are also affected by visibility, as mutual funds tend to trade high covered stocks.

As developed so far, the Governance and Visibility Hypotheses focus on changes in the quantities of various forms of financing employed by firms. For some forms of financing, we can also develop implications for the pricing of new financing. Concerning public debt issuance, we expect that at-issue yields would be smaller when a media-linked director is present if the Governance Hypothesis holds, for it is reasonable that the benefit of the resulting extra transparency applies to bond pricing as well as to issuance. The Governance Hypothesis predicts less reliance on bank loans, as a more governance-intensive form of financing, an effect opposite to the predicted effect on bonds. However, we would not expect the pricing of loans to worsen under the Governance Hypothesis: monitoring is easier, so pricing might even improve.

The prediction of the Visibility Hypothesis for bond pricing at issue time would also be for a lower yield. However, as discussed above, it is more questionable if bond investors are visibility-sensitive. The Visibility Hypothesis has no strong prediction for the pricing of loans. The clearest prediction of the Visibility Hypothesis is for the case of equity issuance. Enhanced visibility and familiarity would be expected to lead to better pricing for seasoned equity offerings, i.e., a less negative announcement effect.

Working from these hypotheses and the idea of a news link, our empirical investigation proceeds in three steps. In the first step, we establish via a panel data investigation that there is a strong statistical association between media-linked directors and changes toward less-monitoring-intensive financing. The tracks of the Visibility Hypothesis are less evident. In the second step, we establish that there is a plausible mechanism to drive the correlation between media-linked directors and financing shifts. Specifically, we show that the presence of media-linked directors predicts more news for their firms, and the news associated with directors is directly associated with financing changes. In the third step of our empirical investigation, we establish several reasons to believe that the media-linked director connection with less-monitoring-oriented financing is causal. We report on the set-up and results of each of these three steps in the three separate sections that follow.

## 3. Empirical method and results for Step 1: the association of media-linked directors with changes toward less monitoring-intense financing

In this section, we show the relations between media-linked directors presence and financing changes.

#### 3.1. Empirical method and data

We assess the statistical connection between the prior-year presence of a media-linked director and each particular type of financing change via a set of panel regressions. Conceptually, our core regression specification is:

Financing change =  $ a_{1} + b_{1} \times MLD + c_{1} \times Control $ Variables

where

• Financing change is the current firm-year observation on a specific type of financing change, measured appropriately for the estimation method used in the particular case;

-  $ a_{1} $ is an intercept term,  $ b_{1} $ is a scalar coefficient, and  $ c_{1} $ is a vector of coefficients;

• MLD is the one-year-lagged observation for an indicator variable set to one if a media-linked director is in a firm's board in a firm-year, and zero otherwise;

• Control variables are one-year-lagged observations for control variables as discussed below;

• Fixed effects refers to industry, year, and geography-specific fixed effects; and

•  $ \epsilon_{1} $ refers to an appropriate error term corresponding to the regression estimation method used (which could be Tobit, OLS, or Probit, as appropriate for the dependent variable, discussed below).

We next discuss the sampling process, data, and variable construction that we use to estimate the conceptual regressions from Eq. (1).

Measures of changes in financing methods. The dependent variables in our regressions are chosen to reflect changes in the financing mix the firm employs, with particular focus on the visibility and monitoring implications of each specific financing method. The specific financing changes we consider are as follow:

• Public issues of bonds and notes;

• New secured bank loans;

• Changes in the share of common equity owned by 5% blockholders;

• Seasoned equity offerings;

• Changes in the balance sheet proportion of financing from bank loans (terms loans and drawn credit lines together, which we refer to as “loans”); and

• Changes in the balance sheet proportion of financing from term loans alone.

For uniformity, we refer to all the above as “financing changes.” In this way of speaking, issues of bonds and shares are themselves financing changes; we are not referring to the year-over-year change in new issues, but to the new issues themselves. Our financing change measures therefore include both measures of gross changes (newly established financing) and net changes (changes in balance sheet levels of financing). Gross changes clearly reflect a firm's decision and a financing provider's agreement, whereas nonzero net changes could instead be incidental to the firm's overall balance sheet evolution, especially in the case of reductions, which could reflect scheduled repayments. On the other hand, gross changes could be offset by other decisions to an unmeasured degree. Because each type of measure has its strengths and weaknesses, we employ both types.

We measure these financing events both in terms of the dollar amount of the financing change for a particular firm in a particular year and, separately, in terms of occurrence-indicator (dummy) variables. For dollar-valued measures of new financing, such as public bond issues and SEOs, zero is an effective lower bound, i.e., there is no new issuance in the firm-year. Therefore, we use Tobit regressions for these dependent variables. For measures of the net balance sheet change in classes of financing and blockholder ownership proportion, both negative and positive values are possible, so we use ordinary least square regressions for these dependent variables. Occurrence is a 0–1 indicator variable for a given firm-year, so we use Probit regressions for such dependent variables. Even though the Tobit and Probit specifications are nonlinear in the standard way, we express the economic relations in a linear form in Eq. (1) for the reader's convenience. We winsorize all regressors at the 1% and 99% percent levels to moderate the influence of possible data-recording errors and extreme outliers. In reporting results, we provide standard heteroskedasticity and firm-level cluster robust standard errors.

We build our sample starting with all nonfinancial firms in Standard and Poor's Compustat over 2003 to 2017. We also require firms to have debt data available in Standard and Poor's Capital IQ, to be able to assess changes in bank versus public debt. We exclude firms that are in the media industry (which we define below) from our analysis sample. For data on public issues of securities—stocks, bonds, and notes—we turn to the Thomson-Reuters SDC Platinum New Issues database. For information on new bank loans and similar private market financings, we rely on the Loan Pricing Corporation DealScan database and the linking tables provided by Michael Roberts on his website (Chava and Roberts, 2008). For data on equity ownership, we turn to the Thomson-Reuters Institutional Holdings Database, a compilation of Form 13F ownership filings drawn from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission Edgar system. For information about the Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs) that underlie geographic fixed effects, we rely on the data provided by the Office for Policy Development of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development and the U.S. Census Bureau. $ ^{13} $

When intersected with the necessary board data described below, these data requirements result in a sample of 32,257 firm-years over 2003 to 2017. The gross financing change measures (i.e., new issues and borrowings) are on average much larger than the net measures (i.e., balance sheet changes): several percentage points versus less than one percentage point. Gross measures are naturally bounded below by zero, while net measures can be negative. Among the gross measures, equity issuance tends to be larger than bond issuance, on average, comprising somewhat above 8% of lagged firm assets, while debt issues comprise somewhat more than 5% on average. For new secured borrowing from a bank or syndicate, the firm-year average secured loan borrowed amounts to about 4% of lagged assets. Net measures are, on average, quite close to zero, but with a very large standard deviation, reflecting the fact that the increase or decrease might be large in a particular firm-year.

Media-linked directors. We first identify “media” firms in a two-step process. We begin with all firms in CRSP that have SIC codes equal to 4830, 4832, or 4833 (radio and television broadcasting stations), 2710–2711 (newspapers), or 2721 (periodicals). Then, we examine websites and related information about each of these firms to learn the nature of their business activities. We eliminate firms that make equipment connected to media but do not deal in news or information, and other firms whose business could connect to media but is clearly something else. $ ^{14} $ Finally, we add some firms that are listed in the cable network SIC industry (SIC 4840 and SIC 4841) but that also produce news and information. $ ^{15} $

Next, we turn to BoardEx for the list of directors of every media firm in every year since 2000. This list defines the potential pool for media-linked directors. For each firm-year in our sample, we designate a media-linked director indicator variable equal to one if there is a positive intersection between the firm's directors for a specific year and the pool of potential media-linked directors. This media-linked director indicator variable, which we often refer to in our tabled results as MLD, is our key regressor.

Descriptive statistics on media-linked directors, and also on some additional board characteristics that we use as control variables, are presented in Panel B of Table 2. Table 1 again reports the exact variable definitions. Almost 5% of firm-years report a board member with media background. The large majority of these media-linked directors are independent directors, as evidenced by the fact that 4.2% of firm-years report an independent media director. The boards in our sample seems broadly typical of U.S. public companies, having about nine board members, on average, (i.e.,  $ e^{2.192} \approx 9 $), of which about three-quarters are independents.

Control variables. Obviously, financing outcomes are driven by many influences, so additional covariates must be taken into account to obtain valid inference. Given that some of our key analyses involve debt finance, we include an indicator variable for the firm's credit rating. To allow for the possibility that a firm's current levels of overall

debt or bank debt influence its financing changes, we include regressors for the recent levels of bank debt and non-bank debt. Analogously, we also control for the fraction of blockholders. Panel C of Table 2 provides descriptive statistics for some dollar measures of the level of financing and also ratios involving financing levels that are used as control variables, with variable definitions again given in Table 1.

For additional firm-characteristic control variables, we draw on Standard and Poor's Compustat, the 1/B/E/S estimates system, and BoardEx. Profitability, firm-size, market-to-book, and leverage are well known predictors of debt outcomes (Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Graham et al., 2015, and many others). Some are standard in financing regressions, such as (log of) total assets, ROA, market-to-book ratio, and an indicator as to whether the firm has a bond rating. Some are intended as controls for the informational environment, considering the focus of our investigation. These include the (log of) firm age, advertising-to-assets ratio, and (log of) the number of analysts. $ ^{16} $ Panel D of Table 2 provides descriptive statistics for various firm characteristic control variables, with the precise definitions for these control variables appearing in Table 1.

Beyond these variables, we also include industry, year, and geography-specific fixed effects to account for otherwise unmodeled influences. The geography fixed effects, in particular, deserve a little discussion. Geography could be a common driver of media-linked directors and financing outcomes. Media firms are concentrated in population centers, making media-linked directors from such areas more plentifully available. $ ^{17} $ Some of those same population centers are also finance centers. Moreover, the finance centers have some variation as to their focus. New York is clearly the stock market center of the United States, but Chicago, Boston, Atlanta, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and other cities are highly prominent banking centers.

We address the geographical drivers' possibility with a design that assesses media-linked director effects net of the mean effects within geographic areas. To do so, we include indicator variables for the metropolitan statistical areas within which each firm's headquarters is located in our regression specifications from Eq. (1). To the extent that financing choices could cluster geographically due to differential financing access that correlates with differential media concentration, these indicator variables should absorb the mean effect in each location, preventing it from being reflected in the estimated regression coefficient relating to media directors.

#### 3.2. Empirical results

Table 3, Panel A, presents estimates of regression coefficients for Eq. (1). The key estimates in this table are in the first row, labelled as lagged media-linked director (MLD). All other estimates in the table are for regression coefficients on the control variables discussed above. Each column of Table 3, Panel A, applies the regression analysis to a different financing change dependent variable.

The focal result in Column (1) shows the relation between lagged media-linked director (MLD) and the issuance of bonds and notes in the public market. The key coefficient is positive, quite large, and statistically significant. The size of the coefficient, about 0.05, indicates that having a lagged media-linked director in place is associated with about a 5% increase in the proportion of lagged assets financed by new public bonds in an average firm-year, controlling for other factors.

The positive media-linked director (MLD) coefficient in Column (1) is consistent with both the Visibility and Governance Hypotheses. On the visibility side, however, one consideration is that the large majority of bond owners are insurance company and mutual fund institutional investors who have more direct sources of information about issuers. Considering this, it could be less likely that this increase in bond issuance is linked strongly to the Visibility Hypothesis. However, recent studies point out that professional investors are influenced by many of the same things as retail investors (see Fang et al., 2014). As discussed in a previous section, bonds are a less monitoring-intense form of finance than bank loans, so the Governance Hypothesis clearly finds support.

New bond issue amount is a gross measure of public debt financing change. In order to have a gross measure of financing change on the private debt side, Column (2) presents a regression in which new secured loans from banks is the dependent variable. As noted previously, among the various forms of bank-provided financing, some likely impound more monitoring forces than others. With secured loans, banks obtain access to detailed information regarding the state of collateral, for example. If lagged media-linked directors are associated with firms choosing or having easier access to debt finance in general, then the key coefficient estimate in this column would also show a positive association between new secured bank loans and lagged media-linked directors. This could be consistent with a visibility or familiarity effect on bank lenders, although, as indicated before, the logical case of a visibility effect on professional lenders may not be so strong. A negative estimated coefficient on lagged media-linked director in this column would indicate an effect opposite to what we observe for bonds, and so would not be consistent with debt becoming generally more chosen or available when a lagged media-linked director is present. A negative estimated coefficient would instead be consistent with a change toward less governance-intensive financing.

A negative effect is exactly what we observe in Column (2). The estimated coefficient on the lagged media-linked director regressor is about -0.06, with a t-statistic of about -2.1. The indication is that the presence of a media-linked director in the previous year is associated with about 6% less new secured bank loan borrowing in the next year as a percentage of lagged assets. The result is direct evidence in support of the idea of a change toward less governance-intensive financing, especially when combined with the in-

dependent result of an increase in bond finance (i.e., also a change toward less governance-intensive financing).

Column (3) shows the lagged media-linked director relation to changes in the percentage of shares owned by 5% blockholders. The key coefficient is negative and statistically significant, with a t-statistic of about -2.3. We have obtained similar results when we use a 1% cutoff to designate blockholders. A decrease in blockholdings is a clear indication of a move toward less governance-intensive finance. $ ^{18} $

Column (4) shows lagged media-linked directors' relation with equity issuance (SEOs) measured in dollar terms relative to lagged assets. The Governance Hypothesis has no strong prediction here, so it is a chance for clean support for the Visibility Hypothesis. The estimated coefficient is not statistically significant. Moreover, it is negative, whereas the hypothesis suggests that it should be positive. Our main takeaway from Column (4) is that the Visibility Hypothesis is not finding support.

The Governance Hypothesis has an ambiguous implication for equity issuance. On the one hand, more equity provides a stronger incentive to monitor, so the negative association of lagged media-linked directors with equity issues would be an indication of less monitoring. On the other hand, more equity can lead to more free-cash-flow agency problems and less monitoring. A non-finding on equity issuance could perhaps be expected if these two lines of reasoning are both operative to some extent, and perhaps differently for different firms.

#### 3.3. Pricing analyses

Up to now, we have always tracked the reaction to the presence of a media-linked director in terms of the quantity of a type of financing used. There is also the possibility of pricing effects. When a firm shifts toward a type of financing that reflects a more appropriate balance of monitoring from all sources, we might expect securities to be priced differently. In principle, the effect could apply to all classes of securities in the firm's financial structure, though we might expect to see the effect most clearly on new securities being issued; the market cannot avoid making a price determination for them.

Therefore, in this subsection, we consider our main question from a different point of view. As discussed previously, the Governance and Visibility Hypotheses also have some implications for the pricing of new financing deals. In particular, the Governance Hypothesis implies that pricing for public debt issues should be advantageous (from the firm's point of view) when a media-linked director is in place. More weakly, the Visibility Hypothesis would also suggest such improved pricing. In contrast, the Governance Hypothesis implies only that the pricing of loans should not be worse. The Visibility Hypothesis implies a pricing advantage on equity issuance when a media-linked director is in place.

## 4. Empirical method and results for Step 2: the association of media-linked directors with increased news outcomes

If there might be a smoking gun in the case that lagged media-linked directors are causally linked to financing changes via variations in the firm's information profile, it will be that these directors are related to the volume of news coverage (i.e., the quantity of news about the firm, which arguably contributes to transparency or visibility) and/or news slant (i.e., the prevalence of news coverage that conveys a tone favorable to the company). We therefore investigate whether media-linked directors' presence is predictive of the volume of news coverage relevant to a company and to news slant. Also, importantly, we are telling a two-step story. Under the hypotheses being investigated, the news volume or slant that is linked to the directors should also be statistically related to the financing: the directors' effect should be via the news.

In this section, we provide evidence from several investigations, all of which strongly suggest that this is the case. We use variations on two-step regressions to decompose the media-linked director effects into two parts: the effects of media-linked directors on news outcomes, and the effect of news outcomes on financing changes. We also use placebo versions of these regressions to ensure that we are not seeing the contaminating influences of cross-sectional regularities such as control variables or fixed effects. To provide confirmation of our interpretations, we apply a separate statistical method, path analysis, that is specifically designed to measure indirect effects of an initial variable (like our MLD) on an outcome variable (like our financing change measures) via a “mediating variable” (such as news or other direct effects of the MLD). Path analysis has been recently used to address a similar two-step situation in the finance literature (Pevzner et al., 2015).

### 4.1. Measuring news outcomes: the volume and slant of news coverage

The hypothesis of change toward less governance-intense financing is most obviously related to volume of news, rather than slant. The governance force of news is usually thought to be related to information flow, where the media act as watchdog (Dyck et al., 2008) and produces objective news about the firm, not just slanted news. The volume of news also seems most strongly relevant for the Visibility Hypothesis, though positive slant could also plausibly contribute to visibility. Studies find that slant is relevant to both investors and firms, so we study both volume and slant of news in our tests. $ ^{24} $

For data on news, we access RavenPack DowJones Edition, which covers all news stories and press releases reported by the Dow Jones Newswires, regional editions of the Wall Street Journal, Barron's, and MarketWatch for more than 40,000 companies around the world. The RavenPack data have been increasingly used in the finance and accounting literature as a source for news analytics (e.g., Dang et al., 2015; Twedt, 2016).

RavenPack applies a machine learning text analysis classification method to all articles in its covered universe. For every news story that mentions a company, RavenPack assigns a relevance score between 0 (low relevance) and 100 (high relevance) to indicate how strongly the firm is related to the underlying news story. To ensure that the firm is predominant in the news story, we follow prior studies (Dai et al., 2015; Dang et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2018; Drake et al., 2014) and focus on news stories with a relevance score of 100. $ ^{25} $ Because we want to consider only news that might be causal for financing, we exclude news that might obviously be caused by debt and equity financing. News stories that have a high relevance score for topics like equity issuance or repurchases and debt issuance are excluded from our counts. Subject to this filtering, our core measure of the volume of news is the firm-year count of news stories with firm-level relevance scores of 100. We will refer to this as the News count.

For a measure of the firm-specific slant of a news story, we follow Dang et al. (2015) in using a metric based on the RavenPack "Event Sentiment Score" (ESS). ESS is an indicator of tone ranging from 0 (most negative) to 100 (most positive) for any news story. ESS is the result of a proprietary machine learning algorithm that produces a score for each story based on its similarity, considering both text and any included numbers, to a training sample of other stories whose sentiment score has been set by "experts." The algorithm keys on sentiment associated with specific events, based on a catalog of more than 2000 types of events defined by RavenPack. As before, we retain all non-financing news stories with a relevance of 100. For a given firm-year, we compute a slant index as the proportion of all eligible stories that are positive out of all positive-leaning and negative-leaning stories about the firm. We will refer to this as the Slant index.

#### 4.2. First empirical method: a two-step regression system

The basic idea we wish to test is that media-linked directors could influence financing change outcomes via the intermediate step of news outcomes. By adapting standard instrumental variables econometrics, we can obtain estimates for both steps of this chain. Mechanically, we use our MLD indicator variable as an instrument for news in IV Tobit or IV linear regressions set-ups (depending on

whether the finance change variable is censored or not). We thus obtain “first stage” estimates of the statistical effect of MLD on news, and “final stage” estimates of the effect of MLD-related news on the finance change variable. $ ^{27} $

Under the instrumental variables adaptation, our Table 3 estimates of the relation of MLD to finance change variables are reduced from estimates. Applying IV Tobit or IV linear regression then provides separate estimates of each component coefficient. Intuitively, an IV Tobit or IV linear system decomposes Table 3 estimates of MLD into two factors. $ ^{28} $ Importantly, standard errors on each factor are also produced, allowing us to discern whether both links in the two-step chain are strong.

Under our hypotheses, we should find that MLD is significantly positively related to the volume of news about the firm (and perhaps also the slant), and that the part of news that is linked to MLD is significantly related to financing outcomes with the appropriate signs. We already know from Table 3 that MLD links to financing changes, so the new information is in the decomposition. It provides two new pieces of evidence: whether media-linked directors link positively to news, and whether news links to financing change outcomes with the predicted signs. If the link from MLD to news is often insignificant or negative, then our news-based thinking would be called into serious question. If the link from news to financing change is statistically insignificant, it would suggest that there are paths other than news.

We can formalize this method using the following two-equation system.

 $$ \begin{aligned}News\ outcome&=a_{2}+b_{2}\times MLD+c_{2}\times Control\ variables\\&\quad+Fixed\ effects+\epsilon_{2},\ and\quad(2)\end{aligned} $$ 

 $$ \begin{aligned}Financing change&=a_{3}+b_{3}\times News outcome+c_{3}\\&\quad\times Control variables+Fixed effects\\&\quad+\epsilon_{3},\end{aligned} $$ 

where

• News outcome is, alternatively, the lagged firm-year observation on a news variable that measures the count or the slant of news stories (when it's count, we use the log form);

• Financing change is the current firm-year observation on a specific type of financing change, as described above in Step 1;

-  $ a_{2} $ is an intercept term,  $ b_{2} $ is a slope coefficient, and  $ c_{2} $ is a vector of slope coefficients;

•  $ a_{3} $ is an intercept term,  $ b_{3} $ is a slope coefficient, and  $ c_{3} $ is a vector of slope coefficients;

• MLD is an indicator variable set to one if a media-linked director is included in a firm's board in the lagged firm year, and zero otherwise,

• Control variables are lagged observations for the same control variables as previously used;

• Fixed effects refers to industry, year, and MSA fixed effects;

 $ \epsilon_{2} $ refers to an appropriate error term; and

•  $ \epsilon_{3} $ refers to an appropriate error term corresponding to the regression estimation method used (which could be IV Tobit or IV linear regression, according to the nature of the specific dependent variable).

As in the discussion above, the key coefficients of interest are  $ b_{2} $ and  $ b_{3} $. Instrumental variable methods must be used to estimate the coefficients because the error of Eq. (2) is obviously wrapped into the error of Eq. (3). $ ^{29} $

#### 4.3. Second empirical method: path analysis

We have argued that the adaptation of IV regression analyses described above reliably traces the influence of

media-linked directors on financing changes via a news channel. We are, however, adapting the IV regression mechanics to our purpose. Also, the analysis does not produce a test statistic for the full two-step path internally, but only for each of the component steps. Therefore, in this subsection, we report a formal path analysis. Path analysis is an application of structural equation modeling, which has a long history of use in fields such as biology, sociology, psychology, and economics, and has been used recently in finance (e.g., Pevzner et al. (2015)). $ ^{31} $

Via path analysis, we are able to check the robustness of the method in the previous subsection using a well-established statistical method that has been developed for exactly the purpose of investigating indirect effects, i.e., where a starting-place variable (which is presumed to be exogenous for the purpose of the path analysis) affects a final outcome via an intermediate outcome. In our context, media-linked director presence is such a starting place variable, news is an intermediate outcome, and financing change is an outcome variable. In the language of path analysis, exogenous variables have effects on outcome variables via middle-step “mediating variables” (Wright, 1934; Baron and Kenny, 1986). In contrast to full-blown structural equation models, path analyses often do not involve latent variables, but rather trace effects via observable variables. This is the case for our application, as well as for the finance literature application of path analysis in Pevzner, Xie and Xin (2015).

Path analyses are estimated as maximum likelihood systems with underlying multivariate Gaussian distributions. For our analysis, we use standardized news outcome measures and the financing change measures, i.e., adjusted by subtracting the respective means and dividing by the respective standard deviations. This has the benefit that the total indirect effect of MLD on a given financing change variable, an estimate produced by the path model, is automatically measured as an economic effect (i.e., in standard deviation units) with an assessed p-value.

Table 7 therefore presents path analyses results as a confirmation and to gain additional statistical inference on the results from Table 6. We have included control variables in all the path analyses, though we do not table their coefficients to save space.

Panel A reports the direct path coefficient from MLD to Standardized news count for each financing change path model. The effect is the same for every financing change model, showing an increase in news count of about one-third of a standard deviation (0.31) for firm-years in which a media-linked director is present. Panel B shows the direct path coefficient between Standardized news count and Standardized financing change for each of our financing change measures. The coefficients have a direct interpretation as endogenous economic effects because we use standardized variables (i.e., effects are measured per unit of standard deviation). Coefficient estimates are strongly statistically significant for all financing changes except equity issuance, and the sign of each coefficient is consistent with the Governance Hypothesis. The results are fully in keeping with our earlier results, suggesting that the Governance Hypothesis, but not the Visibility Hypothesis, is strong in the data. Consistent with the results in Table 6, the news effects are largest for bonds and secured loans.

Panel C reports the indirect path coefficient from MLD, through the news mediating variable, then to each of the standardized financing changes measures. An indirect path coefficient measures the total effect on the outcome along the particular path, and allows for a test statistic. Given the standardized scaling of path variables, these coefficients have the interpretation of economic effects of media-linked director presence on financing changes, i.e., the proportion of a financing-change standard deviation difference that is associated with the presence of a media-linked director in the prior year. The subpanel shows that sizes of the total economic effects are consistent with those from the two-stage method reported in Table 6. Every economic effect, with the exception of the one for equity issuance, is highly statistically significant. As before, the results are most supportive of the Governance Hypothesis and the news path interpretation. $ ^{32} $ By providing a decomposition of our initial findings in Table 3 that traces the statistical effect of media-linked directors via a news path, the results provide further support for the Governance Hypothesis and the news path interpretation.

## 5. Empirical method and results for step 3: media-linked directors and causality for financing outcomes

#### 5.1.1. Instrumental variables analyses

We identify two instrumental variables, and use them in separate analyses to purge any endogeneity-driven biases from estimates of Eq. (1). The instruments share a common logic in that they tie to the likelihood of a media-linked director being available or invited to join a company's board. Our first instrumental variable is the distance between the firm headquarters and the closest media firm. Our instrument is similar to that in Balsmeier et al. (2014) and is in the same spirit as that in Knyazeva et al. (2013). Having a media firm close by would be expected to increase the chance of recruiting a media-linked director to the board simply because media-linked directors are more available in the firm's region. This instrument is obviously connected with geography, and could correlate to the firm's proximity to financial centers, as explored above. Therefore, we continue to include fixed-effect controls for MSAs to be sure that no correlations due to the firm's geography per se, as opposed to its distance-based ease of access to media directors, are creeping in through the back door. We refer to this instrumental variable below as the Distance IV.

Our second instrumental variable is an indicator variable as to whether the company CEO has previously (before joining the current firm) served on another board with a media-linked director. Our reasoning is that at least one media-linked director is already in the network of the CEO, and the CEO is at least somewhat accustomed to working with directors with this orientation. This should raise the probability of the CEO opening the door of his or her firm to a media-linked director in a subsequent time period. We refer to this instrumental variable below as the CEO-Network IV.

We compute our instrumental variables estimates of the effects of media-linked director indicator variables (MLDs) on financing changes following a methodology recommended by Wooldridge (2002) and used by Adams et al. (2009). The procedure is built for the case of a binary endogenous variable, which is exactly our circumstance. In a first-stage Probit regression, we establish the statistical relations between MLD and a particular instrumental variable, allowing for our standard control variables and fixed effects. We then compute the tted probabilities of the MLD, and use them as instruments in a standard IV Tobit or linear regression. $ ^{33} $

We have confirmed that, in our application, weak instruments do not appear to be a concern. As pointed out by Stock and Yogo (2005), Jiang (2017), and others, weak instruments (i.e., ones with weak statistical explanatory power for the endogenous variable) can lead to exaggerated final estimates. In their survey of the weak instrument literature, Stock and Yogo (2005) develop benchmarks for the necessary magnitude of an F-statistic to confidently rule out weak instrument concerns. Following their work, econometric techniques to detect and deal with weak instruments have been further developed. As recommended by Andrews et al. (2019), we compute the Montiel Olea and Pflueger (2013) effective first-stage F-statistic and compare it to the benchmark of 10. The effective F-statistic is around 100 for either instrument, and hence both easily pass this test. Additionally, we do not believe there are strong reasons to expect that these instruments would be causal for financing changes in themselves, and they satisfy the exclusion restriction.

#### 5.2. Time series analysis and Granger causality: set-up

Our instrumental variable regression results support our claim that the influence of media-linked directors on financing is not due to the back door effects of outside variables or to reverse causality. The evidence is suggestive of a causal relation, but, as we have emphasized, we do not claim to prove a causal relation. Therefore, at this point, we investigate another pattern that could potentially add to the suggestive evidence: pure time series relations between the financing decisions and board membership changes that add or subtract media-linked directors.

In particular, we ask whether the arrivals and departures of media-linked directors Granger cause financing choices. In other words, when media-linked directors step-in or step-out of a company's board membership, do financing change decisions follow soon after, considering the dynamic financing changes patterns in the data? Granger causality in the context of vector autoregression (VAR) models is a well-developed statistical technology for answering such questions. Its strengths and limitations are well known, which facilitates judgements about the economic importance of our findings.

In a VAR, all variables are considered endogenous, and are jointly regressed on their own lagged values. A well-developed technology, beginning with Holz-Eakin et al. (1988), exists for estimating VARs and assessing Granger causality relations in panel data for which there are apt to be a large number of panels (firms, in our case) but a small number of time-steps (years, in our case). These "Panel VAR" methods rely on a particular generalized method of moments procedure to avoid biases that exist with ordinary least squares as applied to panel models with lagged endogenous variables. The bias can be especially serious in cases with a small number of time-steps, as is our case. Their suggestions have been widely applied over the years, and recently in the finance literature by Rossi et al. (2018) in a setting with strong structural similarities to ours, i.e., infrequent revisions in a variable that might Granger cause a financial outcome with possibly persistent effects. Therefore, as our main basis for assessing Granger causality, we compute Panel VAR models. Before doing so, we discuss the nature of the Panel VAR method and how it can be applied to our data to assess Granger causality.

#### 5.2.1. The panel VAR method

Previous literature shows that OLS estimates of autoregressive parameters are biased in finite samples due to the correlation of the lagged dependent variable with deeper lags of the error term. The bias in OLS can be especially important in short time series (Sawa, 1978), which is exactly the case in a Panel VAR. Ignoring the fixed effects (i.e., simply using pooled OLS) biases estimates of autoregressive parameters upward, and addressing the fixed effects via the inclusion of dummy variables biases them toward zero (Nerlove, 1971). These biases persist even with a large number of panel units if the number of time periods is kept fixed (Nickell, 1981). Essentially, the issue is that the fixed effects represent additional cross-panel heterogeneity that is either not addressed (pooled OLS method) or imprecisely estimated (dummy variable method).

To help, Holz-Eakin et al. (1988) suggest removing the fixed effects via first-differencing. This has the side effect of inducing the correlation of a regressor with the regression error. $ ^{34} $ To avoid bias in regression estimates, they further suggest a generalized method of moments approach in which deeper lags of the autoregressive variables, as well as their differences, are used as instruments for the lagged endogenous regressors.

We follow the guidance of Holz-Eakin et al. (1988). To allow for the possibility of time fixed effects, we adjust all model variables by yearly means. Additionally, we follow Rossi et al. (2018) in estimating Panel VARs via the Arellano and Bond (1991) generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator. We follow the suggestion of Arellano and Bover (1995) to include orthogonalized forward instruments, in consideration of the very short panels in our data set. Holz-Eakin et al. (1988) recommend careful choice of lag length in Panel VARs. With our very short panels, we don't have much flexibility. We use two lags in our VARs and we economize on the available data by estimating exactly identified models. $ ^{35} $

#### 5.2.2. Data considerations

Firms that choose to have media-linked directors and firms that raise outside financing are likely different from others in unmeasurable ways as well as measurable ones. We therefore restrict our sample to firms that experience an addition or deletion of a media-linked director and that engage in bond market finance at least once during our sample period. $ ^{36} $ We have a sample of 3252 firm-years for use in the time series study and 397 media additions or deletions (steps-in or steps-out). By step-in and step-out, we mean the year-specific events in which BoardEx shows that a particular media-linked director either joins or departs from a board.

Two additional choices must be made for our analysis. First, media-linked directors stepping in could be modelled as the mirror image of stepping out, or as separate processes. Because we do not have predictions as to whether additions (steps-in) and deletions (steps-out) of media-linked directors to a board have a similar but opposite effect on financing, or different-sized effects, we analyze them separately.

Second, the timing and duration of any effects due to stepping in or out are not specified in our hypotheses. We reason that in our analysis we want to understand the effect of shocks in the board composition, and it makes sense to focus most on shocks that are somewhat isolated, i.e., to avoid situations in which a step-out immediately follows a step-in, for a step-in and step-out that are too close in time could muddle the effect on financing. Moreover, the effect of a step-in of a media-linked director on financing could occur only after the director has spent some time in office. Additionally, some effects may not immediately reverse upon the director's departure.

Based on the above reasoning, we distinguish what we call "salient" steps-in and steps-out, though we still include all steps in our Panel VARs, as we explain below. Specifically, for salient steps-in, we require that the new media-linked director spends at least two years on the board. This would give her reasonable time to have an effect on news and, consequently, financing, avoiding situations where a step-in and step-out are too close in time to separate out their effects. Similarly, for salient steps-out, we reason that if the media-linked director has been present in the firm for a long time (more than seven years, for our reported results, though we have checked for robustness to small variations), then her departure might not have a strong effect on the news and financing, as she could have already improved media coverage and information about a company to a point that her departure would not have a near-term effect. To make sure that our results are not biased by the modification of the step-in and step-out definition, we also retain in our VARs the difference between the "total" measure of step-in and step-out (i.e., the step-in and step-out observed directly from BoardEx) and the salient measure of step-in and step-out. We call this difference "residual" step-in and step-out. $ ^{37} $

With the stated criteria, about 73% of the steps are classified as “salient.” The differences between salient and total steps are more rare at lags, due to the short length of the panels: some of the originally measured steps-in and steps-out occur near the end of their firms’ time series. For example, at the third lag, salient and total steps are exactly the same. Finally, salient and total steps-in and steps-out show similar descriptive statistics.

All things considered, to uncover time series predictability in our setting, if it is present, is a challenge due to the small number of time series observations available for each firm, the fact that media-linked directors are persistent, and the fact that their influence could be exerted only over a protracted time period. In view of these challenges, we expect stronger results for financing change variables that emanate from discrete firm decisions (new issues of public debt, new secured loans, and issues of new equity), rather than the amalgamation of granular firm decisions and outcomes over time (balance sheet changes). The former occur at a specific point in time based on a firm's decision and the willingness of capital providers to lend. That said, we report results for all our financing change variables.

Of core interest are the predictive effects of step-in and step-out variables on the financing change variables, so we focus our reporting on those. We also assess whether the financing change variables Granger cause the step measures, and whether the step variables Granger cause each other.

We next discuss these time series results in more detail. In Table 9, Panel A, we report standard  $ \chi^{2} $ test statistics and p-values based on Panel VAR(2) models as described above that include two lags of the respective financing change variable, two lags of the salient step-in variable, and two lags of the salient step-out variable.

Column (1) of Panel A reports that Granger noncausality of salient steps-out is rejected, with a p-value of 0.078. Column (2) reports rejection for salient steps-in for the case of new secured loans, with a p-value of 0.012. Column (3) reports rejection for salient steps-out for the case of change in blockholders, with a p-value of 0.001. Thus, for all financing change measures in Columns (1) to (3), we find that Granger noncausality of salient steps is rejected. These measures come from transactions-based records, so their exact timing is likely more meaningful than the balance sheet-based measures in Columns (5) and (6). These three financing change variables relate especially to the Governance Hypothesis, although the Visibility Hypothesis could also be a reason for rejection in the case of public debt issues. Much doubt is cast on a Visibility Hypothesis interpretation, however, by the finding in Column (4) for public equity issues that Granger noncausality is not rejected at any conventional level for either salient steps-in or steps-out. Neither do we find rejection for the Capital IQ-based changes in bank loans or changes in term loans measures in Columns (5) and (6).

The measures of salient steps-in and steps-out have been netted out of all steps-in and steps-out based on our reasoning above. Since judgement was involved, we present Granger causality tests in Panel B that are based on Panel VARs that also include two lags of residual steps, and we present additional details on all-inclusive Panel VARs in Panel C. $ ^{38} $ These models thus do not eliminate any step data, but merely categorize it.

The pattern of rejections of Granger noncausality in Panel B is exactly the same as that in Panel A. Overall, the statistical significance of the Panel B test statistics for salient steps is very similar to that in Panel A. Moreover, none of the Granger causality tests for residual steps-in or steps-out indicate rejection of the null hypothesis at even the 10% level.

In Panel C, we report on the Panel VAR coefficients that drive these salient-steps rejections of Granger noncausality. In addition to predicting Granger causality of changes in media-linked director presence for financing changes, our hypotheses predict particular directions for the relation. From the Governance Hypothesis, steps-in (steps-out) of media-linked directors should lead to decreases (increases) in monitoring intensive financing. Our hypotheses are not specific as to whether steps-in or steps-out should be the more important, but at least one class of steps should matter. $ ^{39} $

Panel C provides evidence that the signs of the coefficients on the salient step lags that drive the Granger noncausality rejections (i.e., have small p-values) are supportive of the Governance Hypothesis. Specifically, for bond issuance, we find a significant negative coefficient on salient Step-out at the second lag (p = 0.034), underlying in part the rejection of Granger noncausality reported in Panel B. For secured loan taking, we find a positive coefficient on salient Step-in at the second lag (p = 0.024), leading in part to the rejection of Granger noncausality. For the change in blockholdings, we observe a positive statistically significant coefficient on salient Step-out at the second lag (p = 0.004), leading in part to the rejection of Granger noncausality. Consistent with our finding of no rejection of Granger noncausality for public equity issues, none of the individual coefficients on lags of salient steps are significant at the 5% level in the Panel VAR results.

To interpret the magnitude of coefficients in Panel C, it is helpful to keep in mind that the Step-in and Step-out regressors are dummy variables. Thus, a coefficient of 0.01 associated with a step-in or step-out at a particular lag would indicate a direct linear effect to raise the dependent variable by 0.01. As with any VAR, it should be kept in mind that the full effect of any shock depends on the full matrix of coefficients.

We have not found statistically strong results for the two measures of balance sheet loan changes that are derived from Capital IQ. As mentioned above, this could be explained by the fact that the timing of data from issuance or transactions records for the other financing change measures is more precise. These balance sheet-change variables are the amalgam of many influences, so perhaps it is not surprising that they are the ones that are not noticeably sensitive to board changes over specific short periods of time. The Panel C results indicate a second possible reason. At the foot of the panel, we report coefficients on lags of financing change variables as drivers of current finance change variables. The size and statistical significance of these autocorrelation measures are strong for the Capital IQ-based balance sheet change measures, whereas they are weak for public debt issues and secured loan takings. The strong autocorrelation could dominate any effect of the step-in and step-out measures.

As a final analysis, hoping to average across any noise in our step measures, we have also estimated VAR models at the two digit SIC industry level for these two financing change measures. We find Granger causality of Step-out on bank debt at that level, though the results for term loans still fail to be statistically significant. Finally, we do not find evidence that past financing Granger causes the step-in or step-out of a media-linked director (results not reported). We do find, logically, that the steps-in Granger cause steps-out.

In our Granger causality analysis, we do not have a strong prediction as to whether step-in or step-out should matter more, and the fact that steps out tend to drive slightly more of our results could be due to the fact that they represent 62% of our MLD changes.

Taken together, the evidence weighs to a finding of Granger causality for the steps-in and steps-out of media-linked directors as they affects financing change variables. As relates to the Governance Hypothesis, the effects are especially clear with respect to bond issuance, secured loan takings, and changes in blockholdings. We find weaker results for balance sheet-based loan measures that are the amalgam of many small decisions, whereas the results are stronger for finance change measures that are the result of discrete decisions. We do not find strong evidence for the Visibility Hypothesis, in that it has the clearest implications for the case of equity issuance for which we do not find evidence of Granger causality of steps-in or steps-out.

Overall, evidence from these analyses of time series changes represents one more pattern in the data that is supportive of the hypothesis that media-linked directors result in shifts toward less governance-intensive financing.

• Do media connections via current or past employment (such as journalism, rather than via boards) matter?

If the statistical effect of media-linked directors is driven by insiders, not independents, it is less plausible that the story is really about governance. If the effect of media-linked directors is merely coincident with the effect of bankers on boards (e.g., perhaps because access to banks and access to media are correlated), then it is less plausible that the story is really about news. The evidence to answer these questions is presented in Table 10. To construct this table, we estimate our standard set of Tobit and OLS regressions, with our full set of control variables and industry, year, and MSA fixed effects. $ ^{40} $ To save space, we do not report coefficient estimates on control variables, which are much the same as in previous tables. For each panel of the table, we implement a specific change to the standard model to address a specific issue.

Do independent media-linked directors matter? To answer this question, we make a substitution for our usual lagged media-linked director variable. Instead, we use an indicator variable that is set to one for firm-years with the presence of an independent director who is also a director in a media firm. $ ^{41} $ We continue to include an indicator variable regressor for the proportion of independent directors on the board. Thus, the estimated coefficient on the new indicator variable reflects the association of a particular finance change dependent variable with the independent MLD indicator after controlling for the dominance of independents in general. As shown in Panel A of the table, most

of the coefficient estimates follow the by now familiar pattern. Statistical significance is a little weaker than in many of our other analyses, consistent with the smaller proportion of independent MLDs versus MLDs overall. Independent media-linked directors are associated with changes toward less governance-intensive financing.

Do media-linked directors matter, controlling for banker directors? Kroszner and Strahan (2001) suggest the possibility that bankers on boards of directors have important governance effects, and face various conflicts of interest. Given our focus on financing, it makes sense to consider whether the effects we have uncovered are robust to considering banks' direct influence at the board level. To answer the question posed above, we add a regressor to our basic model. Using data from BoardEx, we research whether each director in our sample is on the board of a bank. Based on this, we form an indicator variable that is equal to one for firm-years with a banker on the firm's board, and zero otherwise. $ ^{42} $ We include these Banker director indicator variables in our core regression specification. As shown in Panel B of the table, the pattern of relation between change in financing measures and the media-linked director indicator variable is the same as before. The Banker director indicator is not statistically significantly associated with most change in financing variables, though it is (weakly) positively associated with public debt issued and negatively associated with the change in blockholder ownership. The results are in line with Guner et al. (2008), who find only a weak effect of commercial bankers and investment bankers on, respectively, new loan size and new public debt issues. The statistically stronger effect overall is the one associated with the media-linked director measure.

Do past-board level media connections matter? To answer this question, we add an additional media-linkage measure to the regression specifications. Using BoardEx data, we form an indicator variable that is equal to one when a board includes a director with past (not current) experience on a media board. $ ^{43} $ We enter the value of this indicator variable in our core regression specifications in addition to our usual lagged MLD regressor. The results, reported in Panel C, suggest that past media board experience is (negatively) associated only with new loans issued.

Does journalism experience matter? We answer this question using an analysis that is procedurally similar to that for the previous question. Using BoardEx data, we form an indicator variable that is equal to one when a board includes a director with past or current employment experience in journalism. $ ^{44} $ We enter the lagged value of this indicator variable in our core regression specifications in addition to our usual lagged MLD regressor. The results, reported in Panel D, do not suggest that journalism experience is associated with any change in financing measure. In unreported results, we also check to see whether past experience in public relations, head of communications, or similar positions for board members matter for financing. We do not find any positive evidence in this respect. Overall, it seems that our results are driven more by the current networks of media-linked directors rather than by their accumulated expertise either in media firms or in dealing with media companies.

Does the importance of media-linked directors center on small firms? To this point, we have used total assets in each firm-year as a control variable, but we have not measured whether the importance of media-linked directors might be different for firms in different size classes. This is worth checking because the Visibility Hypothesis has the most intuitive appeal for small firms, the ones that are likely not as visible to begin with and that might benefit most from expanded press coverage. To check, we construct a dummy variable equal to one for firms with size below the sample median for each given year. We interact this small firm dummy with our lagged MLD variable, and then re-run our base analysis including the interaction term along with the small firm dummy. $ ^{45} $

The results are reported in Panel E. The lagged MLD variable retains the same sign and significance as in our base case analysis for each financing change measure. The interaction term is only statistically significant for blockholder changes and weakly for new loans issued. We conclude that the MLD effect on financing change is not concentrated in the large or small company size class, but characterizes the sample more generally.

### 7. Conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated the importance of the media backgrounds of members of firms' boards of directors. Our aim is to understand how media-linked directors could be associated with financing outcomes. We consider hypotheses relating both to the visibility and governance of firms. We find that these directors are associated with a shift toward less governance-intensive forms of financing. In particular, bond financing increases, bank loan financing (and especially its most governance-intensive varieties) decreases, and blockholder ownership decreases. There is no strong effect on equity issuance overall. We find that media-linked directors are associated with more news about their firms, suggesting the mechanism by which media-linked directors have an effect. We perform a number of additional tests that suggest that these linkages are causal.

Most broadly, the results demonstrate that the internal governance set-up of the firm links with governance-related elements of its external situation, such as transparency, in important ways. In our case, we have shown

that there are important financing implications. More specifically, the financing implications substantiate a governance substitution effect in which the stronger external governance force of increased news coverage substitutes for direct monitoring by capital providers. This chain of outcomes begins with media-linked directors, an aspect of internal governance. In contrast to the very limited information of direct visibility effects unlocking access to capital that we uncover, these governance effects are strong and consistent.

Previous literature has emphasized the fact that directors' backgrounds can be important via advising and skill. One important contribution of our study is to provide evidence that the backgrounds of the members of firms' boards of directors are important for governance outcomes.
