## Abstract

We examine changes in bank loan contracts after borrowers experience a nearby local newspaper closure. Compared to a sample of control firms, we find that the closure of a local newspaper leads to higher interest spreads for borrowers. This effect is more pronounced when there are fewer related lenders in the syndicate, when lenders have less prior lending experience in the local area, when the closed local newspapers are associated with increases in misconduct cases, and for institutional lenders who rely more heavily on others for monitoring. In addition, we observe that loan contract amendments become less frequent, while covenant strictness increases following newspaper closures. Our main findings are robust to various research design specifications and are not driven by deteriorating local economic conditions. Our findings suggest that local media still plays a significant role in the debt markets, even as society moves deeper into the internet era.

# Fermetures de journaux locaux et contrats de prêts bancaires

#### Résumé

Cette étude examine les modifications apportées aux contrats de prêts bancaires à la suite de la fermeture d'un journal local, en se concentrant sur l'expérience vécue par les emprunteurs. En comparaison avec un échantillon d'entreprises témoins, les auteurs constatent que la fermeture d'un journal local entraîne une augmentation des

marges d'intérêt pour les emprunteurs. Cet effet est plus marqué lorsque le nombre de prêteurs au sein du syndicat est limité, lorsque les prêteurs ont accordé moins de prêts à l'échelle locale, lorsque la fermeture des journaux locaux est associée à une augmentation des cas d'écarts de conduite, et pour les institutions de prêt qui s'appuient davantage sur d'autres entités pour assureur leur contrôle. En outre, les auteurs observent que les modifications des contrats de prêt sont de moins en moins fréquentes, alors que l'application des clauses restrictives devient de plus ne plus rigoureuse après la fermeture des journaux. Les résultats principaux résistent aux détails particuliers du plan de recherche et ne sont pas influencés par la détérioration des conditions économiques locales. Les conclusions des auteurs soulignent l'importance des médias locaux sur les marchés de la dette aujourd'hui, même si la société évolue de plus en plus à l'ère Internet.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

A growing literature documents the role of the media in financial markets, demonstrating that media coverage plays a role in mitigating information frictions, reducing information risk, and enhancing monitoring (e.g., Bushman et al., 2017; Liu & McConnell, 2013; Tetlock, 2015). Until recently, “media” has been used to broadly describe all manner of publications ranging from newswire coverage to online media such as Twitter, corporate websites, or internet webpages. However, with the rise and spread of new digital media platforms, older forms of media—specifically local print newspapers—are decreasing in popularity. From 2004 to 2018, more than one in five newspapers circulated in the United States closed, and the total number of journalists working for newspapers was cut in half. $ ^{1} $ Increasingly, communities across the country are left without local newspapers, which has resulted in the neglect of stories about local government, local companies, and local schools. $ ^{2} $ As local newspapers have a separate and unique role in shaping firms’ information and monitoring environments, prior findings related to total media coverage and loan contracts may not apply to local media. In this study, we investigate the effect of local newspaper closures on private loan terms, filling a gap in the literature on the role of local media in financial markets.

Local newspapers can provide two broad types of information that can be incremental to what lenders obtain from borrowers (i.e., private and public disclosures) and other sources (e.g., national media and social media). The first type is unique nonfinancial information about

the borrowing firm, particularly negative news (e.g., labor rights problems, environmental pollution issues). The second type is local information, such as local public policy changes (Kyung & Nam, 2023) or information about the local social and political environments. These two categories of information, which serve as relevant inputs for banks assessing credit risk, may require long and in-depth investigations by local newspapers. Other stakeholders, lenders in our setting, are unlikely to obtain those two categories of information by themselves at a low acquisition cost. The information from local newspapers thus can help lenders reduce adverse selection concerns during the ex ante due diligence and moral hazard concerns during the ex post loan duration. In addition, local newspapers can serve as separate watchdogs that can discipline corporate behavior (Heese et al., 2022; Jiang & Kong, 2024). The monitoring role of local newspapers, therefore, can be priced and partially reduce the effort of monitoring by lenders. Both effects predict a negative effect of local newspaper closures on borrowers' loan terms.

However, there are several reasons we may not observe this effect. First, lenders in the syndicated loan market have access to private financial information. It is unclear whether local newspapers can provide marginally relevant information. Second, prior studies document a positive bias of local newspapers for local companies. This bias is driven by advertising revenue incentives, making it unclear whether lenders value and rely on local newspapers' monitoring and information generation (e.g., Gurun & Butler, 2012). Third, the information provided by high-tech media (i.e., social media, online news sources) may perfectly substitute for information provided by local newspapers or even improve information generation and dissemination in the event of a local newspaper closure. Furthermore, local firms themselves may respond to a newspaper closure, acting to fill any information void, resulting in no net change in information available to lenders after a newspaper closure.

Utilizing a sample of 26 local newspaper closures over the period from 1991 to 2016, we employ a difference-in-differences (DiD) research design for a sample of 6,842 loans. We examine loan contract changes after the closure of a nearby local newspaper. Specifically, we examine whether local newspaper closures are associated with higher interest spreads for borrowers in affected locales. We compare the loan terms of borrowers located within 50 miles of a closed newspaper (treatment firms) to borrowers located 50 to 200 miles away from a closed newspaper (control firms). Our empirical strategy allows for strong identification because our sample of newspaper closures is spread across time and geographic locations. Our results show that the closure of a local newspaper is associated with a 30.24 basis point increase in interest spread. For the sample average loan amount of $119 million with a 40-month maturity, a 30-basis point increase translates to approximately $1.2 million in additional interest expense over the life of the loan. The finding is robust to different fixed effects and controlling for national media coverage.

We conduct several tests to provide evidence supporting the information and monitoring channels that we argue drive our results. We find that our documented effect is more pronounced when there are fewer related lenders in the syndicate, when lenders have less prior lending experience in the local area, when the closed local newspapers are associated with increases in misconduct cases, and for institutional lenders who rely more heavily on others for monitoring. Overall, the findings support our argument that local newspapers play an information-generating and monitoring role in the syndicated loan market.

We further find that after a local newspaper closure, the frequency of loan amendments decreases, indicating that lenders have less information. Meanwhile, lenders impose stricter covenants on local borrowers after a local newspaper closure, indicating that lenders impose additional monitoring. We also find that lenders are more likely to include local participants in the loan syndicate, suggesting they try other ways to get information. At the same time, firms are more likely to rely on related lenders to get loans following a newspaper closure. Together, these findings support local newspapers' important and separate role in private debt contract design.

We demonstrate that the parallel trends assumption holds in our DiD design, and we show that the effect is robust to different specifications of our research design, including different

sample constructions (treatment group regressions, state-matched sample regressions, industry-matched sample regressions, propensity score matching [PSM] sample regressions, and balanced sample regressions). Our results are also robust to alternative standard error clustering methods (event, firm, industry, county), a different classification of treated and control firms (using county area instead of distance), and different sample periods.

While local newspaper closures are not exogenous, raising potential concerns related to endogeneity, our research design allows us to largely mitigate endogeneity concerns. In all our tests, we compare the interest spreads of borrowers facing similar geographic environments, varying the distance between borrowers' headquarters and the location of the closed newspaper. To further mitigate the endogeneity concern, we use Craigslist's entry into a local market as an exogenous shock to local newspaper viability and document a robust effect of Craigslist-induced newspaper closures on loan spreads. More importantly, we explicitly test for changes in macroeconomic indicators before and after local newspaper closures to rule out the alternative explanation that a deteriorated local economic environment drives our results.

Our study makes the following contributions to the literature. First, we provide direct evidence that local information and monitoring are important for bank lending, with local newspapers providing relevant information and monitoring on local firms. Our findings are consistent with local newspapers playing an information-generating and monitoring role in the syndicated loan market. By providing direct evidence, we further the understanding of what information is used in the syndicated loan contract design process and how lenders adjust contracts when local information environments change (e.g., Bushman et al., 2017; Graham et al., 2008).

Second, we further the research on the effects of media on lending. Rajan (1992) highlights that more information and positive information signals increase the competition between lenders and lead to more favorable loan conditions for borrowers. Houston et al. (2011) investigate media structures and corruption in bank lending, and they provide evidence that media concentration and national ownership of the media decrease the quality of the lending environment by neglecting stories related to corruption in lending. Bushman et al. (2017) suggest that overall media sentiment helps reduce information asymmetries among the parties of a loan and the loan spreads for US borrowers. Gao et al. (2020) document that the existence of local newspapers reduces the cost of municipal bonds in the public debt market. $ ^{3} $ Our study provides the first evidence that local newspapers play an important and unique information and monitoring role in the syndicated loan market, which is different from that of total media coverage. By doing so, we offer clear evidence that different types of media have different and separate effects on lending.

Our findings are likely to be of interest to managers, investors, and regulators over the foreseeable future. The newspaper industry, especially the local newspaper industry, is likely to continue to decline over time. Our investigation is motivated by the current and ongoing public discussion about the future and importance of traditional print media in society. Recent work investigates the consequences of local newspaper closures and finds that firms react to it by increasing dividend payments to alleviate equity investors' concerns about the loss of a local monitor (Kim et al., 2021). Our results offer insight into what a future could look like where local newspapers play a significantly reduced role.

## 2 | HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

Local newspapers play an important and separate role in mitigating information asymmetry and increasing monitoring. The loss of a local newspaper will influence lending through two

channels, which we label information and monitoring. Through the information channel, local newspapers are uniquely able to produce information about local firms that will reduce both adverse selection concerns during the ex ante due diligence phase and moral hazard concerns during the ex post loan duration. Local newspapers provide two unique types of information that lenders, especially non-relationship lenders, may not easily obtain elsewhere: (1) unique nonfinancial information about borrowers, in particular, negative news (e.g., labor rights problems, environmental pollution issues) and (2) local information, such as local public policy changes (Kyung & Nam, 2023) or information about the local social and political environment. $ ^{4} $

These two types of information are not easily obtained or cheaply collected. For example, publishing articles about local issues often requires long and in-depth investigations relying on local interviews that utilize relationships with local community members and local government employees. $ ^{5} $ Local newspapers have higher incentives to cover local firms (Miller & Shanthikumar, 2010), and they have more original and timely stories about local firms compared to other media because of their proximity to local sources such as firm employees and local suppliers (e.g., Engelberg & Parsons, 2011; Peress, 2014). For example, prior literature finds that local newspapers produce a significant amount of information-creating “isolated” coverage (Kyung & Nam, 2023), $ ^{6} $ and local newspapers continue to produce most original news content in the United States, between 50% and 95% of original content, especially on local stories, despite marked declines (Cagé et al., 2017; Erdal, 2011). Furthermore, local news on national outlets is often picked up from local newspapers (e.g., Nielsen, 2015; Pew Research Center, 2010). Therefore, lenders are unlikely to easily or cheaply obtain this information without local newspapers.

Importantly, these two categories of information are relevant when assessing firms’ operations and future cash flows. $ ^{7} $ During the ex ante due diligence phase, lenders gather necessary information, including financial and nonfinancial, firm-specific, and firm-related macro information, to assess the quality of the borrowers and negotiate terms and conditions (Giannetti & Yafeh, 2012). Declines in information about borrowers and local areas after local newspaper closures will make it harder for lenders, especially those unfamiliar with the borrowers or local areas, to assess firms’ credit risk before loan issuance when setting loan terms, increasing adverse selection concerns. During the ex post loan duration, lenders often regularly request and analyze borrowers’ information to ensure the credit risk is under control (Gustafson et al., 2021). Reductions in new information about borrowers and local area conditions from local newspapers will make it harder for lenders to make timely amendments to loan terms when a borrower’s operational condition or the local area’s economic or policy condition changes, exacerbating moral hazard concerns. While local newspapers themselves do not eliminate (i.e., substitute completely for) the need or desire for private information that lenders are

able to generate for themselves, their presence can complement the information that lenders independently obtain and can reduce the relative information asymmetries. Taken together, the information supplied by local newspapers serves as a relevant input for lenders assessing firms' credit risk before loan issuance, as well as important information for lenders amending loan terms after issuance.

Through the monitoring channel, local newspapers act as external monitors. Local newspaper coverage directs the attention of local residents (i.e., local investors, employees, customers) to local firms, which builds up a stronger local governance system. For example, Gao et al. (2020) find that local newspaper closures lead to excess government salaries and increased municipal borrowing costs, consistent with the loss of a local monitor leading to rent extraction. Heese et al. (2022) show that losing a local newspaper leads local facilities to experience increased violations and penalties, consistent with managers taking advantage of the reduced monitoring. Therefore, when a local newspaper near a firm's headquarters closes, the loss of a monitor may increase the opportunity for managers to extract personal benefits (e.g., Haw et al., 2004). Thus, in addition to delivering information about firms to outsiders, newspapers also act as important monitors and watchdogs (Miller, 2006), which can be priced by lenders and partially reduce the effort of monitoring by lenders. Based on the preceding arguments, a local newspaper closure will amplify both adverse selection concerns during the ex ante due diligence phase and moral hazard concerns during the ex post loan duration for lenders. Moreover, a local newspaper closure will be viewed by lenders as the loss of a monitor and watchdog. Together, these factors may increase interest rates charged by lenders.

On the other side of these arguments, the closure of a local newspaper may not impact the information and monitoring environments of nearby firms and local lending markets. First, lenders already have access to borrowers' private information, so it is unclear whether local newspapers can provide marginally relevant information. Second, local newspapers may be biased in their reporting of local news because a large proportion of the local media income comes from advertisements of local firms. $ ^{8} $ Third, alternative mediums might substantially substitute for local newspapers. Technological development has enabled information to be transferred in more efficient ways through new high-tech mediums such as Twitter (Blankespoor et al., 2014; Elliott et al., 2018), corporate websites (Elliott et al., 2012), mobile phones (N. C. Brown et al., 2015; T. Brown et al., 2020; Grant, 2017), and internet webpages (Ahn et al., 2019; Drake et al., 2017; H. Stice, 2023). These competitors have contributed to several decades of declining subscriptions and advertising revenues for print newspapers (Barthel, 2017), $ ^{9} $ and the higher efficiency of new high-tech mediums may even positively affect local firms' information environments. In addition, firms themselves can react to the loss of a local newspaper to mitigate the bank's concern. $ ^{10} $

Overall, the loss of a local information provider and monitor may increase costs and risk for lenders. However, banks have privileged access to borrowers' information, local newspapers can be biased, other information sources may substitute for the loss of a local newspaper, and borrowers themselves may take actions to mitigate lender concerns after a newspaper closure. Thus, the overall effect of the closure of a local newspaper on a borrower's loan terms is unclear, making it an empirical question. Stated in the alternative:

Hypothesis. A local newspaper closure will not be associated with the private loan terms that a borrower receives.

## 3 | RESEARCH DESIGN AND SAMPLE SELECTION

### 3.1 | Data and sample selection

We manually construct our sample of local newspaper closures from several data sources following prior studies examining newspaper closures (e.g., Gao et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021). We start with a list of defunct newspapers in the United States provided by Wikipedia and Newspaper Death Watch and identify the dates the newspapers ceased publication and their headquarters from Wikipedia or other archived sources. $ ^{11} $ We identify 62 now-defunct US newspapers (including only publications in English) closed between 1991 and 2016 in total. $ ^{12} $

We obtain loan facility data from DealScan and financial accounting data from Compustat. We merge the loan facilities with the borrowing firm's financial information using the DealScan-Compustat link constructed and maintained by Michael Roberts and Wharton Research Data Services. Borrowers' financial statement data is matched with loan data for the fiscal year immediately before loan issuance. Because Compustat only provides firms' latest headquarters location, we obtain historical headquarters data from Joshua Lee, which we use to calculate the historical geographic distance between a firm and a closed newspaper. $ ^{13} $ We limit the facility sample to US dollar-denominated loans for US-domiciled nonfinancial firms (SIC code outside of 6000–6999) over the period from 1990 to 2017. We drop facilities where we cannot identify a lead bank.

Following prior studies, we construct our sample of treatment firms by identifying borrowers headquartered within 50 miles of the closed newspaper's headquarters (Gao et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021). $ ^{14} $ We identify control firms by isolating borrowers headquartered 50–200 miles from the closed newspaper's headquarters. The matching criterion based on geographic location is critical to our identification strategy. This matching is included to ensure that our treatment and control firms face similar geographic and macroeconomic conditions (Black, 1999) and to mitigate the concern that the differences we observe are the result of local economic factors correlated with newspaper closures. Our sample includes observations up to 3 years before and after a local newspaper closure, excluding the event year. We require no overlap among treatment windows, no overlap among control windows, and no overlap between treatment and control windows. $ ^{15} $ We also require that at least one observation exists

before and after the event in both the treatment and control groups for each closed newspaper. Our sample covers facilities from 1990 to 2017 because the DealScan-Compustat link ended in December 2017. After matching and applying observation requirements, we have 26 local newspaper closures and 6,842 loan facilities that remain in our sample. The 26 closed local newspapers are detailed in Table 1, Panel A. The annual facilities distribution is provided in Table 1, Panel B. The detailed sample construction process is provided in Table 1, Panel C. Our county-level macroeconomic data are from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, and national media coverage data are from RavenPack (e.g., D. Stice et al., 2022).

### 3.2 | Descriptive statistics

Table 2, Panel A, reports the descriptive statistics for our full sample. The sample average of loan spread is 216.6 basis points over LIBOR, loan size is $119.2M, loan maturity is 39.6 months, and the average number of lenders in the syndicate is 5.1. Our facility sample contains 57.1% of loans with collateral, and 55.0% of loans obtained from unrelated lenders. Approximately, 43.3% of the loans in our sample include a performance pricing provision. We also provide summary statistics for the treatment and control groups separately in Table 2, Panel B. All loan terms are similar between the treatment and control groups. All variables are defined in detail in the Appendix.

Panel C reports the Pearson correlation coefficients for variables in our main regression. Many of the loan term and firm-level control variables are significantly correlated and consistent with prior literature. For example, loan amount, number of lenders, performance pricing provisions, firm size, ROA, MTB, tangibility, Capex, dividends, Z-score, population, and employment growth rate are negatively related to loan spread. Maturity, collateral, unrelated lenders, leverage, CFO volatility, cash holdings, and bond ratings are positively related to loan spread.

### 3.3 | Empirical specifications

We employ a DiD estimation to examine the changes in loan contract terms in our treatment firms after the closure of a local newspaper relative to control firms, which are less likely to be exposed to a nearby newspaper closure shock. Thus, we include  $ Treat_{i} $,  $ Post_{t} $, and  $ Treat_{i} \times Post_{t} $ in our regression models, where  $ Post_{t} $ is set equal to one if the observation is after the shock for both treated and control firms.  $ Treat_{i} $ is set equal to one for the observations of treated firms (i.e., borrowers headquartered within 50 miles of a newspaper that closes). We only include facilities issued in a [-3, 3] window around an event, excluding the event year. Thus,  $ Post_{t} $ is equal to one for up to 3 years following a newspaper closure and zero otherwise. We include firm×event cohort fixed effects (e.g., Gormley & Matsa, 2011) in all regression specifications, so  $ Treat_{i} $ is omitted in our regressions. Thus, the variation in our analysis comes from within firm-event changes—that is, from firms with loans both before and after a nearby local newspaper closure.

To test our hypothesis, we estimate the following OLS regression:

 $$ \begin{aligned}Spread_{jist}=&\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}Treat_{i}\times Post_{t}+\alpha_{2}Post_{t}+\alpha_{j}Loan Controls_{j}+\alpha_{k}Firm Controls_{it-1}\\&+\alpha_{m}Local Economy Controls_{st-1}+Loan Purpose FE+Loan Type FE+Firm\\&\times Event FE+Year FE+\varepsilon_{jist},\end{aligned} $$ 

where the dependent variable  $ Spread_{jist} $ is set equal to the all-in-interest spread as reported in DealScan for facility j of firm i located in county s issued in year t. The independent variable of interest is  $ Treat_{i} \times Post_{t} $ and  $ \alpha_{1} $ is expected to be positive if treatment firms have higher interest rates after the shock of a local newspaper closure.

### 4.3 | Channel tests: Monitoring channel

To provide support for the monitoring channel, we also perform three tests. Heese et al. (2022) show that local newspaper closures lead to increases in misconduct for nearby firms, indicating that local newspapers monitor local firms. We investigate whether the main effect we document is larger when corporate misconduct increases after a newspaper closure. Specifically, we follow Heese et al. (2022) and utilize corporate misconduct data from Violation Tracker produced by the Corporate Research Project of Good Jobs First. $ ^{18} $ We identify cases of misconduct that occur within 50 miles of closed newspapers’ headquarters in the window  $ [-3, 3] $ around newspaper closures. In doing so, we can calculate the number of nearby misconduct cases in the 3 years before and after a newspaper closure, respectively. We partition our newspaper closure cases into two groups: newspaper closures with increased misconduct cases are classified as More Violations, and other newspaper closures are classified as Less Violations. We interact these two indicator variables with Post and report the regression results in Column 1 of Table 4, Panel B. We find that a newspaper closure is associated with a 27.15 basis point significant increase in loan spreads for loan facilities in the more violation treatment group, but a 17.42 basis point decrease, though insignificant, in spreads in the less violation treatment group. The coefficient difference is statistically significant, providing evidence consistent with the monitoring channel. In other words, when the local newspaper closure causes an absence of monitoring and increased misconduct, lenders respond by requiring higher interest rates.

Next, we consider the effect of differences in the monitoring ability of banks and institutional lenders. As documented in prior studies, banks are able to actively monitor borrowers themselves (Acharya et al., 2014; Gustafson et al., 2021; Sufi, 2009), but institutional lenders are less active in monitoring and more likely to rely on others to monitor (Berlin et al., 2020; Beyhaghi et al., 2019; Drucker & Puri, 2009). We investigate whether the main effect we document is larger for loan facilities held more by institutional lenders rather than banks. $ ^{19} $ We partition our treatment group into Institutional Lender and Bank Lender and interact these two indicator variables with Post. This partition regression result is shown in Column 2 of Table 4, Panel B. We find that a newspaper closure is associated with a 69.08 basis point increase in loan spread for institutional lender-dominated loan facilities, but only a 20.63 basis point increase in

loan spread for bank-dominated facilities. The coefficient difference is statistically significant at the 1% level, providing evidence consistent with the monitoring channel—when lenders cannot monitor by themselves, they rely more on local newspapers for monitoring.

To provide further direct evidence of local newspapers’ monitoring role, we consider lenders’ use of covenants for monitoring during the ex post loan duration phase (Ma et al., 2024; Wang & Xia, 2014). Specifically, we investigate whether local newspaper closures are associated with stricter loan covenants, which would be evidence of lender’s active monitoring after newspaper closures. $ ^{20} $ We report this result in Column 3 of Table 4, Panel B. Our variable of interest (Treat×Post) is significantly positive, indicating that lenders impose stricter covenants after the closure of a local newspaper and consistent with lenders relying on local newspapers’ monitoring of local firms. Overall, the tests in Table 4 provide evidence consistent with our arguments that local newspapers help produce and disseminate information about local firms and provide ongoing monitoring of local firms. Together, the roles played by local newspapers are incorporated by lenders in loan contracts.

## 5 | ADDITIONAL ANALYSES

### 5.1 | The effect of local newspaper closures on loan syndicate structure

We also consider the effect of local newspaper closures on loan syndicate structures. Given our arguments above, the closure of a local newspaper may reduce the available information set from lenders' perspectives, thus increasing information asymmetry between borrowers and lenders. We posit that this may lead us to observe that fewer lenders are willing to join loan syndicates and that borrowers will rely more on related lenders (i.e., lenders with which they have previously worked) to obtain loans. At the same time, a newspaper closure may cause lead arrangers to prefer that local participants join the loan syndicate to provide local information. To test these predictions, we investigate the effect of newspaper closures on the number of lenders in loan syndicates as well as the presence of related lead arrangers and local syndicate participants. We present the results in Table 5. Overall, our results are consistent with our predictions. While we find no effect on the number of overall lenders in loan syndicates (Column 1), we find a significant increase in the likelihood of related lead arrangers (Column 2) and local participants in the syndicate (Column 3). This table provides further evidence indicating that local newspapers do serve as local information providers in private lending.

We also consider the effect of local newspaper closures on other non-price loan terms. Specifically, we investigate the effect of closures on loan amount, loan maturity, and the number of financial covenants. We do not find significant effects, on average, for these non-price loan terms, which are untabulated for brevity. One possibility for these “no” results is that lenders view interest spreads, covenant strictness, and syndicate structures as the most cost-effective way to respond to local newspaper closures.

### 5.5 | Further additional analyses: Untabulated

We perform a variety of additional cross-sectional tests, untabulated for brevity, but which together increase the plausibility of our inferences. First, we examine whether a newspaper closure effect is stronger for firms located in counties where local neighborhoods rely more on a local newspaper. To test this, we (1) consider counties with different population sizes because counties, and their local firms, with a small population receive less attention from national newspapers and other media types (Seamans & Zhu, 2014) and (2) examine the differential effect of a newspaper closure on firms located in states with low versus high internet usage because internet usage is correlated with online news consumption (Gao et al., 2020). As predicted, we find that our effect is statistically and economically larger in counties with small populations and low internet usage. $ ^{25} $

Second, we investigate the role of firms' information environments and accounting quality in our setting. The effect of accounting quality on loan contracts has been widely tested in prior studies (Graham et al., 2008; Sunder et al., 2018; Zhang, 2008), in part because banks can use accounting information as a monitoring tool after loan issuance (Minnis & Sutherland, 2017). We identify firms with high bid-ask spreads, high abnormal accruals, and high accruals volatility as having "poor" information environments and accounting quality, respectively. We find that loan spread increases after a local newspaper closure are larger for firms with poor information environments and accounting quality.

Third, we investigate the role of external monitoring environments. We expect that the monitoring effect of local media will be smaller when the existing external monitoring is higher. We define firms with non–Big 4 auditors and low analyst following as having “weak” external monitoring (Chen et al., 2016; Coyne & Stice, 2018; Hallman et al., 2023). We observe a similar pattern of results—loan spread increases after a newspaper closure are higher for firms with weaker external monitoring environments.

Fourth, we examine whether newspapers disseminated more broadly have a larger impact. We classify the newspapers that closed in our sample into “established” and “young,” partitioning at the median on their length in circulation, and re-run our main tests. We find that the closing of an established newspaper has a significantly larger effect on interest spreads of local borrowers compared to a young newspaper.

In our last additional test, we examine whether local newspaper closures make it more difficult for local firms to obtain loan financing. Specifically, we examine whether the firms in our sample (i.e., firms that have borrowed over our sample period) are less likely to obtain a loan after a loan newspaper closure, and we find that borrowers are (1) significantly less likely to obtain a loan in the borrower-years after a newspaper closure and (2) total loan amounts significantly decrease following a newspaper closure.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

In this study, we show that shocks to the local newspaper industry significantly alter the information and monitoring environments of nearby firms. Specifically, we examine changes in bank loan contracts after borrowers experience a nearby local newspaper closure, and we find that the closure of a local newspaper leads to higher interest spreads for treatment borrowers. The economic magnitude of our results is significant—the closure of a local newspaper is associated with a 30.24 basis point increase in interest spread.

We argue for and provide evidence consistent with local newspaper closures driving this effect and their role in facilitating information and monitoring channels. For example, we find that the interest spread effect is stronger when there are fewer related lenders in the syndicate, when lenders have less prior lending experience in the local area, when the closed local newspapers are associated with increases in nearby misconduct cases, and for institutional lenders who rely more heavily on others for monitoring. Furthermore, we find that after a local newspaper closure, the frequency of loan amendments decreases, consistent with lenders having less information, and lenders impose stricter covenants in contracts, consistent with lenders seeking additional monitoring. Together, these and a variety of additional tests highlight local newspapers' important and separate information-generating and monitoring role in private debt contract design.

Our empirical strategy allows for strong identification. Specifically, our sample of newspaper closures is spread across time and geographic locations, and we compare the loans of borrowers located within 50 miles of a closed newspaper (treatment firms) to borrowers located 50–200 miles away from a closed newspaper (control firms). Furthermore, while local newspaper closures are not exogenous, we also use the entry of Craigslist into a local market as an exogenous shock for local newspaper closures, and we find a similar effect for the Craigslist.

induced newspaper closures on the loan spreads of local firms. We also test for changes in macroeconomic indicators before and after a local newspaper closure, and we find no evidence of a change in local economic conditions around a newspaper closure, providing further comfort that our inferences are warranted.

Our investigation is motivated by the ongoing public discussion about the future of traditional print media in society as well as the specific sources of information incorporated into loan contract design. Despite the current debate about the role of media, research on the effects of media, especially local media, on lending is limited. Our findings suggest that local media still play a significant role in capital markets, even in the internet era, as a disciplining device that reduces the concerns of nonlocal investors and also provides important incremental local information to nonlocal investors.
