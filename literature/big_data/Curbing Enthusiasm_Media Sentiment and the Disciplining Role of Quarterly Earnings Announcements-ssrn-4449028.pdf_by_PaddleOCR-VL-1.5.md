# Curbing Enthusiasm: Media Sentiment and the Disciplining Role of Quarterly Earnings Announcements

## ABSTRACT

A longstanding literature suggests that earnings provide the market with relevant information about firm performance, but one often overlooked benefit is their role in disciplining market expectations. This study examines the role of earnings announcements in constraining potential mispricing associated with firm-specific media sentiment. We show that media sentiment driven quarterly returns (orthogonal to risk factors and fundamental news) largely reverse when public earnings are released. Our results suggest that quarterly earnings announcements play an important role in reducing media sentiment-related mispricing.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The role of earnings in conveying new information to the market has been a central focus of accounting research for decades. In particular, a longstanding literature suggests that earnings announcements provide the market with relevant information regarding firm performance (Beaver 1968). However, one often overlooked role of earnings releases is their ability to serve as a disciplining mechanism for market expectations. $ ^{1} $ In this study, we investigate whether earnings announcements play a disciplining role with respect to sentiment stemming from firm-specific media coverage. We document that quarterly media sentiment is negatively associated with earnings announcement returns, which suggests that earnings announcements play an important role in constraining potential mispricing related to media sentiment.

Our results are best explained through the lens of recent asset-pricing models that recognize that a large number of investors with heterogeneous information and horizons characterize modern markets (e.g. Allen, Morris, and Shin 2006). Such Higher Order Belief

frameworks suggest that some of these investors are more interested in predicting future price movements than enacting trades based on traditional valuation models. In particular, some transient short-term investors are rationally willing to temporarily deviate from fundamentals to obtain returns based on predictions of short-term price movements. In these models, public signals (in our setting media articles) play an important role in causing deviations between prices and fundamentals, as they influence average investor trading in the near-term. Because some investors with short horizons are only interested in predicting near-term price movements, they rationally overweight such public signals in their trading. This leads to a prediction of short-term price overreactions to these public signals. Consistent with this prediction, prior research suggests that media coverage can play a role in driving near-term price momentum that is detached from fundamentals (Hillert, Jacobs, and Müller 2014).

Importantly, due to coordination problems, these overreactions may not be corrected until another public event helps to synchronize investors trading in the opposite direction (Abreu and Brunnermeier 2002; 2003). We suggest that earnings reports, which are relatively more reliable (e.g., subject to review and audit), occur on a regularly scheduled basis, and have broad market awareness, provide such a coordinating event. This leads to our prediction that media sentiment is associated with temporary mispricing effects that are then curbed at the earnings announcement.

Prior literature has provided evidence linking media sentiment to sensationalism and investor overreactions (e.g., Hillert et al. 2014; Ahern and Sosyura 2015). $ ^{2} $ Further, the SEC has recently begun prosecuting hedge funds for manipulating firm media coverage to benefit from

short-term price movements (SEC vs. Lemelson 2018). While regulators have little control over the sentiment the media uses when covering a given firm, regulators do have control over the frequency and, to some extent, the content of earnings releases. Thus, we examine whether earnings releases help discipline potential mispricing stemming from media sentiment during the quarter.

Our sample consists of 108,666 firm-quarters from 2007 to 2017. For these firm-quarters, we obtain data from RavenPack on over 6.2 million media articles from thousands of media outlets. We first create a measure of daily media sentiment for each firm-day during the  $ [-60,-3] $ window, where day zero is the earnings announcement. We do this using RavenPack’s Event Sentiment Score (ESS), which we first adjust to be centered around a neutral sentiment of zero. $ ^{3} $ Next, we sum these adjusted-sentiment scores for all articles published about a firm on a given day to obtain our measure of daily firm-specific media sentiment. We then sum this daily media sentiment over the  $ [-60,-3] $ window to obtain our initial measure of firm-specific quarterly media sentiment. $ ^{4} $ For our initial analysis, we rely on this raw (unweighted) quarterly media sentiment measure and document a negative association between it and earnings announcement returns.

Next, we develop several novel measures of quarterly media sentiment to account for factors such as the influence (i.e., audience or impact effects) of the media outlet, as well as the novelty (i.e., lack of staleness) of individual media articles. Perhaps most importantly, we also develop a weighted measure of media sentiment that estimates the portion of quarterly returns

driven by media sentiment that is orthogonal to proxies for common risk factors and fundamental news. This measure provides a clearer link between media sentiment during the quarter, returns on days during the quarter with media articles, and returns at the earnings announcement, helping us better identify the mispricing effects of quarterly media sentiment. While we acknowledge that it is challenging to control for underlying firm fundamental news perfectly, this method enables us to better isolate the incremental effect of media sentiment on stock prices. $ ^{5} $ Importantly, this method allows us to focus our measurement, as best as possible, on the portion of the stock return on a given day with media coverage that is indeed attributable to media sentiment, that is incremental to risk factors and fundamental news. Across these measures, we find consistent evidence of a negative relation between quarterly media sentiment or quarterly media sentiment-driven returns and earnings announcement returns. This finding suggests that earnings releases function as a disciplining mechanism for mispricing sentiment associated with media coverage.

We next test several predictions derived from higher order belief models. First, the higher order belief framework, as applied to our setting, predicts that some subset of the investor base likely overreacts to the sentiment of public media stories (e.g., retail investors). Accordingly, sophisticated short-term investors (e.g., transient institutional investors) that are primarily concerned with predicting price movements would be willing to let prices move away from fundamentals until a more verifiable public information event, such as the earnings announcement, occurs. We test this prediction by examining the strength of our results across investor type and find that our results are strongest when there are high amounts of both retail

investors and transient institutional investors trading during the quarter relative to cases where there are lower amounts of either of these investor groups (and importantly, we observe the most pronounced difference relative to when there are low amounts of trading by both). This evidence is consistent with the idea that there needs to be both unsophisticated investors, who are more likely to trade on media article sentiment, $ ^{6} $ and a sufficient number of transient institutional investors who follow these trades because they are interested in predicting short-term price movements (DeLong, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann 1990).

Further, based on the higher order belief framework we predict that when there is higher uncertainty in future cash flows, investors may be more likely to deviate from investing based on fundamentals and instead focus on predicting future price movements. Consistent with this prediction, we show that our results are more pronounced in firms with greater fundamental uncertainty. Similarly, we predict that earnings announcements will be more likely to serve a disciplining role when there are greater limits to arbitrage. Consistent with this idea, we find that our results are more pronounced when short-selling constraints are higher. Collectively, our evidence supports the notion that overreactions to the public signals provided by media articles persist until the earnings announcement, which serves as a coordinating event that helps synchronize investor trading, as predicted by the higher order belief framework.

We conclude our analyses with two final tests. First, we employ a mixed data sampling approach (MIDAS) to provide better identification and an alternative method of isolating that the return reversal at the earnings announcement date stems specifically from returns on those days during the quarter with media sentiment. A key advantage of the MIDAS approach is that it

enables researchers to incorporate data observed at higher frequencies than accounting data into a parsimonious model (Ball 2013; Ball, Gallo, and Ghysels 2019). In our setting, the MIDAS approach allows us to examine the impact of distinct daily returns during the quarter that are differentially impacted by media sentiment on an earnings announcement return. In other words, it allows greater identification by enabling us to examine the association between return days with media sentiment and the earnings announcement return. We find that stock returns on days during the quarter with extreme media sentiment have an incrementally negative relationship with earnings announcement returns compared to non-media days.

Finally, a potential concern with our study is that our results are influenced by media sentiment during the quarter preempting information disclosed at the earnings announcement. It is worth noting that if this sort of preemption is occurring in our setting, the expectation would be that there would be smaller responses to earnings announcements. In contrast, we observe oppositely signed earnings announcement returns, which helps mitigate this concern. To further mitigate this concern, we more directly examine price formation during the quarter leading up to the earnings announcement. Specifically, we examine whether quarterly media sentiment is associated with a measure of quarterly intraperiod price timeliness (IPT), calculated starting at the beginning of the quarter through one day after the earnings announcement (i.e.,  $ [-60,+1] $ event window). If media sentiment during the quarter leads to the pre-emption of earnings news, we expect to observe faster price formation during the quarter. However, we find no such evidence of quicker price formation during the quarter. Although it is challenging to definitively rule out the possibility that media sentiment can preempt earnings news, we find no evidence to support this scenario in our sample.

Overall, our collective evidence suggests that investors are influenced by the sentiment of the media towards a given firm, and more importantly, that quarterly earnings announcements play a disciplining role in constraining mispricing associated with this sentiment. These findings contribute to the literature on the value and role of earnings announcements in capital markets, as well as the literature on the role of the media as a market information intermediary. With respect to earnings, there is an ongoing debate about the role of earnings in capital markets and the value that quarterly earnings releases provide to market participants (SEC 2018). Many have called for an end to the practice of issuing quarterly earnings, arguing that their provision induces myopic behavior on the part of investors and managers. $ ^{7} $ While our results do not speak to the potential costs of requiring firms to report earnings on a quarterly basis, they do shed new light on one often overlooked benefit, the ability of earnings to constrain mispricing, in our setting mispricing related to media sentiment.

Our results also contribute to the media literature. This literature provides consistent evidence that media coverage and sentiment influence market activity. With respect to the relation between the media and earnings specifically, prior research has generally focused on how media coverage influences the investor reaction to the announcement (e.g., Bushee, Core, Guay, and Hamm 2010; Drake, Guest, and Twedt 2014; Guest 2021). In contrast, we study how the sentiment of the media influences investors throughout the quarter, and how the release of earnings helps discipline this relation. Our evidence thus improves our understanding of the nuanced relation between this important information intermediary and firm disclosures. Our

findings show that while the media can serve as a watchdog for the firm (Miller 2006; Chen, Cheng, Li, and Zhao 2021), financial reporting can also be useful as a disciplining mechanism in situations where sensational media sentiment may unduly impact investors.

## III. BACKGROUND, RELATED RESEARCH, AND PREDICTION

### Background and Related Research

Beginning with the Securities Act of 1934, the SEC has required all publicly traded U.S. companies to issue annual financial reports. This reporting frequency was increased to a semiannual basis in 1955, and to the current quarterly reporting period in 1970. While some have argued that modern technology would allow for, and markets would benefit from, even more frequent reporting (Elliott 2000; Litan 2002), others have expressed concern that the current system of quarterly reporting is detrimental to long-term market welfare. Consistent with these concerns, Kraft, Vashishtha, and Venkatachalam (2017) document that increased reporting frequency is associated with increased managerial myopia, as observed through large declines in investments. Further, Butler, Kraft, and Weiss (2007) find that mandated increases in reporting frequency have no impact on the speed with which investors are able to incorporate earnings information into price, while Kajüter, Klassmann, and Nienhaus (2019) show that small firms experience substantial net costs when required to increase reporting frequency. Due to such costs and unclear benefits, the European Union and the United Kingdom recently (in 2013 and 2014 respectively) stopped requiring companies to issue quarterly reports, and the SEC is currently studying whether to take similar action (SEC 2018).

Ball and Shivakumar (2008) examine how much new information is provided to the market by quarterly earnings announcements and conclude that it is a relatively small amount. This is also consistent with the evidence in Beyer, Cohen, Lys, and Walther (2010) that earnings

announcements provide only eight percent of all accounting-based information to investors. They conjecture that the primary economic role of earnings, therefore, lies not in providing new information to investors but in fulfilling other roles such as the disciplining of prior managerial disclosures. This role has primarily been studied through management earnings forecasts, which have been shown to generate large price reactions relative to earnings announcements (Pownall and Waymire 1989; Beyer et al. 2010). However, these reactions are dependent on the credibility of the forecasting firm or manager, which is developed as the market compares prior forecasts to realized earnings (Williams 1996; Yang 2012). The incentive financial analysts have to issue optimistically biased earnings forecasts is constrained in a similar way (Jackson 2005).

We argue that earnings releases may also play a role in disciplining information that does not originate from explicit earnings predictions. We examine this possibility in the context of firm-specific media sentiment. Before discussing prior research on the market effects of media sentiment, we highlight that the growing literature on the business press's role in capital markets provides substantial evidence of the positive market effects of media coverage. A number of these studies focus on media coverage of earnings announcements specifically. For example, Bushee et al. (2010) document a negative relation between earnings coverage and information asymmetry, while Drake et al. (2014) show that earnings coverage mitigates cash flow mispricing. Relatedly, Guest (2021) finds that media coverage of earnings is associated with improved price discovery, while Blankespoor, deHaan, and Zhu (2018) document positive liquidity effects. Beyond earnings announcements, media coverage has also been shown to be associated with increased detection of accounting fraud (Miller 2006), faster incorporation of voluntary disclosure news into price (Twedt 2016), and reductions in the cost of capital (Fang and Peress 2009) and earnings management (Chen et al. 2021).

While the above studies examine media coverage, or the level of attention a firm receives from the media, another line of research examines the tone, or sentiment, the media uses when discussing a firm. In the context of media sentiment specifically, reporters themselves claim to prioritize accuracy (Call, Emett, Maksymov, and Sharp 2021), and media sentiment has been shown to affect firms' cost of capital (Kothari, Li, and Short 2009), and interest rate spreads (Bushman, Williams, and Wittenberg-Moerman 2017), and to be useful in predicting their future earnings (Tetlock et al. 2008).

Despite these benefits provided by the media, a number of studies provide evidence that media sentiment can induce investor trading that can lead to temporary distortions in asset prices (Tetlock 2007; Tetlock 2011; Hillert et al. 2014; Ahern and Sosyura 2015) $ ^{8} $. Further, the SEC has recently begun to prosecute hedge funds for manipulating firm media coverage to benefit from short-term price movements (SEC vs. Lemelson 2018). While regulators have little control over the sentiment the media uses when covering a given firm, regulators do have control over the frequency and, to some extent, the content of earnings releases. Thus, we examine whether earnings releases help discipline potential mispricing effects stemming from media sentiment during the quarter.

#### Prediction

Recent asset-pricing models provide a framework for thinking about why the sentiment of media articles may potentially drive prices away from fundamentals and then reverse when a more verifiable public signal is released. In particular, these models have begun to recognize that a large number of investors with heterogeneous information and horizons characterize modern

markets (e.g. Allen et al. 2006). A Higher Order Belief framework suggests that some of these investors are more interested in predicting future price movements than enacting trades based on traditional valuation models. In particular, some transient short-term investors are rationally willing to deviate from fundamentals in order to obtain returns based on their predictions of short-term price movements (DeLong et al. 1990). In these models, public signals (in our setting media articles) play an important role in causing deviations between prices and fundamentals, as they are predicted to influence average investor trading in the near-term. Because many investors with short horizons are only interested in predicting near-term price movements, they rationally overweight such a public signal in their trading. $ ^{9} $

This combination of investor behaviors is predicted to lead to a short-term overreaction of the stock price to the public signal. Further, due to coordination problems, these overreactions may not be corrected until another, more verifiable, public event helps to synchronize investors' trading in the opposite direction (Abreu and Brunnermeier 2002, 2003). We suggest that an earnings announcement may provide such a coordinating event. The hard-information nature of earnings releases, the fact that the underlying numbers are ultimately subject to assurance, their regularly scheduled occurrence, and their broad market awareness are features that support their suitability as a coordinating event.

This leads to our prediction that media sentiment can result in temporary mispricing

effects that are eventually curbed at the earnings announcement. In sum, our central prediction is

that media sentiment can create lasting distortions in market expectations, and left unchecked,

this sentiment can distort the market away from firm fundamentals. However, the revelation of

earnings can help discipline this by realigning those expectations with the firm's economic reality and reducing related mispricing.

## III. DATA AND SAMPLE

Our sample of media articles comes from RavenPack. This database provides detailed analytics on every article written by over 6,400 media outlets. The use of this database thus alleviates concerns raised in Miller and Skinner (2015) regarding the generalizability of studies relying on a limited number of media sources. We retain those articles classified by RavenPack as fully relevant to the firm of interest (i.e., Relevance Score = 100), and we remove press releases and tabular material. Our removal of press releases when calculating media sentiment helps ensure that only sentiment derived from original media content, rather than content originating from the firm, is included in our measures. We obtain analyst and earnings data from I/B/E/S, financial statement, and earnings announcement data from Compustat and stock returns data from CRSP.

Our sample period begins in January 2007, when RavenPack coverage for the full set of media outlets becomes available, and runs through the end of 2017, for an initial sample of 173,247 firm-quarter observations. We require at least one media article published during the  $ [60,3] $ event window and one during the  $ [0,1] $ event window, where day zero is the earnings announcement date, which reduces the sample by 43,224 firm-quarters. We impose these requirements to ensure that our results are not driven by differences in the media’s decision to cover a firm or its earnings announcement. $ ^{10} $ We further require at least one analyst forecast of current period EPS in the 90 days ending three days before the earnings announcement date,

which reduces the sample by 20,454 firm-quarters. Last, we require specific data from Compustat, CRSP, and I/B/E/S to calculate various control variables, reducing the sample by 903 firm-quarters. The intersection of the RavenPack data with the I/B/E/S, Compustat, and CRSP data yields a final sample of 108,666 firm-quarter observations in our primary sample. We winsorize all continuous variables over the entire sample at the one and ninety-nine percent levels.

## IV. PRIMARY RESULTS

## Variable Measurement – Quarterly Media Sentiment

As previously discussed, for our measure of the media’s sentiment towards a given firm, we rely on RavenPack’s ESS score. $ ^{11} $ This variable has been used extensively in prior research (e.g., Dang, Moshirian, and Zhang 2015; Shi, Connelly, and Cirik 2018; Bushee, Cedergren, and Michels 2020; Rees and Twedt 2022). The ESS score as provided by RavenPack ranges from 0 (negative) to 100 (positive), where a score of 50 implies a neutrally toned article. This variable is determined based on training sets in which financial experts classify firm-specific events as conveying either positive or negative sentiment, and to what degree. $ ^{12} $ As a first step, we center the ESS variable around 0 by subtracting 50 from it. Next, we take the centered scores across all articles written about a firm over a given period of time (i.e., day or quarter), and we aggregate them by adding their scores together. We use these summed zero-centered ESS scores to calculate media sentiment during the quarter in several ways as discussed below. Appendix A provides more detailed descriptions of the calculation of each of these measures.

Our first measure of quarterly media sentiment is simply the sum of all zero-centered ESS scores from RavenPack for all articles published about the company during the [-60,-3] event window, where day zero is the earnings announcement date. This measure allows us to capture whether the media is, cumulatively, positive or negative when discussing a particular firm during the quarter, and also the extent to which that sentiment is reflected across a breadth of articles and outlets. Note that as we have centered the individual RavenPack ESS media sentiment scores on 0, this is conceptually distinct from the magnitude of media coverage, which we control for in all of our analyses. We label this variable Unweight Media Sent.

Second, whereas our initial measure equally sums the sentiment of all media articles regardless of their publication source, we derive a second measure that adjusts for the fact that different media outlets have different breadths of readership. We do this by developing a novel method of weighting each outlet in our sample based on rolling calendar quarter outlet fixed effect regressions to determine the impact of specific media outlets on share turnover. We detail this rolling estimation approach further in Appendix A. Ultimately, this approach yields an estimated discount factor based on the extent to which articles published by a particular media outlet (e.g., Wall Street Journal, Boston Globe, etc.) relatively impact investor trading in the prior quarter. We use these discount factors to weight the zero-centered ESS sentiment scores published about a company during the [-60,-3] event window, where day zero is the earnings announcement date, and then sum these weighted sentiment scores. We label this variable Outlet Impact Media Sent.

Third, we attempt to mitigate concerns about the impact of stale news through the use of another article weighting methodology. In this case, we rely on a variable provided by RavenPack called the Global Event Novelty Score, or  $ G\_ENS $. This provides a measure of how

new or novel an article is, where the first story (across all RavenPack sources) covering a firm-event receives a score of 100. Subsequent articles about the same event follow a decaying function whose values decline steadily down to 0. To identify more novel versus stale news, we weight each article by its G_ENS score (100 percent, 75 percent, etc.). Thus, the first article about an event is fully weighted, while subsequent articles receive relatively less weight. We label the resulting variable Article Uniqueness Media Sent.

Next, we combine these two weighted measures (Outlet Impact Media Sent and Article Uniqueness Media Sent) to create a measure of quarterly media sentiment that is weighted for both outlet impact and article uniqueness. We do this by first weighting each article's sentiment based on its outlet impact, and then by its article uniqueness. We label the resulting variable Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent.

For our final media sentiment measure, we take our combined weighted media sentiment variable at the daily level (i.e., daily media sentiment adjusted for outlet impact and article uniqueness) and use it to create a measure that estimates the portion of stock returns driven by media sentiment during the quarter, based on rolling annual regressions. Specifically, we estimate rolling regressions using daily stock returns, media sentiment, risk factor measures, proxies for firm cash flow news, and firm-initiated disclosures over the year period ending three days before the current quarter's earnings announcement. We use these regressions to estimate a beta coefficient which represents the elasticity of daily stock returns to daily media sentiment while controlling for risk factors, cash flow news, and firm disclosures.

Next, we apply this estimated beta coefficient to the actual daily media sentiment the firm receives during the quarter leading up to the earnings announcement (i.e., during the  $ [-60,-3] $ day window) to estimate the portion of stock returns during the quarter that can be plausibly

attributed to media sentiment (i.e., that is orthogonal to fundamental news released during the quarter). This approach, discussed in more detail in Appendix A, provides a clearer link between media sentiment during the quarter, returns on media days, and returns at the earnings announcement, helping us to better identify the mispricing effects of quarterly media sentiment. It thereby enables us to provide more direct evidence of potential overreactions to the sentiment of media articles during the quarter that reverse when firms release earnings. We label this variable  $ Q_{trly} $ Returns - Media Sent Driven.

## Quarterly Media Sentiment and the Reaction to Firm Earnings News

To test our prediction that the sentiment of media articles over the quarter can lead to investor overreaction that is corrected by the regular quarterly reporting of financial information, we examine whether there is a negative association between media sentiment during the quarter and the market reaction around earnings announcements. We test for this relationship by estimating the following least squares regression:

 $$ EA\ \left[0,1\right]Abn\ Return_{it}=\alpha+\beta_{1}Qtrly\ Media\ Sent_{it}+Control\ Variables+\varepsilon_{it}, $$ 

where the dependent variable, EA [0,1] Abn Return $ _{it} $, is the cumulative size-adjusted stock return during the [0,1] window around firm i's earnings announcement date for quarter t. We use size-adjusted returns in order to abstract away from any general market news that could impact the firm's return during its earnings announcement window. $ ^{13} $ Qtrly Media Sent is one of the five test variables described in Section IV and in Appendix A. A negative and significant  $ \beta_{1} $ would be consistent with our prediction that investors overreact to media sentiment during the quarter leading up to earnings announcements, and that the announcements play a role in reducing media sentiment-related mispricing.

In this test, we control for the level of quarterly media coverage (Number of Qtrly Articles), as well as the number of press releases (Number of Qtrly Press Releases) issued and 8-Ks filed (Number of 8-K Filings) by the firm during the quarter and the sentiment of the firm's press releases (Qtrly Press Release Sentiment) to help separate the sentiment of the media from

the firm and its economics (Chapman, Reiter, White, and Williams 2019). We further control for the level of media coverage during the earnings announcement period (Number of EA Articles) and the sentiment of that coverage (EA Media Sent) (Bushee et al. 2010). We also control for earnings news for the current quarter (Earnings Surprise) as well as any bundled guidance news about next quarter's earnings (Qtr Guidance News) or annual guidance news (Ann Guidance News). Additionally, we control for the following firm characteristics that have been shown to determine press coverage in prior research (Bushee et al. 2010): analyst following (Analyst Following), firm size (Ln(MVE)), and the book-to-market ratio (BTM). Standard errors are adjusted for heteroscedasticity and clustered by firm and calendar quarter. $ ^{14} $

Table 2 tabulates the results of estimating Eq. (1) on our sample of firm-quarter observations. Column 1 presents the results for our simplest measure of the sentiment of media coverage of the firm over the quarter prior to its earnings release (Unweight Media Sent). The results indicate a strong negative association between quarterly media sentiment and abnormal earnings announcement period stock returns (t-statistic: -9.17). In the next four columns we replace the test variable with one of the adjusted media sentiment variables constructed to address potential shortcomings in the measurement of quarterly media sentiment.

Specifically, in column 2, we replace the test variable with our measure of quarterly sentiment that weights articles for estimated outlet readership breadth (Outlet Impact Media Sent). The results indicate a strong negative association between Outlet Impact Media Sent and EA [0,1] Abn Return (t-statistic: -8.79). In column 3, we replace the test variable with our measure of quarterly sentiment that weights articles based on the uniqueness of the article

(Article Uniqueness Media Sent). The results similarly indicate a strong negative association between Article Uniqueness Media Sent and EA [0,1] Abn Return (t-statistic: -12.05). Next, in column 4, we replace the test variable with our measure of quarterly sentiment that weights articles based on both the estimated outlet readership breadth and the uniqueness of the story (Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent). The results similarly indicate a strong negative association between Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent and EA [0,1] Abn Return (t-statistic: -11.63).

In the final column of Table 2, we use our constructed measure of quarterly media sentiment derived from an estimate of how strongly stock returns during the quarter are driven by media sentiment (Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven). This approach provides a more direct test of whether there is evidence of overreaction to the initial sentiment of media articles during the quarter that reverses when the firm releases its financial results. Perhaps most importantly, as previously discussed this measure helps us to orthogonalize the measure to common risk factors and proxies for fundamental news. Consistent with the results in columns 1 through 4, the findings in column 5 indicate a strong negative association between Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven and EA [0,1] Abn Return (t-statistic: -8.21). Collectively, the results in Table 2 are consistent with the prediction that media sentiment drives market overreaction during the quarter that is curbed by the release of earnings at the end of the quarter.

##### Robustness Tests

Next, we conduct several analyses to examine the robustness of our primary results. First, to mitigate concerns about skewness in the quarterly media sentiment test variables, we transform each variable into a quintile rank. To ensure that we are not injecting any look-ahead bias into the creation of the quintile ranks, we first calculate the statistical distribution of each variable (e.g., Unweight Media Sent) from the prior calendar quarter. We then use these lagged

values to assign quintile ranks for the current period quarterly media sentiment measures. The results of re-estimating Eq. (1) with these quintile-ranked test variables are tabulated in Table 3 Panel A. Consistent with the findings in Table 2, the results continue to indicate a strong negative association between the five test variables and the stock market reaction at the firm's earnings report. These results help mitigate concerns that skewness in the quarterly media sentiment measures is unduly affecting the findings.

Further, while Eq. (1) includes controls for bundled guidance news that is released at the earnings announcement, we alternatively examine whether the results are robust to removing quarterly earnings observations where either quarterly or annual EPS guidance is bundled with the earnings report. Columns 1 and 2 in Table 3 Panel B display the results when Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent and Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven are the test variables, respectively. For parsimony, starting in Table 3 Panel B, we limit the presentation of the results to using Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent and Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven as the test variables. After removing these earnings announcement observations that are accompanied by bundled earnings guidance, we continue to find a strong negative coefficient on Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent and Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven. ### Cross Sectional Tests

As theorized by DeLong et al. (1990), sophisticated traders may be willing to allow market prices to move away from fundamentals if they can profit from these movements in the near-term. More specifically, if unsophisticated investors (e.g., retail investors) are likely to overreact to the sentiment of the news, then it may be in sophisticated investors' interest to trade with this expected momentum in the near-term. Prior research suggests that media articles stimulate retail trading and that this associated increase in retail trading can result in price overreactions (Tetlock 2011; Bushee et al. 2020). Accordingly, we examine whether our results are stronger when the quarter is characterized as having a high degree of retail and short-term institutional trading (i.e., transient investors).

To measure retail trading, we use the algorithm developed in Boehmer, Jones, Zhang, and Zhang (2021) and daily TAQ trade data to estimate the total dollar volume of trade initiated by retail investors, scaled by the total dollar volume of trade for the day to calculate the relative share of trade volume initiated by retail investors. We then calculate this measure for every day during the  $ [-60,-3] $ event window and average this over the period.

To proxy for quarters with relatively more short-term institutional trading, we merge the Bushee (2001) permanent institutional investor classifications with 13F filing data. $ ^{17} $ Next, we identify the 13F filing date closest to, but not greater than, the fiscal quarter beginning date, and the 13F filing date closest to, but not greater than, the fiscal quarter ending date. We then calculate the change in the percentage of shares owned by transient institutions over the quarter. We next calculate the absolute value of this change in transient institutional ownership. In addition to trader type, higher order belief theory predicts that the initial overreaction to public signals, like high sentiment media reports, will vary based on the fundamental uncertainty of the firm that is the subject of the media coverage. Specifically, in the model of Allen et al. (2006), traders price stocks around public signals by combining private and public signals. If fundamental valuation is more difficult, then traders find it difficult to generate precise private signals. Consequently, this theory predicts greater initial overreliance on the public signal when fundamental uncertainty is high (Cremers, Pareek, and Sautner 2021).

We then tercile rank this measure by calendar quarter and compare the top tercile firms to the bottom tercile firms. ## V. ADDITIONAL ANALYSES

In this section, we conduct two additional analyses that explore our primary findings in more depth. First, recall that the primary intent behind our Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven measure was to obtain a clearer link between media sentiment during the quarter, returns on media days, and returns at the earnings announcement, helping us to better identify the mispricing effects of quarterly media sentiment. As an alternative to this approach, we employ a mixed data sampling approach (MIDAS) to isolate that the return reversal at the earnings announcement stems to a greater degree from returns on days during the quarter with extreme media sentiment. A key advantage of the MIDAS approach as it applies to our setting is that it enables researchers to incorporate data observed at higher frequencies than quarterly accounting data into a parsimonious model (Ghysels, Santa-Clara, and Valkanov 2006). Although this MIDAS approach has been used in other disciplines to provide similar advantages, its advantages to accounting research have recently been demonstrated by Ball and Gallo (2018) and employed in several recent studies (e.g., Ball 2013; Ball et al. 2019).

In our setting, the MIDAS approach allows us to examine the relation between distinct daily returns during a firm quarter and the quarterly earnings announcement return. Specifically, we use this approach to focus on daily stock returns during the quarter when media sentiment is extreme (both positively and negatively). We then examine whether the relationship between stock returns on those days versus non-media days during the quarter is incrementally negatively.

autocorrelated with the quarterly earnings announcement stock return. In other words, it allows greater identification by enabling us to examine the association between return days with extreme media sentiment and the earnings announcement return.

We implement a MIDAS approach by ranking all firm-days with media coverage over the [-60,-3] event window into quintiles based on the daily value of Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent. Last, we sum the size-adjusted returns on trading days z and z+1 where day z was identified as an extreme media sentiment day. Additionally, we make sure not to double-count any overlapping daily return windows in measuring the variable. ## VI. CONCLUSION

We provide evidence consistent with the idea that sentiment originating from the media during the quarter can push prices away from fundamentals, and quarterly earnings announcements help to curb this source of mispricing. These effects are stronger for firms whose shares are traded by a relatively higher proportions of retail and transient institutional traders, those with more uncertain valuations, and those with higher short-selling constraints. These

novel findings help to showcase the important disciplining role of earnings announcements that is often overlooked.

While our study does not explicitly examine periods with different financial reporting frequencies, we believe these results help to identify a benefit of regular financial reporting that should be of interest to government regulators deciding whether to change the frequency of financial reporting in markets. Namely, regular financial reporting can help to calibrate investor expectations that can be unduly influenced by external sources that are largely outside the control of regulators, such as the media.

Our results also contribute to the literature on stock mispricing and correction. While prior theoretic literature predicts that mispricing can persist until a future event synchronizes investor trade in the opposite direction, little research has attempted to identify characteristics of efficient synchronizing events. Our study suggests that due to their “hard-information” nature and wide dissemination, earnings announcements possess characteristics that make them good empirical proxies for this largely theoretical construct. Future research could examine the cross-sectional characteristics of earnings announcements that make them more or less efficient synchronizing events.

# Appendix A Quarterly Media Sentiment Test Variable Construction

 $ \underline{\text{This appendix provides an explanation of the construction of the various quarterly media sentiment variables used in our study.}} $

#### Unweight Media Sent

For our initial measure of quarterly media sentiment, we first take the event sentiment score (ESS) provided by RavenPack for each article published by the media about the company during the  $ [-60,-3] $ event window, where day zero is the earnings announcement date. This score ranges from 0 (negative) to 100 (positive), with 50 being neutral. We adjust this score by subtracting 50 from the values in order to center the scores around zero (“zero-centered”). Next, we sum these zero-centered scores together to obtain our measure of quarterly media sentiment.

For example, suppose a company has four articles published about it during the  $ [-60,-3] $ window with the following ESS scores: 60, 46, 70, and 52. First, we adjust these scores by subtracting 50 from each of them, yielding the following adjusted scores: 10, -4, 20, and 2. Next, we sum these adjusted scores, which results in an ‘Unweight Media Sent’ of 28 (10-4+20+2).

### Outlet Impact Media Sent

This measure discounts the zero-centered RavenPack ESS scores by a computed adjustment factor for differences in the implied impact of the publishing outlet. To calculate this adjustment factor we rely on the economic intuition that if a publication outlet has more impact (e.g., a greater readership) a new article authored by it should drive more trading volume (all else equal).

More specifically, we use a sample of firm-day-article observations where a firm had a media article in RavenPack, and regress the firm's market-adjusted [0,1] day share turnover on firm, news category, and media outlet fixed effects. The implicit assumption in this model is that, on average and after controlling for firm and news category effects, turnover should provide an approximation for the attention (or readership) given to a particular news article relative to the articles of other outlets during that quarter.

Specifically, we estimate the following regression separately for each calendar quarter on the sample of firms with at least one media article during that quarter in RavenPack:

firm market-adjusted share turnover  $ [0,1]_{it}= $

firm fixed effects + article topic fixed effects + media outlet FE +  $ \varepsilon_{it} $,

where firm market-adjusted share turnover [0,1] is the sum of the firm’s share turnover (i.e., trading volume/shares outstanding) on the day the article is published (i.e., day 0) and the next trading day (i.e., day  $ t+1 $) adjusted by the average share turnover for all firms in the market to account for macro time effects. The model controls for firm fixed effects in case firm share turnover rates vary in the cross-section over the course of the quarter due to factors such as differences in liquidity. Next, we include fixed effects for the primary topic (i.e., RavenPack variable ‘category’) of the article (e.g., merger, product release, layoffs, etc.) for two reasons. First, these fixed effects help to control for different news topics on-average resulting in

heterogeneous trading reactions from investors. Second, including these fixed effects helps to control for the types of stories different news outlets choose to cover.

For our next, most important step, we calculate a media outlet specific fixed effect for the quarter that measures the on-average share turnover that an incremental story from a specific news outlet brings about after controlling for time, firm, and article topic effects. $ ^{20} $ We then rank these outlet specific estimated fixed effects by their magnitude each quarter. Then, we weight the sentiment of each media article in our sample by the relative rank of its estimated fixed effect from the prior quarter regression (i.e., to avoid any potential look-ahead bias). We use these weights to calculate a new measure of quarterly media sentiment (Outlet Impact Media Sent).

As an illustrative example of how these weights are determined and implemented, assume that after estimating eq. (a) BBC News is estimated to have the 500 $ ^{th} $ highest estimated fixed effect (where 1 $ ^{st} $ is the lowest) out of 520 total media outlets that we are able to estimate fixed effects for during the first quarter of 2013. We would thus use the following weight for BBC articles that quarter: [500 (BBC's rank) divided by 520 (the total number of media outlets with an estimated fixed effect)] = 96.2%. As such, when we aggregate sentiment up for individual firms during Q2 of 2013, we would multiply the zero-centered ESS sentiment scores provided by RavenPack by 96.2% for all articles published by BBC News during the second quarter of 2013.

### Article Uniqueness Media Sent

This measure of media sentiment discounts the zero-centered RavenPack ESS scores by a computed adjustment factor for the uniqueness of a news article. To do this, we rely on a variable provided by RavenPack called the Global Event Novelty Score, or  $ G\_ENS $. This provides a measure of how new or novel an article is, where the first story (across all RavenPack sources) covering a firm-event receives a score of 100. Subsequent articles about the same event follow a decaying function whose values decline steadily down to 0. To identify more novel vs stale news, we weight each article by its  $ G\_ENS $ score (100%, 75%, etc.). Thus, the first article about an event is fully weighted, while subsequent articles receive comparatively less weight. For example, suppose a company has a new article published concerning its performance and RavenPack assigns it a  $ G\_ENS $ score of 100. We would take the zero-centered RavenPack ESS score and give it full weight. However, if an article were published later in the day concerning the same issue and RavenPack assigns it a  $ G\_ENS $ score of 75, we would take the zero-centered RavenPack ESS score and multiply it by 75%.

### Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent

This measure of media sentiment incorporates the discount factors for article impact and article uniqueness (see prior two sections). For example, assume that an article is published by BBC News in the second quarter of 2013 and RavenPack assigns that article a G_ENS score of 75. Here we would take the zero-centered RavenPack ESS score and multiply it by 96.2% to account

for outlet impact (as hypothetically discussed above) and then further multiply this ESS score by 75% to adjust for article uniqueness.

#### Otrly Returns - Media Sent Driven

For our final measure of quarterly media sentiment, we develop and estimate a measure of the extent to which media sentiment during the quarter has influenced the firm’s stock returns. To do this we build upon the measure above that adjusted the firm’s zero-center RavenPack ESS scores for article impact and article uniqueness (Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent). Specifically, for the one-year horizon ending on day t-3 prior to the earnings announcement for the current quarter we calculate a firm-specific rolling regression that regresses a two-day stock return on daily media sentiment, common risk factors, and proxies for innovations in fundamental news. $ ^{21} $

Specifically, we estimate the following regression separately for each firm for the one-year window ending on day t-3, where day t is the earnings announcement for the current quarter:

 $$ \begin{aligned}risk-adjust return_{[z,z+1]}=\beta_{1}daily media sentiment_{i,z}+\beta_{2}mktrf_{z}+\beta_{3}hml_{z}+\beta_{4}smb_{z}\\+\beta_{5}umd_{z}+\beta_{6}revfcst curr_{z}+\beta_{7}revfcst next_{z}+\beta_{8}pr sentiment_{z}\\+\beta_{9}count8K_{z}+\varepsilon_{[z,z+1]},&e\end{aligned} $$ 

where risk-adjust return  $ [z,z+1] $ is the two-day stock return for the firm adjusted by the risk-free rate. Daily media sentiment is the sum of all zero-centered RavenPack ESS scores adjusted for article impact and article uniqueness (i.e., Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent) on day z. During days when there is no media coverage, Outlet & Article Wgt Media Sent are set equal to zero.

Mktrf, hml, smb, and umd are the daily Fama and French (1993) and Carhart (1997) risk factors from WRDS on day z. We also include several proxies to account for potential innovations in firm fundamentals. Specifically, we measure the change in the consensus EPS forecast for the current year from the prior day (i.e., day z-1) to the end of the current day (i.e., day z), scaled by the stock price at the end of the previous month (revfcst curr). Similarly, we measure the change in the consensus EPS forecast for the next year from the prior day (i.e., day z-1) to the end of the current day (i.e., day z), again scaled by price (revfcst next). We also control for the RavenPack ESS score of all press releases issued by the firm on day z (pr sentiment). Finally, we control for the number of 8-K filings issued by the firm on day z.

The estimated  $ \beta_{1} $ coefficient from eq. (b) represents the elasticity of daily stock returns to daily media sentiment while controlling for risk factors, cash flow news, and firm disclosures. Next, we multiply this estimated  $ \beta_{1} $ coefficient by daily media sentiment for every day the firm receives media coverage during the quarter prior to the earnings announcement (-60,-3). We then sum the daily products of  $ \beta_{1} $ and daily media sentiment over this window. This process yields an estimate of the portion of firm stock returns during the quarter leading up to the earnings announcement that were impacted by media sentiment. The resulting variable (Qtrly Returns - Media Sent Driven) is a measure of the effect of media sentiment on returns that is orthogonal to proxies for risk factors and fundamental news.
