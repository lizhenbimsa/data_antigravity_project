# Using GPT to Measure Business Complexity

Darren Bernard

Elizabeth Blankespoor

Ties de Kok

University of Washington

Sara Toynbee

The University of Texas at Austin

ABSTRACT: Business complexity involves important tradeoffs for managers and investors, but empirical evidence is limited by measurement issues. We construct and validate a measure of business complexity using a GPT model fine-tuned on narrative disclosures and inline XBRL tags. We first show that our measure is associated with slower price formation in capital markets, consistent with complexity increasing processing costs. Next, we apply our measure to study the complexity of debt, an economically important topic that encompasses a wide range of features. The results show that nonstandard debt features such as call and convertibility provisions underlie debt complexity. We also find that debt complexity correlates with more persistent interest expense and better performance when lending conditions worsen, suggesting it is in part an adaptive response to manage financial risk. Overall, our study underscores the tradeoffs of business complexity and provides a flexible measure of complexity for future research.

## I. INTRODUCTION

B usiness complexity reflects decision makers' difficulty in understanding the economic substance of the firm's transactions and financial position ("financial facts"). It is often cited both as a friction to decision-making in management and valuation and as an outcome of sound business decisions that generate benefits (e.g., Bushman, Chen, Engel, and Smith 2004; Loughran and McDonald 2024; Albuquerque, Carter, Guo, and Lynch 2025). For example, complexity in a firm's financing arrangements can arise to mitigate agency conflicts or financing uncertainty (Smith and Warner 1979), yet its presence likely increases processing costs for investors and managers. Despite the importance of assessing its tradeoffs, it is challenging to create a measure of complexity that encompasses a broad set of transaction categories across a broad set of firms. We leverage technological advances in natural language generation and financial reporting to address this challenge. We develop a measure of business complexity and examine its relation with investors' processing costs. We then use the measure's granularity to study why firms might have complexity in debt, a prevalent and economically important topic.

Our guiding principle for measurement is that complex financial facts are more difficult to understand and articulate. For example, we expect that it is more difficult to understand and explain the value and potential outcomes of a firm's debt when it includes nonstandard features such as conversion or call options, interest rate caps or floors, etc. Following this principle, we train a large language model (LLM) to explain what financial statement footnote numbers represent based on surrounding text. The LLM “reads” the words around a number, classifies the concept the number conveys, and scores its confidence about the classification. This approach is possible with the recent adoption of inline eXtensible Business Reporting Language (iXBRL), a financial reporting standard that identifies the fundamental accounting concept conveyed by a fact (i.e., number) in firms' financial reports. Because regulations require financial reports to fairly represent the firm's material economic activities, financial position, and performance, this approach allows us to evaluate material information for a range of potentially complex areas without directly examining underlying contracts.

We fine-tune (i.e., train) Meta’s Llama 3 open-source model on a random sample of 200,000 financial statement footnote sentences with iXBRL tags. We focus on footnotes because only the footnotes include iXBRL embedded within text. We choose an open-source model to allow easy sharing and updating of the model and results, and our fine-tuning approach narrows performance differences across models, reducing the importance of model choice. $ ^{1} $ The model classifies over 8 million iXBRL tags in the footnotes of over 50,000 quarterly or annual financial reports from July 2016 through April 2024 and provides confidence scores, which are based on token probabilities in the algorithm, that range from 0 to 1 for each classification (see Section III and Appendix B for detail). We measure the complexity of each fact as 1 minus the algorithm’s confidence in its classification of that fact. Intuitively, when a trained reader is more confident in their classification of what a number represents, it is likely a less complex concept.

After training our model, we document several features of the measure that help establish its construct validity. First, the model is more confident in its predictions of standard (versus custom) tags. This result is consistent with more complex concepts being less clearly classified and thus less likely to have a tag in standard taxonomies. Second, we find it is highly correlated with accuracy—if the model is more confident, it is more likely to accurately predict the tag the firm uses. $ ^{2} $ Third, we use the average complexity of the financial statement footnote facts to measure a firm's filing-level complexity. Our filing-level measure incorporates the breadth of each firm's material transactions (i.e., both stock and flow elements) and does not rely on researcher subjectivity to identify an ad hoc selection of complex items. We find that it is positively correlated with several existing measures of other complexity constructs at the filing level, including the length of a firm's financial report (Loughran and McDonald 2014), the Fog score (Li 2008), and the number of unique XBRL tags (R. Hoitash and U. Hoitash 2018), suggesting we are measuring an adjacent construct. $ ^{3} $

A key prediction of our complexity construct is that complex filings will be more difficult, and thus costly, for decision-makers to interpret. Thus, we investigate how our filing-level measure captures the processing costs imposed on one specific group of decision-makers: equity investors. To do so, we follow prior research and examine price responsiveness around the filing of firms' financial reports (e.g., Blankespoor, deHaan, and Marinovic 2020; Lee and Zhu 2022). We find that greater filing-level complexity is associated with a slower market price response to financial reports. This relation remains even after controlling for other complexity-related measures such as number of unique XBRL tags and filing length, and further, none of these other measures exhibit the same significant relation. This result helps validate our measure and suggests that it captures complexity that is relevant for capital market participants.

A key strength of our approach is that we measure complexity at the financial fact-level. This allows us to examine the sources and breadth of complexity using footnote categories. We find that collaborative arrangements, government assistance, and commitments and contingencies are among the more complex categories, and segment reporting, goodwill, and earnings per share (EPS) are among the less complex categories. These results reinforce the construct validity

of our measure. For example, complex topics often involve more variation in terms and restrictions, making it difficult to understand the economic substance of the transactions underlying the number.

Next, we use our category-level measure to examine an important issue—why complexity might arise despite its costs. We do so in the context of debt complexity for two reasons. First, debt is an economically important category that includes more tags than any other in our sample and exhibits meaningful variation in complexity. Second, debt has high dimensionality, and our measure provides an opportunity to consider the complexity of a wide range of debt features. This is important as we expect complexity to be increasing in nonstandard debt features (e.g., convertibility provisions, collateral requirements, call features). $ ^{4} $

Our core prediction in these analyses is that financial constraints engender debt complexity. Debt agreements arise from negotiations between borrowers and lenders to address adverse selection, mitigate risks of moral hazard, or accommodate different expectations about the borrower's performance (e.g., Smith and Warner 1979; Gorton and Kahn 2000). Financially constrained firms operate under more uncertainty about future performance, which exacerbates information problems (e.g., moral hazard due to the risk of asset substitution) and therefore increases the value of customized debt tailored to the circumstances of the borrower and the lender-borrower relationship (de Fontenay 2020). Determinants analyses support this prediction. For example, debt complexity is lower for large, profitable, steady-state, mature firms and higher for firms with greater return volatility. We then examine how debt complexity correlates with borrower outcomes. If complexity reflects more complete contracts (Eggleston, Posner, and Zeckhauser 2000), it could be beneficial for borrowers aiming for greater financial stability (e.g., Matvos 2013). Consistent with this notion, we find that interest persistence significantly increases with debt complexity, suggesting complex debt features tend to mitigate volatility in financing costs. We also find that firms with more complex debt had significantly higher stock returns when inflation rose along with expectations for interest rate hikes in late 2021 and early 2022, increasing borrowing costs for firms. Together, these findings are consistent with the view that complex debt is, in part, an adaptive response to financial constraints that is a negotiated solution to information problems in the lender-borrower relationship.

Like any new measure, ours has both limitations and opportunities. One key limitation is that several factors could create measurement error. For example, our GPT approach is designed to handle a variety of reporting approaches given its random and varied training sample, but fully disentangling business complexity from managerial reporting discretion is difficult (Leuz and Wysocki 2016) given we derive our measure from corporate reports. Other sources of measurement error arise from the mechanics of how we train the model. We assume that iXBRL tags in footnotes reasonably represent the firm's material transactions and thus sources of complexity. This assumption is necessary for our approach, but some transactions may not be well-represented in footnote information or related numerical tags. In a similar vein, XBRL regulation affects the measure. Specific areas where the XBRL taxonomy is less well-developed might be classified as more complex than they would if the taxonomy were clearer or more complete. This is a limitation of the measure, except to the extent clarity issues in the taxonomy are driven by the complexity of transactions in those areas. Taxonomy concerns are mitigated by the fact that the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) actively refines and maintains the taxonomy, and most public companies have extensive experience with XBRL during our sample period.

There are also opportunities. The trained algorithm can be applied in other areas of financial reports, such as the MD&A, or even conference call transcripts. Caution should be taken, however, as measurement error is likely to increase the more wording varies from the typical sentence structure of financial statement footnotes. Our measure is best suited to recent years as our training sample is limited to when firms report with iXBRL, which became mandatory in 2019 and later. However, our measure can be estimated for earlier time periods, particularly when combined with cross-sectional designs that account for time-series variation in estimation quality.

Our study provides several contributions. First, we build on the information processing literature. Given that our measure of business complexity more consistently explains stock price responsiveness than other complexity-related measures, our results speak to the types of complexity that impede processing and can therefore affect users' decisions (e.g., Hirshleifer, Lim, and Teoh 2009; You and Zhang 2009; Miller 2010; Engelberg, Reed, and Ringgenberg 2012; Lawrence 2013; Huang 2015; Frank and Sanati 2018; Wagner, Zeckhauser, and Ziegler 2018). Our descriptive evidence also highlights topic areas that are more complex, which could help firms, standard setters, and regulators target efforts to reduce complexity for financial statement users.

Second, we contribute to the accounting and finance literature on debt. Our approach adds to recent efforts to understand features of debt beyond standard elements such as interest rates, covenants, and duration (e.g., Badawi,

Dyreng, de Fontenay, and Hills 2022; Ambrose, Han, Korgaonkar, and Shen 2024; Murfin and Sun 2024). For example, Murfin and Sun (2024) explore the evolution of boilerplate in credit agreements using natural language processing techniques, and Badawi et al. (2022) examine the use of accounting addbacks to contractual ratios. Unlike these papers, we measure the complexity of financial statement footnote descriptions to construct our measure, not the underlying transactions or contracts themselves. Our results also highlight substantial variation in debt complexity across firms and suggest that complex features of debt arise, in part, as a way for more constrained firms to manage financial risk, building on studies that examine the efficiency of contract completeness (e.g., Smith and Warner 1979; Gorton and Kahn 2000; Tirole 2006).

Finally, we provide important methodological contributions. We build on literature that uses textual analysis of firm disclosure to measure underlying economics (e.g., Hoberg and Maksimovic 2015; Hoberg and Phillips 2016), using a novel technique to capture an elusive construct at a finer level than other studies. Future research can use our measure to study topics that require a proxy of complexity for one aspect of the firm, building on recent research that focuses on specific types of complexity (e.g., Albuquerque et al. 2025). We are also one of the first papers in the accounting and finance literature to illustrate several uses of GPT algorithms for modeling complex narrative disclosure tasks: capturing the confidence of complex textual prediction and creating alternative forms of the same content (e.g., Bai, Boyson, Cao, Liu, and Wan 2023; Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev 2024; Jha, Qian, Weber, and Yang 2024). Our examples convey opportunities using GPT models and provide guidance to researchers implementing similar techniques (de Kok 2025). Finally, studies of narrative disclosures are beginning to link numerical and qualitative content, suggesting an important contextualizing role for qualitative disclosures (e.g., Ahn, R. Hoitash, and U. Hoitash 2022; Allee, C. Do, and H. Do 2023; Burd, Casi, Grossetti, and Lisowsky 2025; Kim and Nikolaev 2024). We go further by training an algorithm to proxy for sophisticated interpretation of numerical concepts from narrative text.

## II. BACKGROUND ON BUSINESS COMPLEXITY

##### Definition and Adjacent Constructs

We define business complexity as a decision-maker's difficulty in understanding the economic substance of the firm's transactions and financial position. This definition has several important features. First, given that processing costs impede understanding (see Blankespoor et al. 2020), our definition has clear parallels to the definitions of computational complexity in computer science and contract complexity in law (Eggleston et al. 2000; Arora and Barak 2009). Second, business complexity reflects the complexity of the material features of many underlying transactions and contracts. These different transactions and categories of transactions can exhibit substantially different levels of complexity (e.g., Skinner 2024). This means that firms may have varying levels of overall complexity, or when comparing firms with the same level of overall complexity, the sources of complexity may differ. For instance, a firm with especially complex financing activities (e.g., due to the use of debt with embedded options, guarantees, etc.) could simultaneously have simple compensation or investment practices.

Finally, business complexity is conceptually related to, but does not fully overlap with, other types of complexity such as financial reporting and valuation complexity. Business complexity is itself an important determinant of some of these adjacent forms of complexity. For example, difficult-to-understand transactions make financial reporting more challenging and the company more difficult to value. However, both reporting complexity and valuation complexity are also a function of other factors. Reporting complexity is a function of the complexity of relevant accounting standards and the level of reporting discretion. $ ^{5} $ Valuation complexity requires users to not only understand the economic substance of business and contract characteristics, but also glean their subsequent implications (e.g., long-term revenue prospects). Business complexity is less clearly connected to some other forms of complexity, such as product and process complexity or organizational complexity. For instance, although a semiconductor foundry's products and processes are clearly more complex than those of a business that operates retail stores, the commitments, financing, compensation arrangements, etc. of the retail business could be more complex than those for the foundry. Similarly, more layers of management—one element of organizational design that increases organizational complexity—does not necessarily make it more difficult to understand the firm's transactions and financial position.

#### Implications for Empirical Approach

Prior studies develop and examine proxies for several types of complexity adjacent to business complexity. Bushman et al. (2004) and many later studies (e.g., Ashbaugh-Skaife, Collins, Kinney, and LaFond 2009; Doyle, Ge, and McVay 2007; Cohen and Lou 2012) proxy for organizational complexity based on the firm's revenue concentration across (or number of) industry and geographic segments. Loughran and McDonald (2024) use a text-based measure of valuation complexity based on a list of words that appear in at least 5 percent of all firm filings, reduced by a penalized regression approach. Hoitash and Hoitash (2018) measure accounting reporting complexity (ARC) using the count of monetary items disclosed via XBRL within company filings.

We build on these studies by constructing a measure of business complexity that reflects the key features of our definition and, compared to other complexity-related measures, allows for a finer level of analysis. In particular, we develop our measure at the fact-level, not the firm-level or even transaction-level, using the firm's complete description of the fact in the text of the financial footnotes. Thus, like many adjacent studies, we use financial reporting output to construct our measure. Our assumption in doing so is that regulations require financial reports to fairly represent the firm's material economic activities, financial position, and performance, allowing us to evaluate material information for a range of potentially complex areas without directly examining underlying contracts. Unlike related studies, our approach allows for complexity to vary both by and within transaction category and does not impose researcher subjectivity around the source of the complexity (e.g., selecting the types of transactions that are complex). For example, using our approach, the type of transaction can affect complexity, following the intuition that more unusual underlying transactions tend to impose greater processing costs—i.e., uniqueness matters. At the same time, uniqueness is not a sufficient statistic for complexity. Our measure should increase to the extent specific economic features that add complexity are discussed in text, holding constant the frequency of a given type of transaction. For instance, not all inventory write-downs are equally complex, and the language used to describe an inventory write-down for one firm helps distinguish its level of complexity from the complexity of a write-down for another firm. Similarly, transactions discussed in the text that require users to have (or develop) a deep knowledge base to understand the underlying economic substance should also lead to high complexity, again holding constant their uniqueness (e.g., pension liability disclosures).

## III. CONSTRUCTING A MEASURE OF COMPLEXITY

#### XBRL and Sample Construction

The introduction of XBRL requirements for financial statements and footnotes of U.S. public issuers in 2009 brought the promise of low-cost, machine-readable financial information that enables seamless comparisons across firms and time. The power of this new disclosure approach comes from its standardized financial reporting structure, where different firms choose the same XBRL element for a given piece of financial information, regardless of how they portray that information in their financial statements (e.g., Blankespoor, Miller, and White 2014; Blankespoor 2019). In this way, XBRL tags provide a way to measure the economic substance of a firm's transactions, events, and circumstances across a large cross-section of firms.

The adoption of iXBRL increases the contextual information of XBRL tags by connecting tags with the surrounding narrative disclosures. Prior to iXBRL, filers would provide their standard HTML financial statements and separate XBRL files. Users could download the XBRL metadata and load it in their software of choice, but it was difficult to link XBRL tags to specific locations in financial reports. iXBRL addresses this challenge by embedding the tag information directly in financial reports. We use iXBRL to identify the exact location of the tag and surrounding text.

Because our empirical approach requires iXBRL filings, we begin our sample construction by downloading all iXBRL quarterly and annual filings for U.S. public companies from the SEC's EDGAR website. iXBRL was required to be adopted for financial statements and footnotes on a staggered basis depending on a firm's accelerated filer status, with the first mandatory set including filings for fiscal periods ending on or after June 15, 2019. However, some firms voluntarily adopted iXBRL earlier than required. Therefore, the filings in our sample were filed between July 1, 2016, and April 30, 2024. We focus on financial statement footnotes to train the model and generate our measure because only the footnotes include iXBRL tags embedded within text.

We also require data from other sources. We use Compustat for financial statement information, including firm characteristics such as size and segments. We use CRSP for stock price and returns information, I/B/E/S for analyst following, and Thomson Reuters for institutional ownership. Finally, we use Audit Analytics for accelerated filer status. We list specific data sources for each variable in Appendix A. Our baseline sample covers 58,148 financial filings (10-K and 10-Q filings) with over 8 million tag instances in the financial statement footnotes, for 4,795 firms. However, the number of observations in our tests varies based on specification and data availability. We outline our sample selection process in Table 1.

### Large Language Model

To derive a measure of complexity, we aim to identify variation in users’ difficulty of understanding concepts, where iXBRL tags name the concepts for us. $ ^{6} $ Thus, we require a way to predict these concepts. One challenge with predicting iXBRL tags is that there are many standard tags (around 17,000 including deprecated legacy tags) and an infinite possible set of custom tags. In this context, traditional machine learning algorithms are not well-suited, as they require a fixed set of tags, struggle with the breadth of possible tags, or need an infeasible amount of training data. $ ^{7} $ Further, understanding and interpreting text surrounding a number is a challenging task that requires a model with state-of-the-art language understanding capabilities. $ ^{8} $

LLMs use sophisticated multidimensional logic as opposed to relying solely on simple heuristics and correlations, and the resulting model can be adapted to perform a wide range of tasks with state-of-the-art performance (de Kok 2025). Ultimately, we fine-tune the open-source Llama 3 8b model by Meta for two reasons. Further, by owning the model weights, we and other researchers can expand or update the complexity dataset in future years. Second, at the time of fine-tuning, Llama 3 8b was the most capable open-source LLM that was fully fine-tunable using generally available hardware (H100 GPUs in this case). $ ^{10} $ Of the fine-tuned models we test (Microsoft's Phi-2 and Phi-3, Google's Gemma 2b, OpenAI's GPT-3 Babbage, and Meta's Llama 3 8b), untabulated results show that

We fine-tune the Llama 3 8b model on 200,000 examples of financial statement footnote excerpts with embedded iXBRL tags. We use 200,000 sentences in our training sample because exploratory analyses indicate the marginal benefit of increases in the training sample size (i.e., greater breadth of tags the model sees) diminishes beyond this point, and larger training sample sizes are more costly. $ ^{11} $ We then provide the fine-tuned model with narrative text from the full sample of footnotes excluding the company-provided iXBRL tags and have it classify the concept of the numerical content. The model's confidence in its classification is the main input to our complexity score. $ ^{12} $ We detail our prediction pipeline and fine-tuning procedure in Appendix B.

#### Complexity Measure

We construct our measure of complexity based on the LLM's confidence in its predictions. Specifically, for every prediction (i.e., fact), we calculate complexity as 1 minus the confidence score of the algorithm for its prediction. This measure identifies cross-sectional variation in complexity and as we discuss and illustrate further below, can be evaluated at various aggregation levels. We do not contend that the measure's level represents the precise complexity faced by any particular user nor does it have a technical interpretation—e.g., a confidence score of 95 percent does not have an interpretation akin to 95 percent confidence in frequentist statistics. Also, if we were to adjust our training sample to attempt to align the model's knowledge with a particular user (e.g., use an industry-specific training model to resemble industry-specialized expertise), we would expect similar cross-sectional variation in complexity even if the measured level of complexity changed. Thus, we identify and validate variation in complexity driven by differences in financial facts and leave examination across users for future research.

#### Fact-Level Descriptive Evidence

Before analyzing complexity at different aggregation levels, we note several properties of the fact-level complexity scores that support the construct validity of our measure. We find that fact-level confidence scores have a 0.75 correlation with accuracy, measured as whether the model's tag prediction is identical to the tag used by the firm. This high correlation is consistent with the confidence score being a reasonable assessment of model ability, while still capturing the difficulty of understanding the concept regardless of whether it exactly matches management's classification. We also find that the model has 90 percent confidence when the firm uses a standard tag, compared to 78 percent confidence when the firm uses a custom tag, on average. The model's lower confidence for custom tags is intuitive to the extent these tags reflect more unique and therefore complex concepts. At the same time, uniqueness is neither necessary nor sufficient for complexity. Intuitively, if the text around a tag provides context that affects the difficulty of understanding the transaction, our measure would capture this whereas tag uniqueness would not. Empirically, the correlation between tag complexity and uniqueness (i.e., the logged frequency of each tag in the sample multiplied by -1) is 0.22, indicating partial but incomplete overlap.

Given that the model uses surrounding text to form its prediction, we examine how sensitive the confidence scores (and thus our measure of complexity) are to textual readability. Although the model is trained on sentences that often do not use plain English and on iXBRL tags that often consist of complicated words, it may still more easily process and interpret “plain English” text, such as short sentences written in active voice. If so, our measure could be overly affected by linguistic features of readability potentially unrelated to business complexity (e.g., managerial discretion in writing style). To mitigate this concern, we randomly select 250 footnote sentences to examine how readability affects our measure. We first feed each sentence into the GPT-4 API with a prompt asking GPT to simplify the sentence. We then calculate readability measures (i.e., Fog and Bog scores) for the original and simplified sentences and feed both through our LLM to calculate the model’s tag prediction confidence. The results, presented in Figure 1, show that the GPT-4 model dramatically reduces the Fog and Bog of the sentences, but the LLM exhibits somewhat lower confidence.

## IV. FIRM-LEVEL BUSINESS COMPLEXITY

##### Motivation

We average the fact-level complexity estimates within a filing to form a filing-level complexity score (Complexity), where a filing with a high score contains a larger proportion of hard-to-process financial facts. This measure has a mean of 0.118 and appears well-behaved, exhibiting no obvious skewness or floor or ceiling effects (see Table 2). We conduct a series of tests with this filing-level measure, in part because most existing complexity-related measures are constructed at the firm-level (e.g., Loughran and McDonald 2014, 2024). We first examine broad financial characteristics as determinants. This evidence helps characterize the features that explain overall business complexity in both the cross-section and time-series. Then, we test whether our measure captures users' difficulty of understanding the firm's business transactions and financial position by examining the speed of price response to financial filings.

#### Descriptive Statistics

To help contextualize the firm-level complexity measure, we correlate it with measures of adjacent forms of complexity (untabulated). We find positive but modest correlations with: Hoitash and Hoitash's (2018) measure of the number of unique XBRL tags in the financial statement footnotes (0.25), number of sentences in the filing (0.33), and the Fog score (0.15). $ ^{14} $ However, it is negatively correlated with the number of operating and geographic segments (-0.19), which is perhaps not surprising given our measure's focus on broader sources of complexity and concerns about segment measurement (Botosan, Huffman, and Stanford 2021; Loughran and McDonald 2024).

We also illustrate variation in complexity aggregated to the industry level. Figure 2 presents a box and whisker plot summarizing the average filing complexity by industry (Fama-French 30), with industries sorted from most to least complex. Each box represents the interquartile range of complexity for the industry, and whiskers present the range of complexity, excluding extreme values. We find that complexity is highest in industries such as natural resources, financial services, healthcare, tobacco products, and utilities. Complexity is lowest in industries such as retail and apparel, business and professional services, and wholesale trade. This sorting of industries is broadly consistent with our expectations, given that more complex industries tend to have unusual regulatory oversight and unique contracting arrangements (e.g., financial services, utilities, and natural resources).

##### Price Responsiveness

A key implication of our definition of complexity is that complex financial facts require users to incur greater processing costs. To the extent these processing costs affect investors' allocation of limited processing capacity, our measure of complexity should be associated with outcomes indicating impaired or incomplete processing (see Blankespoor et al. 2020). In this vein, a fundamental test to both validate our measure and assess complexity's implications for capital markets is whether it is associated with the speed of investors' price response to a firm's filing.

To measure price responsiveness, we follow Blankespoor et al. (2020) and use intraperiod efficiency (IPE). This measure captures the speed with which the released information is reflected in returns after the filing (and penalizes for overreactions and reversals), akin to similar measures in Butler, Kraft, and Weiss (2007); Bushman, Smith, and Wittenberg-Moerman (2010); Wagner et al. (2018). We begin by assuming that the bulk of the price response occurs within six trading days starting on the filing date  $ (0,+5) $ and estimate the proportion of the cumulative abnormal return recognized at the end of each day. $ ^{16} $ Following prior literature, we then estimate the area under the curve, with larger areas (and thus IPE measures) corresponding to faster price discovery.

Table 4 regresses  $ IPE_{5} $ on our filing-level complexity measure and control variables. We include controls for firm financial characteristics such as size, market-to-book, performance (ROA, Loss), analyst following, and institutional ownership, which are likely correlated with complexity and the information environment that shapes price formation around a firm's filing. Further, like prior work (e.g., Blankespoor, deHaan, and Zhu 2018), we control for several factors likely related to processing speed, including the time between the end of the quarter and the date of the filing (FilingLag), the market price of the stock (Price), and indicators for whether the filing is released on a Friday (Friday) or concurrent with the firm's earnings announcement (Concurrent). All specifications include form type (10-K, 10-Q) and industry and calendar-quarter fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered by firm.

As shown in column (1), Complexity is significantly negatively related to  $ IPE_{5} $, consistent with our measure capturing the processing costs that investors face when attempting to understand the firm's economic transactions. In terms of economic significance, the coefficient of -0.040 implies a 7.9 percent decrease in the speed of price discovery (relative to the unconditional sample mean) between filings in the bottom and the top deciles of complexity.

An important question is whether existing measures that capture adjacent types of complexity (e.g., organizational complexity and reporting complexity) exhibit the same relations and if so, whether our complexity measure provides incremental explanatory power. For example, prior research has found some evidence that annual reports with more words or less readable words have greater long-run price drift, suggesting these other proxies might have a relation with short-run price responsiveness (You and Zhang 2009; Callen, Khan, and Lu 2013). Thus, in column (2), we add other measures of firm-level complexity commonly used in prior literature: the number of segments, number of unique tags, number of sentences in the filing, and Fog (all decile ranked to facilitate interpretation). Our measure remains significantly negatively related to the speed of price discovery. In contrast, the alternative measures have no significant relation, except for the number of segments, which positively predicts the speed of the price response.

In columns (3)–(6), we re-estimate these specifications using two alternative measures of price responsiveness. In columns (3) and (4), we examine  $ IPE_{11} $, which allows the price response to occur over 12 trading days starting on the filing date  $ (0,+11) $. In columns (5) and (6), we follow Lee and Zhu (2022) and examine \%Ret5, which equals the proportion of the 12-day buy-and-hold abnormal returns that is realized within the first five days after the earnings announcement. Our results are similar using either of these alternative measures. In contrast, the other complexity measures do not have consistently significant relations across specifications.

Overall, the results suggest that firm-level business complexity captured by our measure impedes price formation, consistent with the intuition that business complexity leads to valuation complexity. In addition, the significant negative relation, which holds even when controlling for proxies of adjacent constructs, underscores the incremental contribution from our measure’s ability to capture complexity that is relevant to decision-makers such as capital market participants.

### Debt Complexity

##### Motivation

In our last set of analyses, we illustrate the benefits of a category-level complexity measure by exploring why some firms have complex debt, given our evidence and prior literature that suggests complexity can be costly (e.g., Hoitash and Hoitash 2018; Ghent, Torous, and Valkanov 2019; Loughran and McDonald 2024; Albuquerque et al. 2025). We define debt complexity as the difficulty a user would have in understanding a firm's financial structure and obligations to debtholders based on the financial statement footnotes. $ ^{18} $ For example, whereas discussion of fixed-rate 20-year corporate bonds is relatively easy for a user to understand, discussion of debt that includes more, and nonstandard, features, is more difficult to understand (e.g., convertible debt instruments with capped calls and embedded interest rate swaps).

Although our measure can be used to examine why complexity arises in any transaction category, we focus on debt complexity for two reasons. First, Table 5 shows that the debt category has the largest number of tag instances in our sample and the second largest number of total unique tags (behind accounting policies), underscoring its economic importance and significant heterogeneity. Second, although firms take on varying degrees of debt complexity, most of the existing debt literature focuses on features that are easier to observe and quantify (e.g., principal amount, interest rates, duration, use of covenants) (e.g., DeFond and Jiambalvo 1994; Sweeney 1994; Beneish and Press 1995; Nikolaev 2010; Christensen and Nikolaev 2012; Demerjian 2011, 2017). These elements are clearly important, but they are typically only a subset of the terms. Our approach enables us to build on a small but growing literature that explores terms and features left unstudied by most academic work due to the difficulty of gathering and quantifying them (Graham and Leary 2011; Badawi et al. 2022; Ambrose et al. 2024; Murfin and Sun 2024).

### Sources of Debt Complexity

We first provide visibility into sources of debt complexity by examining how various debt features relate to overall debt complexity. Specifically, we use keyword searches of the original iXBRL tags to identify the presence of ten categories of debt features that we expect could be more complex for users to understand, encompassing convertibility and call features, interest rate caps and floors, and others. We provide the list of keywords, the percent of filings with each feature, and the average complexity of tags that include these words in Appendix D. The average complexity of debt tags is 0.110 (see Table 5), whereas the average complexity of tags in nine of the ten categories (excluding covenants) is substantially higher, ranging from 0.145 to 0.302. $ ^{19} $ We then examine how these features are associated with debt complexity at the filing-level by regressing the firm's overall debt complexity (measured at the filing-level) on the presence of each category, using either the percent of debt tags with each feature (columns (1) and (2)) or an indicator for the presence of one or more debt tags with that feature (columns (3) and (4)). The results, which we present in Table 6, indicate that, except for covenants, the features are generally positively and significantly related to debt complexity. Overall, this evidence illustrates sources of debt complexity, underscoring that nonstandard features make it more difficult for users to understand a firm's debt structure obligations via the debt footnote.

#### Tradeoffs of Complex Debt

On the surface, debt complexity is costly. It requires borrowers to incur transaction costs and navigate provisions as they maximize firm value via their operational and capital structure choices. For example, a firm with debt that includes a convertibility provision must incorporate the possibility of lenders converting debt to equity when assessing the desirability of a proposed project, and a firm with debt that includes covenants with deductions must assess whether a proposed equity issuance would trigger technical default. More debt features (and interactions between them) increase the challenge of understanding and articulating the implications of those features for firm value under different states of the world.

Theory suggests that complex debt arrangements and more complete debt contracts can help resolve agency conflicts between equity holders and creditors (e.g., Barnea, Haugen, and Senbet 1980; Haugen and Senbet 1981; Green 1984) or allow firms to avoid other costly outcomes such as higher or more variable interest rates (e.g., Smith and Warner 1979; Graham and Harvey 2001; Matvos 2013). For example, complex features that give the lender some upside or additional protection (e.g., quicker transfer of control rights under bad outcomes) likely benefit the borrower by increasing the amount of credit they can access or providing other advantages. $ ^{20} $

The benefits of complex debt could be particularly valuable when firms are more financially constrained and face more uncertainty about future performance. Such firms have greater uncertainty about their ability to invest, get new financing, or even survive downturns, implying lenders to these firms face greater adverse selection and risks of moral hazard via asset substitution. Uncertainty could also create heterogeneous expectations among lenders and borrowers. $ ^{[21]} $ These factors increase the value of customized debt to both parties because customizations mitigate negative effects of information frictions in contracting and provide opportunities for bargaining gains (e.g., Smith and Warner 1979; Matvos 2013). For example, financially constrained borrowers are more likely to prioritize features that increase predictability of financing costs. Or, financially constrained borrowers may find that lenders are more willing to provide credit if the debt includes features such as convertibility, and borrowers can in turn include capped calls to limit dilution risk. Based on the above discussion, we expect that firms with financial constraints have more complex debt, in part to ease these constraints and mitigate future financing risk.

### Firm-Level Determinants of Debt Complexity

We examine the broad financial characteristics associated with debt complexity, empirically testing the arguments in Section V that complexity is likely to appeal to financially constrained borrowers. We present the results in Table 7, column (1). $ ^{22} $ Perhaps the most striking result relates to firm size. In contrast to the results in Table 3, size has a strong negative association with debt complexity, underscoring that the determinants of complexity for a given category can differ from overall complexity. This finding also contrasts with work that documents a strong positive relation between size and adjacent measures of overall firm complexity (e.g., Hoitash and Hoitash 2018; Loughran and McDonald 2024). The results further illustrate that, like overall business complexity, debt complexity declines with profitability, analyst coverage, and institutional ownership, but increases with return volatility. Mature firms appear to have the lowest average debt complexity, whereas new (“introduction”) and end-of-life (“decline”) firms have higher debt complexity.

Overall, we interpret the results as evidence that debt complexity is greater for firms with characteristics typically associated with credit risk and financial constraints—i.e., more financially vulnerable firms. $ ^{23} $ For example, Ashbaugh-Skaife, Collins, and LaFond (2006) find that corporate credit ratings (an inverse proxy for credit risk) increase with profitability, size, and institutional ownership. Whited and Wu (2006) find that financial constraints decrease in cash flow, size, and analyst coverage. Hadlock and Pierce (2010) draw similar conclusions about the effects of cash flow and size and also emphasize firm age—younger firms are more constrained. $ ^{24} $ For additional context, in column (2) we estimate the same determinants model with equity complexity as the dependent variable instead of debt complexity. The results show differences in the signs and significance of several variables, including size, book-to-market, and life cycle indicators. These findings further underscore the advantage of our methodology, as even within a relatively disaggregated construct such as financing complexity, the source of complexity can differ meaningfully (e.g., debt versus equity complexity).

Collectively, the results suggest that firms with greater financing constraints and credit risk have more complex debt, but greater complexity improves the predictability of subsequent financing costs. These findings are consistent with the interpretation that complex debt provisions, although costly in some ways, are valuable in addressing the information problems between borrowers and lenders that are especially acute when the firm is constrained. This interpretation contrasts with the possibility that firms are forced into taking on debt complexity, and it underscores why some firms might choose greater debt complexity in equilibrium.

## VI. CONCLUSION

we define business complexity as a decision-maker's difficulty in understanding the economic substance of the firm's transactions and financial position. We construct a fact-level measure of complexity using an LLM and embedded iXBRL tags. We validate the measure in several ways, including by documenting intuitive variation across industries and topic categories. Our results highlight that complexity imposes significant processing costs on capital market participants, evidenced by a negative relation between filing complexity and the speed of price responsiveness. We then

leverage the flexibility of our fact-level measure to address the important question of why firms engage in complex debt arrangements. We find evidence that debt complexity is taken on by financially constrained firms, and that complexity is associated with favorable outcomes such as lower financing risk.

Our discussion and analysis of business complexity highlights the benefit of a fact-level, algorithmic-driven approach. Although we note caveats about its limitations, our measure can be used by future researchers attempting to examine or control for various types of business complexity. Future research can also build on our approach. For example, one opportunity is to attempt to proxy for the processing of less sophisticated and more sophisticated market participants using different models—e.g., using less advanced models to capture less sophisticated participants. Regardless, the measure's level of disaggregation creates a richer and more flexible measure that can be applied in a variety of settings to answer fundamental economic questions.

#### APPENDIX B

## Model Fine-Tuning and Prediction Pipeline

#### Conceptual Introduction

We train a GPT model to add XBRL tags to quantitative footnote facts based on the surrounding narrative. The model generates predicted XBRL tags along with an underlying probability. We use the probability as a measure of model confidence, and inversely as a measure of complexity. Low model confidence when assigning an XBRL tag to a financial fact is indicative of high complexity and vice versa.

We use the following steps to construct our measure: design the prediction task, construct a training sample from existing iXBRL filings, fine-tune a GPT model on the prediction task, generate predictions for the entire sample of footnotes, construct fact-level complexity scores based on confidence scores. The sections below provide background and implementation detail for each of these steps. For a more conceptual and general introduction to generative LLMs, we recommend the overview paper by de Kok (2025).

### Step 1—The Prediction Task

We design the prediction task to use the following prompt and completion template:

##### Prompt:

&lt;|context|>During the second quarter of 2020, we acquired certain assets and liabilities of MARS Food Processing Solutions, LLC (MARS).&lt;|target|>This transaction was accounted for as a business combination with a purchase price of $5 million.

####

Completion:

This transaction was accounted for as a business combination with a purchase price of $\$<tag\ standard="business\ combination\ | consideration\ | transferred\ | 1" standard--businessacquisitionaxis="custom--marsfoodprocessing\ solutionsllcmember">5<ltag>million.

END

(continued on next page)

# APPENDIX B (continued)

This design embeds the following design considerations:

- Every footnote sentence with one or more financial facts receives an individual prompt. The  $ |\text{target}|> $ marker indicates where the focal sentence starts. (In the example above, the bolded sentence in the prompt reflects the focal sentence.)

- The sentence by itself does not always provide enough context to determine the most suitable XBRL tag. To avoid ambiguity, we also provide the model with the preceding sentences in the same paragraph as additional context in the prompt. These context sentences are provided after the <|context|> marker and before the <|target|> marker.

- The completion repeats the prompt focal sentence but also embeds the XBRL information into it. The XBRL information is indicated by the <tag></tag> markers, which are bolded in the completion example above. We train it to provide a simplified version of the more complex inline XBRL syntax to aid with our analysis (i.e., focusing just on element name and namespace and not units, periods, and other meta-data; using “|” to delineate words within a tag rather than capital letters, etc.). One sentence can contain multiple <tag> markers, one for each financial fact.

- The information contained in each <tag> marker always starts with the XBRL tag prediction consisting of two parts, the namespace (standard or custom) followed by the delimited XBRL tag. In the example, the tag is “business | combination | consideration | transferred | 1” which is a standard tag as indicated by the preceding “standard=” portion. The delineation between words using “|” is deliberate to ensure more meaningful tokenization. See our step 5 discussion.

- The <tag> marker can then include additional XBRL dimensions to report multidimensional facts, such as specifying that the revenue amount is for Product A in Geographic Segment X. Each dimension consists of a key and a value, which can be either standard or custom. In the example, the tag contains one dimension with the standard key “businessacquisitionaxis” and the custom value “marsfoodprocessingsolutionsllcmember” to denote that the business combination consideration transferred amount was for the Mars acquisition. Two things to note:

As the above example illustrates, the paragraph context can directly influence the XBRL tag and its dimensions, which is why we include the context in the prompt.

We do not use the dimensions in our complexity measure. However, dimensions are common in practice and the taxonomy is designed around them. Excluding the dimensions would result in less realistic and more verbose predicted XBRL tags as the model attempts to incorporate dimension-level descriptions in the main XBRL tags.

### Step 2—Construct the Training Sample Based on Existing iXBRL Filings

We start with the iXBRL filings from July 2016 to April 2024 and choose a training sample of 200,000 unique footnote sentences containing a financial fact. We draw three random sentences from every filing in this sample and randomly draw 200,000 examples from that stratified sample to create our final training sample for the following reasons:

- Training sample size is a trade-off between XBRL coverage and efficiency. Our model learns the XBRL taxonomy through the training sentences. So, we need to ensure the sample is large enough to comprehensively represent the standard taxonomy and common conventions for custom tags. However, training time and costs scale with the size of the training dataset, and an unnecessarily large dataset is infeasible. We settle on 200,000 examples because coverage simulations indicate that this probabilistically covers 100 percent of the standard tags and 99 percent of the custom tags while still being feasible to train with conventional hardware.

- We use stratified sampling by filing because the resulting model might be more confident for observations in the training data. Stratified sampling affects all filings equally and avoids structural biases in our measure without having to drop entire filings from our sample.

##### Step 3—Fine-Tune a GPT Model That Can Complete the Prediction Task

We conduct a full fine-tune of the open-source Llama 3 8b model for ten epochs to create our model. $ ^{27} $ This reflects the following empirical choices:

- We follow the decision diagram in Figure 1 by de Kok (2025) and fine-tune a local GPT model rather than using a third party API, such as OpenAI's fine-tuning API. Fine-tuning an open-source model yields two main benefits: (1) feeding a large volume of documents through the model on our own hardware is substantially less expensive and (2) the model weights will always be accessible in the future to expand and/or update our dataset. $ ^{28} $

- We choose the Llama 3 8b model as our base model because it represents the best model fully fine-tunable as of May 2024 using 8x H100 GPUs. We test several different alternatives, such as OpenAI GPT-3 and Microsoft Phi-2 and Phi-3, and although all models can successfully complete the task after fine-tuning, we find that Llama 3 8b performs the best for our task. It is possible to fine-tune larger models using parameter efficient fine-tuning techniques (PEFT) such as LoRA and QLoRA. However, our task requires the model to learn a large volume of complex information (i.e., the entire XBRL taxonomy, including custom tag conventions) and full-fine-tuning substantially outperforms PEFT for such tasks (see e.g., Biderman et al. 2024).

- We fine-tune our model for ten epochs, where the epochs represent the duration of training and the number of times the model has seen the entire set of training examples. Ten epochs reflect around eight hours of fine-tuning with 8x H100 GPUs. One can fine-tune a model for any number of epochs; too few epochs results in a model that underperforms and too many epochs can overfit the model to the training data. We use three primary metrics to evaluate the appropriate epoch: (1) the in-sample training loss, (2) the percentage of tags correctly predicted in a 25,000 hold-out sample, and (3) the average level of confidence in the 25,000 hold-out sample. We choose ten epochs for our model as the ideal balance because the incremental improvement in training loss and prediction accuracy flatten around that point. Later epochs do not yield materially better performance but the average model confidence does keep increasing, which we interpret as an indicator that the model is primarily overfitting beyond ten epochs. $ ^{29} $

### Step 4—Generate Predictions

- We construct a prompt using the template in step 1 for each financial fact sentence in our sample, creating a dataset of ~4.3 million sentences that need processing. We use eight single H100 instances from Runpod.io and the VLLM library to feed all observations through our fine-tuned model.

### Step 5—Construct Fact-Level Complexity Scores

- GPT models generate text token by token, where a token is generally a word, part of a word, or a special character. To generate a token, the model assigns each possible token in its vocabulary a probability and chooses a token using these probabilities. We set the “temperature” hyperparameter to 0 such that the model always chooses the token with the highest probability. We use these probabilities as our measure for model confidence, where a lower token probability indicates lower confidence.

- Our task predicts XBRL tags, and we use the prediction probabilities of the tokens that make up the XBRL tag to construct our confidence-based complexity score. Practically all XBRL tags consist of multiple underlying tokens. Different model families tokenize text differently, and we prefer the XBRL tag to be broken up into one token per word whenever possible as that yields more meaningful confidence scores for our task. To achieve this, we provide the Llama 3 tokenizer with “|” delineated lowercase words as we find that best reduces the number of unnecessary word splits. For example, the XBRL tag “partners | capital | account | units | converted” is tokenized into the tokens: “partners,” “|,” “capital,” “|,” “account,” “|,” “units,” “|,” “converted” with the Llama 3 tokenizer.

Note, some words are still broken up into multiple tokens, especially longer words. For example, Llama 3 tokenizes “restructuring” into “re” and “structuring.”

We retrieve the token probabilities, i.e., the token confidence scores, by exponentiating the log probabilities (commonly referred to as “logprobs”) of the tokens that make up the XBRL tag. These logprobs are available through the OpenAI API and the open-source VLLM package. $ ^{30} $

We calculate the fact-level confidence score as the minimum confidence score among its tokens to ensure the length of the XBRL tag does not influence the confidence score. For example, the predicted XBRL tag “business | acquisition | percentage | of | voting | interests | acquired” would receive a fact-level confidence score of 97.6 percent based on the following token-level confidence scores: “business” (100 percent), “| (100 percent), “acquisition” (97.6 percent), “|” (100 percent), “percentage” (100 percent), “|” (100 percent), “of” (100 percent), “|” (100 percent), “voting” (100 percent), “|” (100 percent), “interests” (100 percent), “|” (100 percent), “acquired” (100 percent).
