## Abstract

Research Summary: The emergence of large language models (LLMs) offers new opportunities for AI integration in research, particularly for data annotation and text classification. However, researchers lack guidance on implementation best practices, as the benefits and risks of these tools remain poorly understood. We develop a foundational framework for effective LLM implementation in management research, providing structured guidance on key decisions throughout the research process. We illustrate this framework through an empirical application: classifying sustainability claims in crowdfunding projects. While LLMs can match or exceed traditional methods' performance at lower cost, we find that variations in prompt design can significantly affect results and downstream analyses. We develop procedures for sensitivity analysis and provide detailed documentation to help researchers implement robustness while maintaining methodological integrity.

Managerial Summary: Large language models (LLMs) offer powerful new tools for business research, especially for analyzing and categorizing text data. However, managers and researchers lack clear guidance on how to use these tools effectively and reliably. This study creates a practical framework for implementing LLMs in

management research, covering key decisions from model selection to result validation. Using a real-world example of analyzing sustainability claims in crowdfunding campaigns, we demonstrate that LLMs can match traditional methods while being faster and cheaper. However, small changes in how you instruct the AI can significantly alter results and business conclusions. We provide systematic procedures for testing result reliability and offer practical tools to help managers implement AI-powered analysis while maintaining rigorous standards and avoiding misleading findings.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The advent of large language models (LLMs) presents numerous research applications, representing a paradigm shift across various fields (Eloundou et al., 2024). As articulated by Tu et al. (2023), LLMs “cultivate an extensive understanding of language, common sense, and reasoning, showcasing a semblance of intelligence.” As this technology advances, applied researchers are grappling with the profound implications for their research processes.

A particularly promising use case for LLMs in social science research is data annotation: the creation of structured variables from unstructured text for the purposes of use in downstream analysis. The prior methodological standard in this area has typically involved either human coding—often through a complex process of training research assistants—or automated methods ranging from dictionary- or keyword-based methods to custom-built supervised machine learning classifiers (see Choudhury et al., 2021; Miric et al., 2023). Early experiments have demonstrated LLMs' potential in labeling or annotating unstructured texts, consistently matching or exceeding the performance of both crowd workers and expert coders (Gilardi et al., 2023; Rathje et al., 2023; Tornberg, 2023), and doing so with significant cost and time savings. However, while the technical capabilities of these models are increasingly well documented, the implications for research validity and reproducibility remain unclear.

This gap is particularly urgent in the social sciences because social science applications of LLMs differ fundamentally from typical computer science or commercial use cases. Rather than optimizing for accuracy alone, researchers often use LLM-generated labels as an intermediate step to study downstream relationships, frequently while explicitly testing hypotheses. This introduces unique methodological challenges.

Emerging evidence suggests that subtle implementation choices can significantly affect LLM performance (Battle & Gollapudi, 2024; Meincke et al., 2025a; Sclar et al., 2023). More concerning for social scientists, small variations in these choices can alter the interpretation of downstream findings. However, what initially appears as a methodological weakness potentially offers a novel opportunity. While traditional approaches to data annotation, like human

coding, were constrained by cost and feasibility, LLMs provide an affordable means to systematically explore how research design choices affect downstream conclusions.

In this paper, we draw from both computer science and social science—as well as insights from our own applied empirical tests—to develop a foundational framework for using LLMs in management research, focusing particularly on data annotation tasks. Our framework breaks down the implementation process into five key stages: method selection, model configuration, prompt engineering, cost and scale considerations, and validation and robustness. For each stage, we identify core decisions researchers must make, analyze their tradeoffs, and provide evidence-based best practices.

We demonstrate the utility of this framework through an empirical application: using LLMs to classify Kickstarter campaigns along the dimension of product sustainability, a task for which we had previously developed a supervised machine learning model. Through systematic testing of different prompting strategies across multiple models, we show that LLMs can match traditional methods at significantly reduced cost and time.

However, our analysis also underscores the concern that variations in model choice and prompt design can lead to markedly different labels, which in turn affect downstream analyses and conclusions. Moreover, we demonstrate that more accurate LLM prediction in the first stage does not automatically translate to better inference in the second stage. A key theoretical insight from our analysis is that higher first-stage classification accuracy does not guarantee unbiased downstream inference when classification errors are systematically correlated with outcome variables. This finding has important implications for any research design using intermediate classification steps, whether employing LLMs or other automated annotation methods.

Based on these findings, we propose a systematic approach to sensitivity analysis, aiming to provide researchers with tools to evaluate how variations in prompting and other methodological choices affect their results. In this approach, we leverage variation to help bound the range of possible research conclusions. We accompany this with detailed documentation and reusable code to help researchers implement these sensitivity checks in their own work. $ ^{1} $ We argue that these types of robustness checks will remain important even as models continue to advance—as with any empirical method in which the researcher has significant degrees of freedom.

Our paper contributes to the growing body of knowledge on the integration of AI and machine learning in management (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 2023; Dell'Acqua et al., 2023; Doshi et al., 2024). While a rich emergent literature focuses on the integration of such technologies within organizations (e.g., Boussioux et al., 2024; Csaszar et al., 2024; Jia, Luo, et al., 2025; Jia, Roh, et al., 2025; Otis et al., 2024), researchers increasingly recognize the methodological potential of LLMs in social science research (Chopra & Haaland, 2023; Weber & Reichardt, 2023). We build on this work by providing a foundational methodological framework that introduces core principles for LLM implementation, emphasizing sensitivity analysis and the propagation of methodological choices through the research pipeline. Rather than offering definitive guidance, we establish baseline methodology and structural principles that future researchers can build upon and adapt to their specific contexts and evolving technological capabilities. We hope that this work provides a structured foundation for the responsible and rigorous use of these technologies in research methodology, facilitating continued methodological innovation as the field develops.

## 2 | A BASELINE FRAMEWORK FOR IMPLEMENTING LLMs IN MANAGEMENT RESEARCH

Large language models (LLMs) employ transformer architectures that can process and generate human-like text across a wide range of applications (Bommasani et al., 2021; Brown et al., 2020; Chowdhery et al., 2023; Vaswani et al., 2017). As these models become increasingly integrated into the research process (Chattopadhyay et al., 2024; Tranchero et al., 2024), researchers across disciplines have begun developing guidelines for their use. Drawing from emerging work in computer science, the social sciences, and management—as well as our own empirical experimentation—we synthesize and expand upon these insights to establish foundational principles that future researchers can build upon to ensure both rigor and reproducibility. These principles translate the broader goals of research transparency and reproducibility into specific protocols for LLM implementation (Bender et al., 2021; Weidinger et al., 2021).

Our framework breaks down the LLM implementation process into five key stages: (1) method selection and integration, (2) model selection and stability, (3) prompt engineering, (4) cost and scale considerations, and (5) validation and robustness assessment. Below, we discuss each stage, identifying trade-offs and developing specific recommendations for management scholars (Table 1).

(1) Method selection. The first fundamental consideration concerns whether LLMs are necessary at all for a particular research application. We argue that it is useful to position LLMs within the researcher's methodological toolkit. While LLMs can process unstructured text without task-specific training data, they should be evaluated against established alternatives, each with documented strengths and limitations. Human coding allows for nuanced interpretation of context and ambiguity but faces constraints of scale and cost (Grimmer & Stewart, 2013). Dictionary or keyword methods offer computational efficiency and perfect reproducibility but struggle with semantic complexity and context dependence, often performing best for narrow technical applications (Miric et al., 2023; Quinn et al., 2010). Supervised machine learning approaches enable scalable, consistent classification but require substantial labeled training data for good performance (Choudhury et al., 2021; Miric et al., 2023). LLMs occupy a distinctive position in this spectrum—they can handle complex semantic tasks without custom training data but introduce new challenges in reproducibility and output stability. For instance, specialized dictionary methods may outperform LLMs in tasks requiring precise technical or field-specific terminology. This underscores a general point: newer methods are not always better; legacy methods may work just as well or better for certain applications.

As we will demonstrate in our empirical application, one key advantage of LLMs, vis-à-vis prior methods, is the low cost and ease of sensitivity testing. This advantage signals that LLMs may provide utility—in tandem with other methods like human coding, which might be used for a primary analysis—in showing robustness to a variety of methodological choices. We emphasize a first principle for this decision point: to consider LLMs as part of a broader methodological toolkit, carefully matching the use of the tool to the research setting.

(2) Model selection and stability. Once researchers have decided to integrate LLMs into their research pipeline, the next decision point involves which models to use. The rapid evolution of LLMs presents researchers with a constant tradeoff between accessing the latest capabilities and ensuring long-term reproducibility of their findings (Raff, 2019). This requires careful consideration of model selection, version control, and access method (Mitchell et al., 2019). Researchers must document not only which model they use but also how they access it and what steps they take to ensure their analysis can be replicated in the future. While weights can

(3) Prompt engineering. The next stage of LLM implementation concerns prompt engineering. Unlike traditional computational tools where inputs map precisely to outputs in a deterministic manner, LLMs require carefully specified natural language instructions (“prompts”) that fundamentally shape their behavior. This introduces prompt engineering as a critical methodological component (Wei et al., 2022) that incurs unique challenges because, unlike traditional method parameters, prompts are more difficult to systematically optimize.

While this framework focuses primarily on prompt-based approaches—which represent the most accessible starting point for basic annotation tasks—recent methodological work demonstrates impressive performance through specialized training on domain-specific data, particularly for nuanced classification tasks in strategy research (Chattopadhyay et al., 2024; Jia, Luo, et al., 2025; Jia, Roh, et al., 2025). We focus on prompt-based methods as they provide an immediately accessible baseline approach, while noting that fine-tuning represents a valuable extension for researchers with appropriate resources and complex annotation needs (see Appendix C for additional technical implementation details, including fine-tuning considerations).

The relationship between prompt construction and model outputs is complex and often unpredictable—subtle variations in phrasing, example selection, or formatting can significantly affect results (Battle & Gollapudi, 2024; Meincke et al., 2024, 2025a; Sclar et al., 2023). However, rather than viewing this sensitivity as purely problematic, we argue it presents an opportunity for methodological robustness when properly managed. Each prompting approach presents fundamental trade-offs between simplicity, reliability, and depth of analysis—from basic classification instructions to complex reasoning frameworks—which we explore in detail in the following section.

Rather than seeking a single “optimal” prompt, researchers may benefit from developing families of related prompts based on established prompting strategies (e.g., Meincke et al., 2025a). A focus on systematic variation serves two purposes: first, it helps identify which aspects of prompt construction are most consequential for the specific research application; second, it provides bounds on how methodological choices affect research conclusions. This leads us to propose a core principle for prompt engineering: structure prompts systematically and transparently, with thorough documentation of development choices.

(4) Cost and scale considerations. As researchers then look to scale their approach across their research application, they must explicitly plan for computational resource allocation and statistical power. The importance of this stage will vary with the size of the data annotation task and the speed with which costs fall with advancing technology. While LLMs can process individual texts quickly, costs scale with both sample size and prompt complexity. Each API call incurs charges based on input and output tokens, $ ^{4} $ making experimental design choices like prompt length, number of examples, and iteration count directly tied to research budgets. For instance, chain-of-thought prompting can improve accuracy but multiplies token usage, while running multiple prompt variations for robustness checks multiplies total API calls (Kaplan et al., 2020).

In our subsequent empirical example, we provide cost calculations under different configurations, providing some benchmarks for how researchers might budget for both initial analysis and robustness checks. We also demonstrate sensitivity analysis techniques using shortened texts (for lower token costs) and bootstrapping (for large datasets), which can substantially

lower the costs of robustness testing. We believe API costs will continue to drop over the coming years, making these sensitivity techniques feasible for researchers even with very limited budgets. For now, we propose the following principle: researchers should balance resource usage with the demands of statistical power and robustness needs.

(5) Validation and robustness. The final stage of LLM integration concerns validation and robustness testing. As discussed above, variations in prompt construction can materially affect research conclusions, necessitating systematic validation protocols (Dror et al., 2018; Ribeiro et al., 2020). For example, prompts that produce nearly identical accuracy metrics on validation data may still produce different downstream regression estimates. Researchers should maintain gold-standard validation sets, systematically test prompt variations, and examine how these methodological choices feed through their analysis pipeline (Egami et al., 2024). Beyond reporting point estimates, we suggest that researchers should document the sensitivity of their findings to reasonable variations in LLM implementation.

This stage underscores our final principle: researchers should conduct systematic evaluations of result validity and stability. This article suggests a procedure for this sensitivity analysis—generating families of related prompts, testing their impact on downstream analyses, and producing bounded estimates that capture underlying methodological uncertainty. We provide code to facilitate the implementation of these procedures, documented further in Appendix A.

### 2.1 | Prompt engineering strategies

The core principles outlined in the previous section each require careful implementation decisions. Among these, prompt engineering—the systematic design of instructions given to language models—stands out as particularly critical for three reasons. First, unlike other implementation choices that can often be standardized across projects (such as model selection or validation protocols), prompts must be carefully crafted for each specific research application. Second, small variations in prompt design can significantly affect model outputs (Battle & Gollapudi, 2024), making this perhaps the most consequential implementation decision faced by researchers. Third, while other aspects of LLM implementation often parallel established practices in machine learning, prompt engineering represents a fundamentally new methodological challenge, thus requiring distinct guidance.

Importantly, different models respond differently to the same prompting strategies, and effectiveness can vary significantly based on the specific context and task. For instance, while Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting has proven valuable across many applications, recent research suggests that its benefits may be diminishing with more advanced models that have internalized reasoning patterns (Meincke et al., 2025b). This underscores the importance of systematic testing across model-prompt combinations rather than assuming the universal effectiveness of any single approach.

## 3 | EMPIRICAL APPLICATION: IDENTIFYING SUSTAINABILITY CLAIMS IN KICKSTARTER PROJECTS

In this application, we apply the five-stage framework to a real research application. We first explain our method selection process (Stage 1) by comparing LLMs to our prior supervised machine learning approach. We then detail our model selection decisions (Stage 2) and prompt engineering process (Stage 3), before analyzing cost and scale considerations (Stage 4) across different implementation strategies. We report the results of testing different configurations across Stages 2–4 on a gold-standard test set before identifying an implementation decision for the full dataset. Finally, we demonstrate validation and robustness procedures (Stage 5) through systematic sensitivity analysis.

### 3.1 | Stage 1: Method selection

Sample and objective. We use as our setting the rewards-based crowdfunding platform Kickstarter. Crowdfunding has emerged as an increasingly prevalent method for raising capital for entrepreneurial organizations (Agrawal et al., 2014; Mollick, 2014; Sorenson et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2017), and rewards-based crowdfunding is an important type of crowdfunding platform. It includes such platforms as Kickstarter, Indiegogo, Rockethub, and GoFundMe. On rewards-based crowdfunding platforms, entrepreneurial organizations solicit funding in exchange for “rewards” (the reward is what, if anything, the funder receives in the exchange).

We use a database of all Kickstarter campaigns between the years 2009 and 2016, obtained from the CrowdBerkeley Database (managed by the Fung Institute at UC Berkeley). This database includes information about the campaign (e.g., project category and whether it has a video) and its current status (success, number of backers, amount pledged), but does not contain the full description of the project from its campaign page. To augment this, we scraped the full campaign text from each project page URL and matched this text to the project metadata from the database. The full sample comprises 295,985 projects.

The objective of the methods described below is to classify each Kickstarter campaign text along the dimension of perceived social benefit. Examples of campaigns classified as offering socially beneficial products or services in the sample include a food supply company with a mission of serving food deserts, a retail store that prioritizes hiring homeless youth, or a drone imaging company used in search and rescue missions.

Given the size of the complete dataset, hand-labeling the projects was not computationally feasible. In the next two sections, we describe first the approach we took in 2017—a custom-built supervised machine learning model—and then describe the approach to using LLMs to accomplish the same end.

Prior method: Supervised GBM model. Our prior approach followed a traditional supervised machine learning pipeline. First, we created a training dataset of 2068 projects labeled by a combination of Amazon Mechanical Turk workers and university research assistants. Each project was evaluated by multiple raters who determined whether the main product or service benefited the broader society (coded as 1) or provided primarily consumer-only benefits (coded as 0), with substantial inter-rater agreement (Fleiss's kappa between 0.6 and 0.7). $ ^{5} $

Using this labeled dataset, we trained a Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model (Friedman, 2001) to classify the remaining projects. $ ^{6} $ The model processed project descriptions through standard text preprocessing techniques and cross-validation procedures $ ^{7} $ to ensure reliability. When tested against a hand-labeled validation set of 250 examples, the model reached an accuracy of 92%, with balanced false positives and false negatives. While achieving satisfactory performance, this approach required significant upfront investment in training data creation and model development.

Proposed decision. LLMs appear to be a good fit for this research application for several reasons. First, while the GBM model achieved high accuracy, it required substantial upfront investment in creating a labeled training dataset and fine-tuning the model—resources that might not be available to all researchers, and regardless incur significant cost and time to execute. LLMs offer the potential to match this performance without the need for extensive training data. Second, the text classification task at hand—evaluating societal benefit—requires a nuanced understanding of context and implicit meanings that keyword-based or simpler automated methods might miss. LLMs' demonstrated capability in processing semantic complexity makes them well-suited for this type of evaluation. Third, while human coding would offer a similar nuanced understanding, the scale of our dataset (295,985 projects) makes manual coding prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. Given these tradeoffs, we proceed with LLMs as our primary method while maintaining our human-labeled validation set as a gold standard for evaluating performance.

### 3.2 | Stage 2: Model selection

Drawing on our framework's principles for model selection, we identified five LLMs for systematic evaluation, chosen to represent different points in the tradeoff space between performance, cost, and accessibility. GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus represent more mature models optimized for complex reasoning tasks, offering potentially higher accuracy but at greater computational cost. GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Haiku represent newer architectures optimized for efficiency,

potentially offering a better cost-performance balance. We included GPT-4o-mini as an extremely lightweight option to test the viability of low-cost implementations. Proposed decision. Based on these considerations, we proceed with testing five models that span the key tradeoffs in our framework: two models optimized for complex reasoning (GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus), two models optimized for efficiency (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Haiku), and one lightweight model (GPT-4o-mini). $ ^{8} $ We systematically evaluate these models across all prompting strategies, maintaining consistent parameters (temperature = 0) to maximize reliability in classification decisions (Törnberg, 2024).

### 3.3 | Stage 3: Prompt engineering

Table 3 presents a systematic application of different prompting strategies to our sustainability classification task. For each strategy discussed in Section 2.1, we developed a canonical example prompt specifically designed for identifying societal benefits in Kickstarter projects.

To test each prompting strategy, we developed an automated pipeline $ ^{9} $ that:

1. Loads the project description.

2. Constructs the appropriate prompt by combining the preamble prompt text with the Kickstarter project description.

3. Makes an API call to retrieve the classification.

4. Appends the binary  $ (0/1) $ label to our dataset.

We maintained consistent output formatting across all strategies by explicitly requesting binary responses, allowing direct comparison of classification performance. While more complex prompting strategies (like Chain of Thought or Tree of Thoughts) often generate additional explanatory text, we constructed the prompts to extract only a single final binary classification. $ ^{10} $

Proposed decision. We proceed with testing all seven prompting strategies outlined in Table 3, from basic zero-shot instructions to complex reasoning frameworks like ToT. This comprehensive approach serves two purposes. First, it allows us to evaluate whether more sophisticated prompting techniques justify their increased token usage for our binary classification task. Second, and more importantly, testing multiple valid prompting approaches enables us to assess the sensitivity of our research conclusions to reasonable variations in prompt design. This aligns with our framework's emphasis on systematic prompt evaluation, rather than seeking a single "optimal" prompt.

### 3.4 | Stage 4: Cost and scale considerations

Because our full dataset comprises 295,985 Kickstarter projects, several cost and scale considerations inform our testing strategy. First, API costs scale with both input length (Kickstarter project descriptions) and prompt complexity. Second, testing all model-prompt combinations (five models multiplied by seven prompting strategies) across the full dataset would be prohibitively expensive—even using our most cost-efficient model (GPT-4o-mini), processing the full dataset would cost several thousand dollars. Third, and most importantly, testing on the complete dataset would not provide additional insights, because the full dataset does not have any ground-truth labels. Our labeled test set of 250 projects, which was previously used to validate

the GBM and other supervised ML models, provides a consistent benchmark for comparing performance across methods.

Proposed decision. Given these considerations, we proceed with systematic testing of all model-prompt combinations on our test set of 250 projects. This focused approach enables thorough comparison while maintaining reasonable computational costs (ranging from 0.03 to 5.71 USD per test run across different model-prompt combinations, as detailed in Table 4). Following the principle of balancing resource usage with robustness needs, this strategy allows us to evaluate the results of all methodological choices before potential deployment at scale.

Second-stage results comparison. Why is this the case? If the direction of the error in the first stage is systematically correlated with the outcome in the second stage, second-stage estimates may be biased. Consider a model that, for whatever reason, tends to misclassify a certain type of project as a false negative. $ ^{12} $ If these types of false negatives are also disproportionately projects that tend to meet their funding goal, the particular direction of the first-stage error will bias the second-stage coefficients downwards. This can be meaningful in the case of imbalanced classes and, if a systematic tendency, not necessarily a problem that will be overcome by larger samples.

Moreover, the variability in second-stage results also raises concerns about the potential for manipulation in the pursuit of desired outcomes. Each of the prompts we tested is, arguably, reasonable and justifiable on its own. Scanning the variability in coefficients in Figure 1, it is easy to imagine that—even if the “true” relationship were indistinguishable from zero—researchers might be tempted to choose the model-prompt combination that produces a significantly positive relationship. In theory, one could potentially iterate on plausible-sounding prompts until one is found that generates the preferred downstream results, given the increasingly low cost of iteration. Put another way, the use of LLMs for this type of application lowers the cost of p-hacking, making comprehensive sensitivity analysis more urgent. This connects to broader concerns about researcher degrees of freedom in empirical research, where the ease of testing multiple specifications, control variables, or model variations can lead researchers to focus on achieving statistical significance rather than understanding the magnitude and robustness of their findings (Shaver, 2007). The low cost and speed of LLM iteration create analogous opportunities for such methodological cherry-picking.

Proposed decision. Given both the first-stage accuracy results and downstream analysis implications, we would propose implementing GPT-4o with Tree of Thoughts prompting as our baseline classification method for the full dataset. This configuration achieved the highest accuracy, with good balance between false positives and negatives, at a reasonable cost, and successfully recovered the expected relationship between sustainability claims and funding success. Note that we do not expect this model-prompt combination to be optimal for all annotation tasks or to remain the best approach as models and prompting strategies continue to evolve; researchers should ideally conduct their own systematic testing for their specific tasks and contexts. Moreover, our analysis reveals that strong first-stage performance does not guarantee reliable downstream results, and even reasonable methodological choices can produce different research conclusions. Therefore, we would propose a multi-pronged validation strategy: (1) use our best-performing configuration for primary analysis, (2) implement systematic sensitivity testing for all key findings using multiple model-prompt combinations, and (3) report bounded estimates that reflect underlying methodological uncertainty. This approach aligns with our framework's emphasis on rigorous validation while acknowledging the unique challenges that LLMs present for research reproducibility.

Generating prompt variations. To extend our sensitivity analysis, we created a set of 100 unique prompts, similar to those shown in Table 3 and exhibiting a range of prompting strategies. We used the Claude-3.5-Sonnet chat interface for this task, providing it with the prompt

Baseline sensitivity. As demonstrated by the earlier tests, there is considerable variation even within the model across prompt specifications. The overall variance shrinks slightly with quality control restrictions, such as limited to advanced prompting strategies only (Panel c) or dropping prompts below a threshold for first-stage accuracy (Panel e). In all cases, however, the “true” positive relationship between sustainability claims and funding success can be recovered in a confidence interval constructed within one standard deviation of the mean coefficient value across estimates.

We test several cost-reduction strategies to check the limitations of such sensitivity analysis to increasingly lightweight solutions. First, we examine a low-cost mini model, GPT-4o-mini (Panel b), finding that it produces a similar distribution of coefficients to its more expensive counterpart, albeit with slightly higher dispersion. Second, we test replacing the full Kickstarter description with only the short project taglines (typically one to three sentences). This latter intervention reduces the estimated mean coefficient by nearly 30%, but still preserves the overall positive relationship, suggesting that even with massively reduced information, the fundamental pattern remains detectable.

Proposed decision. As discussed above, we proposed to use GPT-4o with Tree of Thought (ToT) prompting as the baseline model-prompt combination for the main analysis, based on its strong performance in our tests and favorable cost profile. We would additionally propose reporting, at a minimum, a sensitivity analysis similar to Figure 3, Panels (a) and (e) as standard robustness checks. This approach demonstrates that the findings are not merely artifacts of the specific prompt selected, but represent a robust relationship that persists across methodological variations.

The specific sensitivity analyses reported may vary according to the needs of a given project and the particular empirical concerns therein. We discuss several of these alternatives below.

## 4 | DISCUSSION

In this article, we provide a foundational framework for implementing LLMs in management research, with a particular focus on data annotation tasks. Our empirical application demonstrates both the potential and pitfalls of using these technologies for classification tasks commonly encountered in social science applications. Several key insights emerge from our findings that have important implications for researchers.

A central finding from our empirical application is that variations in prompt design and model selection can lead to substantially different downstream research conclusions, even when first-stage accuracy metrics appear similar. While this sensitivity might initially seem problematic, we argue that it actually represents a methodological opportunity when properly managed. Traditional approaches to data annotation—whether human coding or conventional machine learning—have always contained subjective elements and researcher degrees of freedom, but the associated costs often prevented comprehensive sensitivity testing. The relative affordability and speed of LLM implementation enable systematic exploration of how key methodological choices affect research conclusions.

This emphasis on sensitivity testing aligns with longstanding principles in social science methodology. Just as researchers routinely employ robustness checks for regression specifications, variable construction, or sample definitions, we propose that LLM implementation should similarly incorporate systematic sensitivity analysis. However, the democratization of annotation through LLMs also creates new challenges for research integrity analogous to traditional concerns about researcher degrees of freedom (Shaver, 2007). The low cost and speed of LLM iteration mean researchers can easily test numerous model-prompt combinations, creating opportunities for specification searching that must be guarded against through transparent reporting of all tested configurations. We emphasize the importance of bounding estimates rather than seeking a single “optimal” implementation.

Our finding that higher first-stage accuracy doesn't necessarily translate to better downstream results deserves particular attention. When a model misclassifies data in ways that correlate with outcome variables, second-stage estimates may be systematically biased. This challenge is not unique to LLMs, but the potential for large-scale implementation makes it particularly consequential. This finding underscores the importance of validating LLM implementations at multiple stages of the research pipeline, not just at the initial classification stage. This principle of multi-stage validation is a fundamental methodological consideration that should remain relevant, regardless of how LLM technology evolves.

The rapid evolution of LLMs necessitates new standards for research transparency and reproducibility. Our framework emphasizes thorough documentation of model selection, prompt design, and any other decision parameters. This documentation serves two purposes: enabling direct replication of findings and allowing for systematic exploration of how methodological choices affect results. The dramatic cost difference between human coding and LLM implementation suggests significant potential for scaling research designs. However, this scale advantage will ideally be balanced against the need for thorough validation and sensitivity testing—that is, the efficiency gains from LLMs can be partially reinvested in more comprehensive methodological validation.

Our approach also underscores the continued centrality of human expertise in the LLM era. While these technologies can dramatically reduce the cost and time required for large-scale annotation, they do not eliminate the need for careful human judgment in establishing ground truth, designing validation procedures, and interpreting results.

Several practical implications emerge from our framework and empirical application. First, researchers should explicitly plan for sensitivity analysis from the outset, rather than treating it as an afterthought. Systematic variation in prompt design, model selection, and parameter settings should be incorporated into research design and budgeting. Second, researchers should consider hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of different methodologies. For example, human coding might be used for creating high-quality validation sets, while LLMs aid in facilitating large-scale implementation. Similarly, multiple LLM implementations with varying approaches might be used to bound estimates and establish robustness. Third, researchers should prioritize reproducibility over cutting-edge performance. Documenting exact model versions, prompts, and parameters remain essential for building on scientific progress.

Our study has several limitations that suggest opportunities for future research. First, while our empirical application focuses on binary classification in a specific context, future work should extend these approaches to more complex annotation tasks. Second, as LLM technology continues to evolve, future research should systematically compare how newer architectures affect sensitivity to methodological choices. More fundamentally, as researchers increasingly adopt LLMs in their methodological toolkit, the field needs continued development of best practices and standards. Just as statistical methods have evolved, established norms for appropriate implementation and reporting, LLM methods will require similar disciplinary consensus. Ongoing refinement through methodological dialogue will be essential.

## 5 | CONCLUSION

LLMs have become a powerful addition to the management researcher's methodological toolkit, reducing costs while maintaining or improving accuracy compared to traditional approaches. However, their implementation requires careful attention to methodological choices and

systematic validation procedures. The fundamental principles of research design—transparency, validity, reliability, and robustness—remain as relevant for LLM implementation as they are for established methods.

As with any methodological innovation, the scientific value of LLMs ultimately depends less on their technological sophistication than on their thoughtful implementation within rigorous research designs. By providing a foundational framework for implementation decisions and validation procedures, we hope to establish baseline methodology that facilitates responsible integration of these tools into management research. As LLM technology continues to evolve rapidly, this framework should be viewed as a starting point for methodological development rather than a definitive guide. We encourage future researchers to build upon these foundations, testing our principles across different domains, tasks, and technological advances, and extending our approach as new models and capabilities emerge. The rapid pace of development in this field necessitates ongoing methodological innovation, and we hope this work provides a structured foundation for such future contributions to advance the responsible use of AI in management research.
