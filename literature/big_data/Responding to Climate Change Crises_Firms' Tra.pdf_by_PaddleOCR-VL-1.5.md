# Responding to Climate Change Crises: Firms' Trade-Offs

Felix Fritsch* Qi Zhang† Xiang Zheng‡

Journal of Accounting Research, forthcoming§

## Abstract

We examine firms' trade-offs in their voluntary disclosure decisions following negative media coverage of climate change incidents. By combining a keyword discovery algorithm and a fine-tuned BERT model, we identify "hard" and "soft" climate disclosures on Twitter. Our findings indicate that firms tend to issue climate tweets as a rapid response to negative climate incidents. Additionally, firms with a history of hard climate change disclosure, as measured by ESG reports, are more likely to issue climate-related responses than firms without such a history. Furthermore, we show that prior hard disclosure is associated with hard responses when the incident receives moderate media attention, but with soft responses when the incident receives low media attention. Our findings provide empirical insights for dynamic disclosure theory by illustrating how prior disclosure shapes firms' response strategies to negative media coverage.

Keywords: Climate change; cost-benefit tradeoffs; soft information; social media; voluntary disclosure

JEL classification codes: D61; M14; M40; Q54

## 1 Introduction

Over the past decade, the growing awareness of climate risks has fueled unprecedented investor demand for climate-related information (Krueger, Sautner, and Starks, 2020; Azar et al., 2021; Lee, 2021). Despite this demand, significant gaps in climate-related disclosures persist, making voluntary disclosures a crucial way for firms to communicate environmental information and shape stakeholder perceptions. In this study, we examine how firms use voluntary disclosures on Twitter (rebranded as X in 2023, though referred to as Twitter here) to respond to negative media coverage of climate change incidents.

We focus on this setting because external coverage of negative climate change incidents—whether from traditional media, social media, or other stakeholders—amplifies stakeholder awareness of firm misconduct (Gantchev, Giannetti, and Li, 2022), intensifying the pressure on firms to repair their reputations. Disclosure serves as a critical mechanism for managing reputational damage (Lee, Hutton, and Shu, 2015; Baloria and Heese, 2018), yet firms exhibit significant variation in their responses. While some disclose extensively, others remain silent or rely on symbolic gestures (e.g., Brendel and Ryans, 2021). This raises two questions: Under what circumstances do firms respond to external coverage of negative climate-related incidents, and what do they choose to communicate?

To address these questions, we draw on dynamic disclosure theory, which views a firm's disclosure decisions as part of an ongoing, multi-period flow of information (e.g., Guttman, Kremer, and Skrzypacz, 2014; Aghamolla and An, 2021; Bagnoli and Watts, 2021). At the core of this theory is the idea that stakeholders interpret a firm's disclosures in the context of its past communications, meaning that historical disclosures shape market expectations and influence future disclosure decisions.

Two key perspectives explain how past disclosures affect a firm's subsequent disclosure strategy. The first, represented by Aghamolla and An (2021), suggests that early disclosures generate a real option: by disclosing today, a manager increases the option value of withholding information in the future. This occurs because early disclosure raises the threshold for future disclosures, allowing the manager to withhold information selectively if negative news arises. In this way, early disclosure provides flexibility in managing future market expectations.

The second perspective highlights the role of verifiable, hard information in reinforcing credibility. As Gigler and Hemmer (1998), Stocken (2000), and Lundholm (2003) argue, disclosures such as audited reports establish benchmarks that build trust and enhance the credibility of prior voluntary disclosures. By creating a more reliable information environment, these hard disclosures lower the cost of future communication while also increasing its effectiveness. At the same time, trust built through verifiable disclosures raises stakeholder expectations for consistency, pressuring firms to maintain transparency. Empirical evidence from Ball, Jayaraman, and Shivakumar (2012) supports this view, showing that verifiable disclosures complement voluntary ones by strengthening their credibility.

In summary, a firm's disclosure history influences its future communication in two ways: it can serve as a buffer, allowing firms to rely on soft disclosures based on past credibility, or as a facilitator, lowering the cost and enhancing the credibility of future hard disclosures by building trust and establishing benchmarks. We hypothesize that whether prior disclosure functions as a buffer or a facilitator depends on the trade-off between the penalty for exercising the costless soft-disclosure option created by prior hard disclosure and the cost of current hard disclosure. When the penalty for using the soft-disclosure option is low, firms are more likely to rely on symbolic disclosures, using their past credibility as a shield.

Building on this theoretical foundation, we develop a classification framework that distinguishes among three types of climate-related disclosures: hard information, soft information, and non-disclosure. Hard information refers to objectively verifiable data, such as emission reports or third-party assurance, which carry higher verification costs but significantly enhance credibility and stakeholder trust (e.g., Ijiri, 1975; Bertomeu and Marinovic, 2016). Soft information, by contrast, lacks verifiability and often serves as a symbolic gesture to signal concern for climate change (Lyon and Maxwell, 2011). Firms face a cost-benefit trade-off when deciding how to disclose, weighing the reputational and financial costs of issuing credible, verifiable hard information against the risks of withholding information or issuing empty responses. By framing disclosure decisions within this strategic context, we highlight how firms navigate the interplay between their historical reputation, stakeholder expectations, and the costs of disclosure.

Twitter provides an ideal setting for studying corporate climate-related disclosures, due to its speed, reach, and flexibility. Unlike traditional disclosure channels, Twitter enables firms to

respond to reputation-damaging events in real time and connect with a broad audience of stakeholders quickly (Blankespoor, Miller, and White, 2014; Jung et al., 2018; Crowley, Huang, and Lu, 2024a). The scale of climate-related communication on Twitter highlights its growing role: between June 2018 and December 2019, Moss, Naughton, and Wang (2024) report that 86 firms issued ESG press releases on 798 firm-days, whereas 782 firms tweeted about climate change on 12,427 firm-days.

Additionally, Twitter's lower materiality threshold allows firms to share less formal, nonmaterial information that might not appear in traditional reports (Crowley et al., 2019). For example, firms can use Twitter for disclosing negative information about their peers (Cao, Fang, and Lei, 2021) or for engaging in emotional branding campaigns (Wu and Li, 2018). This flexibility makes Twitter particularly useful for identifying and analyzing soft disclosures, which often lack the verifiability of formal reporting yet play a significant role in corporate communications.

Despite its importance, the academic study of climate-related disclosures on Twitter remains limited, partly due to the empirical challenges, including the relatively low frequency of relevant tweets, the vague and evolving definition of climate change, and Twitter's unique linguistic features, such as hashtags, brevity, and informality. These challenges complicate the creation of training data for machine-learning models, limiting the ability to systematically analyze climate-related content.

To overcome these challenges, we develop Climate-TwitterBERT, a deep learning model that combines a machine-learning keyword discovery algorithm with BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers). This two-step method allows us to accurately identify climate-related tweets at scale. First, we manually label 10,000 corporate tweets as climate-related or not. During this process, we observe that only 182 tweets directly address climate issues, highlighting the challenge of identifying relevant content in a vast dataset. To enhance the dataset, we apply an adaptive keyword algorithm inspired by King, Lam, and Roberts (2017) and finance research (Sautner et al., 2023a), which identifies bigrams and hashtags frequently associated with climate change. From this expanded set, we manually review 2,000 additional tweets to ensure their relevance. This process yields a robust dataset of diverse and representative climate-related tweets.

In the second step, we use this dataset to fine-tune a BERT model, leveraging its ability to generate context-aware embeddings. Unlike traditional keyword-based approaches, which often produce false positives, BERT captures the contextual relationships between words, improving the accuracy of tweet classification. Through this method, we identify 136,899 unique climate-related tweets, offering unprecedented insight into firms' use of Twitter for climate disclosures.

We further extend Climate-TwitterBERT to classify tweets into Hard, Soft, and Promotional categories, consistent with our earlier theoretical framework. This classification operationalizes the hard versus soft distinction by focusing on verifiability: Hard tweets provide verifiable, quantifiable information, such as emissions data or awards, while Soft tweets reflect unverifiable statements, such as general aspirations or industry trends. Promotional tweets focus on marketing green products and are excluded from later analyses.

To validate our classification, we conduct a series of tests. First, we show that Hard tweets are associated with higher specificity, numerical intensity, and objectivity than are Soft tweets, confirming that the categories capture meaningful distinctions. Furthermore, we find that Hard tweets generate greater stakeholder engagement, as measured by likes, retweets, and replies, suggesting that stakeholders value verifiable disclosures more highly than aspirational or promotional content.

Using the output from our Climate-TwitterBERT model, we find that firms are 37% more likely to disclose climate change information within three days of experiencing negative climate-related incidents. In contrast, there is little evidence that firms respond to nonclimate-related negative incidents with climate-related information. These findings suggest that, absent disclosure requirements, firms are driven to communicate climate-related information primarily when their actions are publicly scrutinized through external coverage.

We examine whether firms' prior ESG disclosures influence their responses to negative climate incidents. Using an indicator for whether the firm issued an ESG report in the previous year and a composite score reflecting disclosure quality, we find that firms with prior ESG reports are more likely to respond on Twitter. This likelihood further increases when the ESG report is assured, suggesting that assurance reinforces the commitment to consistent communication.

When examining hard and soft disclosures separately, we observe that prior ESG disclosures

increase the likelihood of both types of responses. However, an assured ESG report is more strongly associated with a hard disclosure than is an unassured report, indicating that assurance is particularly linked to more substantive disclosures.

Next, we classify negative climate incidents based on their level of public influence and analyze how firms respond. Our findings show that low-influence incidents are typically followed by soft responses, while medium-influence ones prompt hard disclosures. High-influence incidents, by contrast, result in both hard and soft responses. This supports the idea that higher-influence incidents pressure firms to respond across multiple fronts.

Finally, our findings suggest that prior ESG disclosures play a dual role in shaping firms' responses to negative climate incidents. For low-influence incidents, firms with prior ESG reports are more likely to issue soft disclosures than firms without such reports, likely using their established credibility to provide empty, low-cost responses. In contrast, for medium-influence incidents that attract significant public attention, firms—particularly those with assured ESG reports—are more likely to issue hard disclosures, addressing heightened stakeholder expectations with substantive information. This pattern highlights the trade-off firms face between minimizing disclosure costs and avoiding reputational damage from withholding material information. When comparing firms with assured ESG reports to those with unassured reports, we find that the observed effects are primarily concentrated among the former firms.

We contribute to the literature in three main ways. First, our findings contribute to dynamic disclosure theory by demonstrating how prior disclosures can act as both a buffer enabling empty responses in low-pressure contexts and a facilitator that enhances the credibility of substantive communication under scrutiny. While models of reputation building often emphasize truthful disclosure in cheap talk settings (e.g., Kim, 1996; Stocken, 2000), our evidence aligns with the notion that firms strategically balance hard and soft disclosures (Bertomeu and Marinovic, 2016). This balancing act is consistent with the interpretation that prior disclosures may create a “real option” for firms to tailor their responses according to stakeholder expectations (Aghamolla and An, 2021). By highlighting how firms adapt their communication strategies to different levels of reputational risk, we extend the literature on how firms manage the costs and benefits of disclosure (Gigler and Hemmer, 1998; Ball, Jayaraman, and Shivakumar, 2012).

Second, we extend the literature on ESG disclosures and voluntary communication by focusing on social media as a disclosure channel. Research has examined ESG disclosures in reports (Rouen, Sachdeva, and Yoon, 2024), conference calls (Sautner et al., 2023a), and general corporate communications (Crowley et al., 2019). We add to this body of work by showing that soft disclosures constitute the majority of climate-related discussions on social media and that the choice between hard and soft disclosures is context-specific, depending on prior disclosures and the influence of the incident.

Our study complements the work of Crowley et al. (2019), who examine the relationship between CSR performance and the volume and tone of tweets, by focusing specifically on firms' real-time communication strategies in response to negative climate-related incidents. Whereas Crowley et al. (2019) analyze how firms use Twitter to enhance their overall CSR narrative, our study explores the cost-benefit trade-offs firms face when deciding whether and how to disclose information during reputational crises. We also acknowledge related concurrent work by Pu, Shan, and Yuan (2024), who examine corporate ESG-related disclosures on Twitter following negative events. Although both studies document that firms respond to ESG incidents on social media, our study develops a more refined approach to distinguishing between hard and soft disclosures and provides additional insights into the conditions under which firms opt for substantive communication versus symbolic gestures.

Third, we contribute to the growing literature on the use of natural language processing (NLP) in finance and accounting research. Although studies have used textual analysis to detect green-washing or infer soft information (Crowley et al., 2019; De la Parra, 2022), our approach focuses on the contextual meaning of words rather than simple linguistic features. We extend the application of BERT-based models (e.g., Bingler et al., 2022; Huang, Wang, and Yang, 2023) to a relatively underexplored setting of ESG disclosures on social media. Our application of BERT enables us to differentiate between hard and soft disclosures, providing a methodological framework for future research on corporate communications.

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Identification of climate change disclosure on Twitter

Our aim is to identify climate change disclosures from tweets. This task is challenging due to the special features of Twitter posts and climate change disclosures. Prior approaches to text classification include dictionary-based methods and machine learning methods (Brown, Hinson, and Tucker, 2024; Huang, Wang, and Yang, 2023; Bingler et al., 2022). A simple dictionary-based method requires researchers to predefine word lists (dictionaries) to identify the desired text. That is easy to implement, validate, and interpret and thus is widely used (e.g., Lerman, 2020; Ang et al., 2021). Machine learning methods, on the other hand, consist of either well developed machine learning models (e.g., naive Bayes, decision tree, and LDA) or more recent deep learning models such as CNN or BERT. These methods generally require a labeled training dataset and predict the classification. $ ^{1} $ All these approaches have strengths and limitations.

On the one hand, a simple dictionary-based method does not incorporate the semantic features of the text. It classifies all tweets with hits as positive, regardless of the context of the words or bigrams. Ignoring context might dramatically increase the number of Type I errors in text classification (Bochkay et al., 2023). For example, the bigram $ ^{2} $ “climate change” can have different meanings. When associated with other bigrams, like “Ice Age” or “mammoth extinction,” it probably refers to the global warming that happened 2.6 million years ago. On the contrary, if it occurs with “future target” or “reduce emissions,” it very likely describes firms’ current efforts to combat climate change. To improve identification, we need a filter for bigrams based on their exact meanings within sentences.

The implementation of a keyword-based approach is also very challenging in our setting. It is hard to predefine a comprehensive list of keywords for climate change disclosures. Climate change encompasses many topics within ESG disclosures. In addition, the language used on Twitter often includes unique features (e.g., hashtags) that do not appear elsewhere. Therefore, it is

inappropriate to use corporate reports, newspapers, or textbooks as benchmarks for extracting bigrams.

Furthermore, the issue of Type II errors can also undermine the validity of the bigram approach. This issue is particularly severe in our setting because Twitter limits the number of characters in a single tweet, making meaningful bigrams less detectable. This limit was 140 characters before November 2017 and 280 characters thereafter. In other words, a tweet typically contains fewer than about 30 words (around 29 bigrams). The selection of meaningful words and bigrams within a 29-bigram tweet is highly restrictive, making the classification less reliable.

The use of machine learning-based approaches also has certain limitations in our setting. Traditional supervised machine learning models, like naive Bayes and random forest, are often based on the bag-of-words structure, which means they ignore much of the sentence context and how individual words contribute to this context. This limitation is manifested in two ways.

First, a word or bigram has the same embedding across sentences and is treated the same, irrespective of its position relative to other words, inhibiting distinguishing words with multiple meanings. Second, across closely related words, we may find embeddings that are quite different from one another. These limitations of traditional machine learning models can hinder the accurate representation of relationships. Although deep learning approaches can address this issue, they generally require a large training set (Gentzkow, Kelly, and Taddy, 2019). In our setting, there is no existing annotated sample of corporate tweets, and a manually labeled training sample consisting of only a few climate change tweets can severely limit the ability of machine learning models to extract enough variation to make accurate predictions. What's more, the imbalanced nature of climate change tweets biases the algorithms toward the majority group (i.e., nonclimate change tweets). The limited size of the training sample, combined with the imbalanced dataset can weaken the learning ability of these models (Krawczyk, 2016; Madabushi, Kochkina, and Castelle, 2020).

We overcome these identification challenges by building a deep learning approach based on a training sample identified by a dictionary-based approach. We proceed in two steps: (1) constructing the training sample and (2) training the model. In the first step, the training sample relies mainly on a relatively complete bigram and hashtag list built with a self-discovering key

word model (King, Lam, and Roberts, 2017; Sautner et al., 2023a), which maximizes the ability to capture the language pattern of climate change tweets in the training sample. In the second step, we use the training sample to fine-tune a pre-trained BERT model, which integrates the context of words within tweets and mitigates the Type I and Type II errors of the dictionary-based approach. With our two-step model, we maintain the quality of the training sample and take advantage of a context-based deep learning model that provides better identification of climate change tweets. #### Construction of the training sample

Our training sample for climate tweets is comprised of two parts. In the first, we randomly select and manually annotate 10,000 corporate tweets. Firms use Twitter to disclose a variety of information, so it is unsurprising that the majority of this random sample is unrelated to climate change. After labeling, we can only identify 182 climate change tweets, a sample that is so small that it is unlikely to cover all aspects of climate change.

To address this issue, we build the second part of our training sample from tweets identified by a set of bigrams and hashtags that represent the language patterns of climate change topics. This set is based on a machine learning keyword self-discovery algorithm developed by King, Lam, and Roberts (2017). This approach was introduced by Sautner et al. (2023a) and Sautner et al. (2023b) in the accounting and finance literature to capture both overall and disaggregated climate change topics and measure firms' climate change exposure based on conference call transcripts. The aim of this approach is to produce a list of unique bigrams related to the topic that can be used for in-text search. In our setting, we use this list to find tweets related to climate change. To implement this approach, we require a researcher-defined initial set of bigrams,  $ K_{R} $, a randomized search corpus set S from the original corpus, and a randomized reference corpus set R. Unlike other keyword algorithms, this approach identifies target tweets based on the initial bigram, uses supervised machine learning models to make predictions for previously nonclimate change tweets, and extracts new bigrams based on newly identified climate change-related tweets. In other words, it adaptively expands the bigram list without extra inputs.

We illustrate the algorithm construction procedure in Figure 2. Our first step is to construct

an initial set of bigrams  $ K_{R_{1b}} $. In this step, we first select five leading climate change nonprofit organizations (NGOs), which we believe are most relevant and up-to-date in terms of the linguistic features of climate change tweets $ ^{3} $ and extract all of their tweets as our material for  $ K_{R_{1b}} $. Then we embed the bigrams from those tweets with a pre-trained BERT model. $ ^{4} $ To extract the most relevant bigrams, for each tweet, we choose the top five candidate bigrams with the highest cosine similarity between the bigram embedding and the tweet embedding. Finally, we aggregate these candidates for each NGO’s Twitter account and select the most frequent 10 bigrams per account. $ ^{5} $ We repeat this process to get the initial set of hashtags  $ K_{R_{1h}} $. Combining  $ K_{R_{1b}} $ and  $ K_{R_{1h}} $, we obtain a preliminary set  $ K_{R_{1}} $, consisting of 35 initial bigrams and 16 initial hashtags that are commonly mentioned in all NGO tweets. We report these initial bigrams and hashtags in Table 1.

Next we use randomized firms’ tweet samples to construct the search set $S$ and the reference set $R$. We define $R_1$ as a set of documents that contains $K_{R_1}$ and $S_1$ as the set of documents that does not contain $K_{R_1}$.$^{6}$ After that, using a random forest algorithm,$^{7}$ we extract features from the reference set $R_1$ and predict $S_1$ on whether the tweet relates to climate change. Based on the predictions, we produce a new set of bigrams $K_{R_2}$ that can best distinguish climate change and nonclimate tweets. Then we construct the new search and reference set, $S_2$ and $R_2$, based on $K_{R_2}$. In theory, this process can be repeated several times to expand the key bigram size until $K_{R_n}$ does not produce any meaningful bigrams. Note that the selection of bigrams from $K_{R_n}$ and the repetition of this process is generally up to the researchers’ discretion. To minimize potential Type I errors, we retain the top 15% bigrams and remove those that are less likely to correlate with climate change issues. We repeat the process three times for bigrams and four times for hashtags, as the number of meaningful keywords becomes very marginal after that.$^{8}$

We use  $ \sum_{i=1}^{i=4} K_{R_i} $ as the final bigram list.

We apply the algorithm discussed above to both bigrams and hashtags. Finally, we identify 294 bigrams and 143 hashtags in our final keyword list (as reported in Tables 1 and 2). We randomly select 2,000 tweets identified as climate-change related and conduct manual checks to minimize Type I errors. In the end, the second part of the training sample consists of 1,471 climate and 529 nonclimate tweets. In sum, our training sample contains 12,000 tweets, with 13.8% of them relating to climate change.

#### Training the BERT model

To maximize the utility of our training sample and address the potential Type I and Type II errors embedded in a single keyword approach, we further train a BERT model to tackle the climate change tweets classification problem at its root.

BERT is a state-of-the-art large language model that was trained based on millions of documents, including Wikipedia, and was made available to developers in 2018 (Devlin et al., 2018). Unlike other supervised machine learning models, BERT inherits attention layers from Transformers and specifically trains the algorithm on two tasks: masked-language-modeling (MLM) and next-sentence-prediction (NSP). It compares words and their orders within a sentence with each other and simultaneously with all other sentences to capture the contextual meaning of a word. This information can then be transferred to downstream tasks. All these differences enable the model to better incorporate context. $ ^{9} $ More importantly, the model can also summarize hidden language patterns that are either neglected in the training sample or not identifiable by a bigram approach. Therefore, the BERT model is more likely than other approaches to resolve concerns related to Type I and II errors. We illustrate the idea of how we fine-tune the model for our classification task in Figure 3.

We next use a pre-trained BERT model from Covid-Twitter BERT (CT-BERT) developed by Müller, Salathé, and Kummervold (2020). This pre-trained model is based on BERT-LARGE and was trained on 160 million Covid-related English tweets. Compared to other pre-trained models, CT-BERT is more suitable in our setting, as it was trained based on a context-like

our corporate and NGO tweets. To further familiarize the model with corporate language on Twitter, also conduct domain adaptive training (masked-language modeling task), using 100,000 randomly selected corporate tweets, add another classification layer, and create our own model, Climate-TwitterBERT. To fine-tune and evaluate the model's performance, we split our sample into training (70%), validation (10%), and test (20%) sets. Climate-TwitterBERT outperforms other pre-trained models, with a higher F1-Score, including BERT-base (Devlin et al., 2018), DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019), RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019), ClimateBERT (trained with articles about climate change and corporate sustainability reports by Bingler et al. (2024)), FinBERT (trained with financial texts, including corporate annual or quarterly filings, analyst reports, and conference call transcripts by Huang, Wang, and Yang (2023)), and CT-BERT without fine-tuning with the masked language model (Müller, Salathé, and Kummervold, 2020). We optimize the model with the validation set using a grid search of the following hyperparameters: batch Size (B), number of epochs (E), learning rate ( $ \eta $), and weight decay (w). We also add 5% of the total training steps as a warm-up.

We then evaluate the performance of different optimized models in the downstream task, specifically text classification, using the test set. The evaluation metrics we present include loss, F1 score, and accuracy in Table OA1 and Figure OA4. The metrics indicate that our model, ClimateTwitterBERT, significantly outperforms our pre-trained models, consistent with our prior that Climate-TwitterBERT, with domain adapted fine-tuning, can better capture the language features of corporate tweets.

Finally, we apply the model to predict climate change-related tweets across the full sample of all 35 million corporate tweets and label a tweet as positive only if the predicted probability exceeds 95%. $ ^{10} $ In total, we identify 136,899 corporate tweets related to climate change.

### 2.2 Categorizing climate tweets: hard, soft, or promotional

Our next task is to differentiate between various types of climate change tweets. These tweets can convey different types of information: hard information, such as significant improvements in emissions; soft information, like expressions of support for the Paris Agreement; and promotional

content related to climate change. To ensure accurate classification, it is crucial to establish clear rules for distinguishing between these categories in our training samples.

We define ‘hard information’ as content that presents identifiable and measurable patterns, which can be used to quantify a firm’s efforts and commitment to combating climate change. The specific classification rules we use to construct the training sample are outlined in Table A2. The Climate-TwitterBERT should then be able to extract other hidden language patterns associated with what we identify in the training sample.

Note that promotional tweets, which are prevalent in our sample, may share similar language patterns with hard and soft information. However, the messages to stakeholders differ. To address the risk of misclassification, we introduce "promotion" as a third category, ensuring that our model accurately reflects the distinctiveness of these tweets.

Figure 1 and Figure 4 illustrate each step of our approach to categorize the climate tweets. To classify these tweets empirically, we rely on our pre-trained BERT model and fine-tune it with a new training sample. We build a training sample from 2,000 randomly selected climate change-related tweets identified in the previous task. We label the tweets manually. In the final training sample, the distribution across the three categories is as follows: hard (30.3%), soft (50.2%), and promotion (19.5%).

We fine-tune the model on the multi-class classification tasks with the training sample. Our algorithm categorizes a climate tweet into one of the three content groups based on the highest assigned probability fit. Empirically, if the algorithm struggles to classify a tweet, the highest probability assigned by the model should be relatively small, given the algorithm considers an equal chance for some of the categories. Figure 5 shows the performance evaluation of our fine-tuned BERT model. As this model is designed for multi-class classification, the overall accuracy is naturally lower compared to the initial text classification task. $ ^{11} $ Overall we achieve a prediction performance (as measured by F1 Score) of 78% on our test set, indicating that our model can identify the three categories of firms' climate change tweets.

### 2.3 Validation of the categorization

We conduct two tests to validate our three categories. First, we correlate each indicator of different categories of climate tweet (i.e., hard, soft, and promotion) with four proxies for disclosure firmness from De la Parra (2022): specificity, vagueness, numerical intensity, and objectivity. Following Hope, Hu, and Lu (2016), we define specificity as the number of details (e.g., time, location, or organization) mentioned in a tweet. Vagueness measures the number of vague words, as defined by Loughran and McDonald (2011) (e.g., ambiguous, approximately, probable). Numerical Intensity indicates whether a tweet contains hard numbers following Blankespoor (2019). We employ the long short-term memory model trained by De la Parra (2022) to assess whether a tweet is objective. We present the correlation tables in panel A of Table 3. At the tweet level, we find that the hard climate tweet indicator is positively correlated with specificity, numerical intensity, and objectivity and negatively correlated with vagueness. In contrast, the soft climate tweet indicator is negatively correlated with specificity and numerical intensity and positively correlated with vagueness. The association between soft climate tweet and objectivity is statistically insignificant, which is expected, as a soft climate tweet may include descriptions of scientific facts or industry trends. Regarding promotional tweets, we find that they are less specific, less vague, more numerically intensive, and less objective, indicating that they differ from both hard and soft climate tweets.

Second, we analyze Twitter users' reactions to different types of tweets. Using a tweet-level dataset and a fixed-effects Poisson model (Cohn, Liu, and Wardlaw, 2022), we test whether hard climate tweets generate more user engagement within the same firm-year. Our results show that hard climate tweets receive significantly more likes, retweets, and replies than do soft climate tweets, while the difference between soft climate tweets and promotional tweets is statistically insignificant. This evidence suggests that Twitter users can distinguish between hard and soft climate tweets.

## 3 Sample construction

We collected our sample from multiple databases and through self-construction. Table 4 provides an overview of the sample construction process. Table 5 presents the descriptive statistics of our

main variables. To gather firms' voluntary disclosures on Twitter, we start with all U.S. public firms in Compustat from 2007 to 2020. We identify U.S. firms by extracting firms' historical headquarters from SEC filings, resulting in a sample of 5,820 U.S. firms. To identify each firm's unique Twitter account name, we extract their official Twitter accounts from their official websites. $ ^{12} $ We then exclude firms that either do not have a Twitter account or do not tweet during our sample period. This leaves us with 2,735 firms with valid Twitter accounts. Using Twitter's advanced search function, we then collect a total of 35 million historical tweets.

Most of our analyses are conducted on the firm-day level. To create a sample at the firm-day level, we first construct a complete time series for each firm, spanning from the day of the first tweet to the end of the sample period. This process results in a full panel dataset consisting of 7,956,035 observations. Next we merge this firm-day level data with ESG incidents data from RepRisk, additional ESG data from Refinitiv Eikon, firms' fundamentals from Compustat, trading data from CRSP, filings data from SEC EDGAR, and Twitter sentiment data from Bloomberg. We apply the following filters to our merged dataset: First, we exclude delisted firms and those suspended from trading for more than one year. $ ^{13} $ We also excluded observations that could not be matched to the RepRisk or Refinitiv Eikon databases. Ultimately, our final sample consists of 3,137,468 firm-day observations covering 1,958 unique firms from March 30, 2007, to August 22, 2020. $ ^{14} $

### 3.2 The measurement of ESG incidents

We use data from the RepRisk database to capture firm-related ESG incidents. RepRisk has tracked ESG incidents globally across all sectors since 2007, drawing from media, stakeholders, and third-party sources, such as regulators and social media, while excluding corporate self-reporting. The database provides firm-day level risk incidents across approximately 30 major ESG topics, including environmental footprint, community contribution, employees, and corporate governance. Each incident is assigned a reach level, referred to as the influence level, from 1 (lowest) to 3 (highest). For our analyses, we select seven issues related to climate change, including animal mistreatment, climate change, greenhouse gas emissions, global pollution, impacts on landscapes, ecosystems, and biodiversity, local pollution, waste issues, other environmental issues, and overuse and wasting of resources. $ ^{15} $ Furthermore, we categorize the incidents into 8,758 climate change incidents and 21,172 nonclimate-change ESG incidents. $ ^{16} $ The distribution of incidents across sectors is reported in Table 5 panel C, which shows that firms in the energy and utility sectors account for about 48% of all negative climate change incidents in our sample.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Do negative climate change incidents trigger disclosures on climate change on Twitter?

Table 6 presents our findings from the first main test, which aims to examine whether firms tweet about climate change after experiencing a negative incident related to it. For robustness,

we employ various model specifications, the details of which are explained below.

 $$ \begin{aligned}Climate\ Tweet_{i,t}&=\beta_{1}Climate Incident[-3,0]_{i,t}+\beta_{2}Climate Incident[-7,-4]_{i,t}\\&+\beta_{3}Other ESG Incident[-3,0]_{i,t}+\beta_{4}Other ESG Incident[-7,-4]_{i,t}\\&+\beta_{5}Prior5D Return_{i,t}+\beta_{6}Filings10K_{i,t}+\beta_{7}Filings10Q_{i,t}\\&+\beta_{8}Filings8K_{i,t}+\beta_{9}NonclimateTweet_{i,t}+Fixed effects+\epsilon_{i,t}\\ \end{aligned} $$ 

Climate  $ T_{weet_{i,t}} $ indicates whether firm i issues any tweets related to climate change on day t. We use a dummy variable for several reasons. First, due to the character limit on Twitter, firms can send multiple tweets as part of a single disclosure. Second, firms sometimes reply to different users’ similar inquiries with nearly identical tweets, resulting in repetitive messages.  $ ClimateIncident_{i,t} $ ( $ OtherESGIncident_{i,t} $) stands for whether the firm experienced a negative climate change-related incident (a negative ESG incident unrelated to climate change) during the corresponding time window. We focus on the [-3, 0] time window as the primary period for analyzing firms’ responses to negative incidents, with [-7, -4] serving as a control window. $ ^{17} $

We also control for several firm-day level characteristics. First, we include Prior5DReturn $ _{i,t} $ to control for the impact of events in the preceding days. Second, we control for periodic and current filings (10-Ks, 10-Qs, and 8-Ks) to rule out the potential influence of concurrent information releases. Lastly, we include Nonclimate Tweet $ _{i,t} $, an indicator equal to one if the firm issues at least one nonclimate tweet on the same day, to account for a possible Type I error in identifying climate tweets. By using data at the firm-day level, we can employ different types of fixed effects to examine variation.

In our main analyses, we choose to use the linear probability model (LPM) as our primary model because Timoneda (2021) shows that it outperforms maximum likelihood specifications, such as logit or probit, in accuracy and predicted probabilities when fixed effects are included and the dependent variable has less than 25 percent of ones. Consequently, some recent studies prefer LPM over nonlinear models in the presence of rare events and fixed effects (e.g. Dang et al.,

2022; Li and Wu, 2022). For robustness, we repeat our analyses (including the subsequent ones) with a BRFE probit model in Section F of the online appendix.

In column 1, we present our baseline result without including fixed effects. We find significant and positive coefficients (0.0400 and 0.0316, p-value<0.01) for the key variables ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0] $ and ClimateIncident  $ [-7,-4] $, suggesting a strong association between firm-specific negative climate change news and the firm's voluntary climate change disclosures on Twitter.

In columns 2 and 3, we repeat the previous test and apply Day and Industry fixed effects and Day and Firm fixed effects, respectively. We find that, after we replace the industry fixed effects with firm fixed effects, the variables OtherESGIncident  $ [-3,0] $ and OtherESGIncident  $ [-7,-4] $ are no longer correlated with the incidence of climate tweets, suggesting that firms on average do not tend to respond to nonclimate incidents with climate tweets.

In column 4, we apply a more stringent fixed effects structure by controlling for  $ Industry \times Day $ and Firm fixed effects. In columns 5 and 6, we further tighten our fixed effects structure by applying  $ Industry \times Day $ with  $ Firm \times Year $, and  $ Industry \times Day $ with  $ Firm \times Month $ fixed effects, respectively. Our results remain robust. For subsequent analyses, we use the most stringent fixed effects structure from column 6, as it isolates the within-firm effect by removing the industry-day-specific and firm-month-specific effects, which could significantly influence the firm's disclosure decisions. We interpret the coefficient (0.0047) in column 6 to mean that experiencing a negative climate change incident within the window from day t-3 to day t increases the probability of issuing a climate-related tweet on day t by 36.7% (=0.0047/0.0128). In column 6, we do not find a significant coefficient for  $ ClimateIncident[-7,-4] $, suggesting that the responses are likely to concentrate in the short window (i.e., three days) following the negative climate change incident. Regarding control variables, climate tweets are less likely to be issued on the same day as when the firm issues nonclimate tweets but more likely on the day of 8-K filings.

In column 7, we refine the definition of climate tweets to include promotional tweets. Using this definition, a negative climate incident increases the likelihood of relevant responses on Twitter by 27.3%.

In column 8, we remove energy firms (TRBC sector code: 50) from our sample to address concerns of spurious regression. The problem of spurious regression may arise if both negative incidents

and climate change tweets are very frequent. Given that the business models of energy firms are closely tied to climate change, they are very likely to discuss climate change issues, irrespective of negative incidents. As shown in panel C, Table 5, the energy sector has the highest share of negative climate change incidents (31.83%) in our sample. Repeating our test in column 6 without energy firms, we find that the main coefficient for ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0] $ increases from 0.0047 in column 6 to 0.0056 in column 8 and remains significant at the 1% level, indicating our results remain robust even when excluding energy firms.

In the last column, we switch from the linear probability model to a bias-reduced fixed effects (BRFE) probit model developed by Kunz, Staub, and Winkelmann (2021) as a robustness check. Due to computational limits, we only include firm fixed effects in this test, along with several firm characteristics as controls: ROA, BTM, Total assets, and Leverage. We find a positive significant coefficient (0.2061) for the variable ClimateIncident  $ [-3, 0] $. We calculate an approximate upper bound for the effect size in this specification: for a firm with an equal chance of issuing a climate tweet, a negative climate change incident increases the probability of issuing a climate tweet by approximately 8.24 percentage points  $ (=0.2061 \times 0.4) $, which approaches the effect size we find in column 4 when we include firm and industry×day fixed effects.

Overall, our findings indicate a significant increase in the likelihood of firms tweeting about climate change following a negative climate change incident. In addition, there is little evidence to support that firms are likely to tweet about climate change after experiencing negative nonclimate incidents. These results suggest that firms use Twitter to respond immediately to negative climate change incidents and that their responses are likely to be climate-related.

### 4.2 Does prior disclosure influence firms' responses to negative climate change incidents?

#### 4.2.1 Does prior ESG disclosure increase the tendency of firms to issue climate tweets following negative climate incidents?

Table 7 presents our analyses of how firms' prior disclosures affect their voluntary disclosures of climate-related issues after experiencing a negative climate incident. We estimate the following linear probability model:

Climate  $ T_{weet_{i,t}} = \beta_{1}ClimateIncident \left[-3,0\right]_{i,t} + \beta_{2}OtherESGIncident \left[-3,0\right]_{i,t} $

+ (β3ClimateIncident [−3, 0]i,t + β4OtherESGIncident [−3, 0]i,t) × Early Disclosure i,t (2)

 $$ +\gamma_{1}Controls_{i,t}+\gamma_{2}Controls_{i,t}\times Early Disclosure_{i,t}+\eta_{j\times t}+\mu_{i\times m}+\epsilon_{i,t} $$ 

In this set of analyses, we stick to the most stringent fixed effect structure, which incorporates both firm (i)×month (m) and industry (j)×day (t) effects. This controls for the impact of industry-wide news on a given date and firm characteristics that remain constant in a given month. Our setting allows for such a strict fixed effects structure, due to meaningful variation at the firm-month and industry-day levels. We use the same set of controls as in our previous analyses. In column 1, we employ a composite measure ESG DisclosureScore from Refinitiv Eikon as a proxy for Early Disclosure. This score reflects the firm's practices in communicating how it integrates ESG considerations into its decision-making. We find a negative but insignificant coefficient for ClimateIncident  $ [-3, 0] $. However, the interaction term ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0] \times ESG DisclosureScore $ is significantly positive. This suggests that firms with higher ESG disclosure scores in the previous year are more likely to respond to climate change incidents with climate tweets.

Next we repeat the analyses with different early disclosure proxies. In column 2, we examine whether having an ESG report increases the likelihood of the company responding to negative climate change incidents on Twitter. We document a positive and significant coefficient for ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0] \times ESG\ Report $, indicating that firms with ESG reports in the previous year are more likely to respond on Twitter when they experience negative climate change incidents.

In column 3, we further break down the firms with ESG reports into two categories: ESG Report Assured, and ESG Report Unassured. These are indicator variables that equal one if the ESG report in the previous year is assured or unassured, respectively. Assurance aims to enhance the credibility of the ESG reports. Consistent with this argument, we find that firms with assured ESG reports are significantly more likely to respond to negative climate change incidents on Twitter than those with unassured ESG reports. This suggests that the more credible firms are more likely to use their disclosures to manage their reputations.

In summary, we find that firms’ previous ESG disclosures increase their likelihood of issuing climate tweets as a response to negative climate change incidents. Moreover, the increase is more prominent when the early disclosure is harder (e.g., higher ESG disclosure scores or assured ESG reports). Interestingly, we also find that the coefficients of OtherESGIncident  $ [-3,0] $ × Early disclosure are significant and positive in all columns, suggesting that firms with better ESG disclosure histories may use climate information to respond to nonclimate ESG incidents. This aligns with the argument that prior disclosure may provide a buffer for the company, allowing it to respond with irrelevant information during reputational crises.

#### 4.2.2 Hard versus soft responses

In Table 8, we examine how early disclosure correlates with hard versus soft responses. We repeat our last set of analyses with different dependent variables. $ ^{18} $ In columns 1 through 3, we present the results using Hard Climate Tweet as the dependent variable. We repeat the early disclosure proxies we have in Table 7. We show that the interaction terms ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0]\times ESG Disclosure Score (ESG Report) $ have a significantly positive coefficient in column 1 (2), suggesting that early disclosure is associated with the provision of both hard and soft responses.

In column 3, we further partition the firms with ESG reports into those with assured ESG reports and those with unassured ESG reports in the previous year. We show that the coefficient of ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0]\times ESG Report\ Unassured $ is insignificant while that of ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0]\times ESG Report\ Assured $ is significantly positive. We compare the two coefficients and find that they differ significantly. This finding suggests that firms with unassured ESG reports, which are considered softer disclosures than assured ESG reports, are no more likely to respond with hard information to negative climate incidents than firms without ESG reports. In contrast, an assured ESG report is associated with a higher likelihood of a hard response when negative climate incidents occur.

Regarding Soft Climate Tweet as the dependent variable, we present the results in columns 4 through 6. In each column, we document a negative and significant coefficient for the variable ClimateIncident  $ [-3, 0] $, which indicates that soft climate tweets are unlikely responses to negative

incidents in the absence of ESG reports. We also find that firms with prior disclosures are more likely to issue soft climate tweets when a negative climate change incident occurs than firms without prior disclosures. In column 6, we document significant and positive coefficients for both ClimateIncident  $ [-3,0] \times ESG\ Report\ Unassured\ and\ ClimateIncident\ [-3,0] \times ESG\ Report\ Assured $, suggesting that both unassured and assured ESG reports are associated with a higher likelihood of soft responses.

In summary, our results suggest that firms without prior disclosures tend to avoid providing soft responses and are likely unable to provide hard responses. In contrast, firms with prior ESG disclosures have higher flexibility in using either hard or soft information in response to negative climate change incidents. We argue that this confirms the dual role of prior disclosure: it can act as a buffer, shielding firms, or as a facilitator, promoting credible reporting.

Interestingly, we also find that firms with unassured ESG reports are more likely to respond to nonclimate negative ESG incidents with hard climate tweets, while firms with assured ESG reports are more likely to respond with soft tweets. Although in both cases the climate-related responses are considered irrelevant to the nonclimate incident, it appears that the firmness of prior ESG disclosure is associated with the firmness of the irrelevant response.

### 4.3 Do negative incidents with different levels of influence lead to different responses?

In Table 9, we examine the effect of climate change incidents on Twitter responses based on the influence of the incidents. We follow the definition of RepRisk to classify each climate incident into the categories of low, medium, and high influence. According to RepRisk, an incident's influence level is determined by the media attention it receives. An incident is of low influence if it is reported by local media, smaller NGOs, local governments, and social media. A medium-influence incident is covered by national and regional media, international NGOs, and state, national, and international governments. The highly influential incidents are those that are covered by international media outlets, such as The New York Times, BBC, and SCMP. Incidents with medium influence (47%) comprise the majority of our sample, followed by low influence (44%).

In column 1, we show that the incidents with medium or high levels of influence are likely

to trigger a company response on Twitter within three days. However, we do not observe a significant increase in the likelihood of climate tweets following a low-influence negative climate incident. A comparison of the coefficients indicates that the likelihood of responding increases with the incident's level of influence. Examining the different types of responses in columns 2 and 3, we find that low-influence incidents are likely to be followed by soft responses, while medium-influence incidents are likely to be followed by hard ones. High-influence incidents tend to trigger both hard and soft responses.

We also examine how different types of incidents moderate the impact of early ESG disclosure on firms' responses. In Table 10, we interact our early disclosure proxies with different types of negative climate change incidents. Our results here further illustrate the trade-offs companies face when dealing with negative climate incidents.

We find that firms with an ESG report, particularly an assured report from the previous year, tend to issue soft responses when the negative incident is of low influence. This suggests that the penalty for exploiting the option generated by early disclosure is likely smaller than the associated disclosure costs, making it more likely for companies to exercise this option. Meanwhile, an assured ESG report is positively associated with hard responses to negative incidents of medium influence. This is likely due to the significantly higher penalty for withholding material information, prompting companies to reassess their disclosure strategies. Additionally, a previously assured ESG report enhances the credibility of their voluntary disclosures, resulting in lower certification costs. Consequently, with medium-influence incidents, the facilitating effect of early disclosure appears to outweigh its buffer effect. For highly influential incidents, the likelihood of soft responses increases if the firm has an ESG report, assured or not. Our further analyses reveal that this effect is mainly driven by a small subsample that exhibits a relatively high frequency of high-influence negative incidents.

## 5 Additional analyses

### 5.1 Stakeholder reactions to responses on Twitter

We examine how stakeholders respond to firms' climate change tweets following negative climate incidents. Using Twitter sentiment data from Bloomberg, we find that medium- and high-

influence incidents significantly lower sentiment, while low-influence incidents show no significant effect. As shown in Table OA3, soft responses improve sentiment after low-influence incidents but further decrease sentiment following medium-influence incidents, suggesting that soft disclosures may be viewed as inadequate under heightened public scrutiny.

### 5.2 Big 4 versus non-Big 4 assurance

We analyze how different assurance providers influence firms' Twitter responses to negative climate incidents, with results shown in Table OA4. Within the  $ [0,+3] $ window after low-influence incidents, non-Big 4 assured firms are more likely to issue soft responses, a pattern also observed for Big 4 assured firms. For medium-influence incidents, non-Big 4 assured firms tend to issue hard responses, while Big 4 assured firms primarily issue soft responses. For high-influence incidents, both unassured and non-Big 4 assured firms favor soft responses within the  $ [0,+3] $ window, with no significant pattern detected for Big 4 assured firms. These findings suggest that Big 4 and non-Big 4 assured firms adopt different disclosure strategies during the initial response period following negative climate incidents.

### 5.3 Exclude firm-years with a high proportion of promotional tweets

One concern in our analyses is whether our results are driven by the firms that heavily promote their own climate-related products or services. As the boundary between hard/soft climate tweets and promotional tweets is particularly blurred for these firms, we perform a robustness check by replicating Table 10 with a sample that excludes them. We define a firm-year as a heavy-promotion firm-year if 10% or more of the days on which the firm posts climate tweets are exclusively promotional tweets. We present the results in Table OA5 and show that the results are robust to excluding firm-years with a high proportion of promotional tweets.

### 5.4 Exclude frequent offenders

In Table 10, we observe that firms are more likely to provide a soft response after experiencing high-influence incidents. Although high-influence incidents show a different pattern, they are scarce and mainly concentrated in a small sample of firms. To further understand this result, we identify a subset of firm-years with more than two days with high-influence incidents for a given firm in a given year and call them “frequent offenders.” These firms consistently experience

high-influence incidents and may not view these incidents as shocks. Thus, we contend that they may adopt different strategies than other firms. We reconduct our empirical analyses on the frequent offenders subsample and the rest of the firms. Results in Table OA6 indicate that our previous observation of firms' responses to high influence only manifests in the frequent offenders subsample. Our main results remain robust after excluding frequent offenders.

## 6 Conclusion

We analyze the cost-benefit trade-offs firms face when deciding to voluntarily disclose climate-related information on Twitter following negative climate incidents. We find that, on average, firms are more inclined to provide relevant disclosures on Twitter following negative climate-related incidents. Our analyses reveal that firms are more likely to respond to the incidents if they have previously disclosed hard ESG information (e.g., ESG reports). Additionally, we document that firms with prior hard climate disclosures are more likely to provide soft responses on Twitter when the negative incident attracts low media attention, compared to firms with no hard climate disclosures in the past year. In contrast, for incidents of medium influence, prior hard climate disclosures are associated with a higher likelihood of providing hard responses.

Our findings have several important implications. First, they suggest that Twitter is an important platform for climate change disclosures, enabling firms to quickly communicate with stakeholders, particularly in response to negative incidents. Second, we address the debate over climate disclosure and greenwashing. Our descriptive statistics show that about 65 percent of the climate change tweets in our sample are soft (i.e., either unverifiable corporate claims or general information about climate change) or promotional tweets about the company's own green products or services. Even in the presence of negative climate incidents, firms sometimes issue soft climate tweets if they have built a sufficient reputation by disclosing hard ESG information in the past, especially when their incidents are not influential enough to attract stakeholder attention. Finally, we show that climate change disclosures are intertemporally correlated. In particular, our findings offer empirical insights into dynamic disclosure theory by illustrating that prior disclosures serve both as a buffer and a facilitator for future responses to reputation-damaging incidents. The dominance of one role over the other depends on the incident's influence.

Our paper also speaks to regulators and standard setters. It addresses the information asymme-

try between regulators and firms (Hail, Tahoun, and Wang, 2018; Christensen, Liu, and Maffett, 2020) by illustrating how market power influences firms' information environments in the absence of regulatory intervention. An important takeaway is that firms strategically choose between disclosing hard and soft information based on their assessment of their reputation and the influence of the incident.

Our study does come with caveats and limitations. Our findings are primarily descriptive and based on associations. We caution readers that firms with high-quality ESG disclosures may also exhibit better ESG performance. Given that our sample is limited to U.S. firms, our results are more relevant to the U.S. institutional setting and may not generalize to the reporting practices in other countries. We also acknowledge the limitation of studying only Twitter, as it omits firms that avoid social media but still prioritize timely communication with stakeholders on ESG issues. An interesting extension of our study would be to examine the trade-offs firms make in choosing different disclosure venues for various types of climate-related information and whether firms communicate similarly across different platforms. In general, our study advances the understanding of voluntary climate change disclosure, particularly the disclosure of hard and soft information. We look forward to future studies that examine the impact of climate-related disclosure regulations and how those regulations could alter firms' disclosure practices.

### Figure 3: Identifying climate tweets using Climate-TwitterBERT (step 1)

This figure illustrates the creation of our Climate-TwitterBERT model, which we use to identify climate change tweets in firms' Twitter disclosures. The model's objective is to perform binary classification on each tweet, assigning a probability and label that indicates whether the content pertains to climate change. We select the pre-trained CT-BERT model as our baseline model because it has been pre-trained on millions of Twitter messages and performed best in our initial benchmark tests (presented in Table OA1). We begin with domain-adaptive pre-training for CT-BERT, using a masked language model technique on a randomly sampled set of 100,000 firm tweets. This allows us to optimize model weights to better capture the unique linguistic features of firms' Twitter communications. Next, we create our training dataset, which involves two steps. In the first step, we select 10,000 random tweets and manually label them based on their relevance to climate change topics. This initial labeling reveals a highly imbalanced distribution, with only a small proportion of tweets identified as climate-related. In the second step, to mitigate the imbalance, we leverage a keyword-based approach (outlined in Figure 2) to identify additional climate-related tweets. From this set, we randomly select 2,000 tweets and manually review them to ensure labeling accuracy. This combined approach produces a final training set of 12,000 tweets. We then split the dataset into training (70%), validation (10%), and test (20%) sets. The model is fine-tuned on this labeled data for the downstream task of detecting climate change tweets. Finally, the fine-tuned Climate-TwitterBERT model is applied to the complete set of 35 million corporate tweets to identify climate change-related content.

#### Table 3: Validation for measuring climate tweet types

This table presents the validation tests for our measurement of climate tweets. In Panel A, Specificity indicates the number of specific information mentioned in a tweet (e.g., time, location, or organization). Vagueness captures the number of vague words as defined by Loughran and McDonald (2011) (e.g., ambiguous, approximately, or probable). Numerical intensity indicates whether a tweet contains hard input numbers and is based on Blankespoor (2019). Objectivity indicates whether a tweet is objective and is based on De la Parra (2022). In Panel B, Likes, Retweets, and Replies are measured as the number of respective user activity of the tweet. The estimation method used is Poisson Pseudo Maximum Likelihood (PPML) with high-dimensional fixed effects. All continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% and 99% levels. Standard errors are reported in parentheses.  $ ^{***} $,  $ ^{**} $, and  $ ^{*} $ denote statistical significance at the 1%, 5%, and 10% levels, respectively.

#### Table A2: Climate tweet categorization rules

This table presents the classification rules used to categorize climate change tweets. Our categorization approach is grounded in the theory of hard and soft information disclosure (Bertomeu and Marinovic, 2016). In addition to the existing categories, we introduce an additional category for tweets that promote a firm's products, primarily reflecting marketing efforts. This classification framework is designed to enable our machine learning model to accurately distinguish and group tweets based on their content, meaning, and language patterns.

Hard Climate Tweet</td><td style='text-align: center; word-wrap: break-word;'>A discussion of the company’s verifiable or quantifiable information, which may include past, present, or future actions, commitments, or concrete efforts on climate change or environmental issues, or the dissemination of the company’s sustainability report, awards, or prizes.</td></tr><tr><td style='text-align: center; word-wrap: break-word;'>2.
