# Can generative AI help identify peer firms?

Yi Cao $ ^{1} $ · Long Chen $ ^{1} $ · Jennifer Wu Tucker $ ^{2} $ $ ^{ID} $ · Chi Wan $ ^{3} $

Accepted: 9 May 2025 / Published online: 25 June 2025

© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2025

## Abstract

We evaluate how well generative AI can perform an important task—identifying product market competitors (“peers”). We find that machine-generated peers have a high overlap with the peers identified by human experts as well as with the peers identified by established peer identification systems. Machine-generated peers have high correlations with the focal firm in stock returns, sales growth, and gross profit margin in the subsequent year. The correlations are stronger than those derived from identifying peers by analyzing the similarity of business descriptions in annual reports or by using members in the focal firm’s SIC industry. Machine-generated peers also exhibit higher homogeneity among themselves than those identified via the two alternative systems. We demonstrate the usefulness of machine-generated peers in two settings: (1) compensation benchmarking by investors and (2) hypothesis testing by researchers. Overall, our findings suggest that generative AI can identify peer firms reasonably well, especially for large firms.

## 1 Introduction

Can the advent of generative artificial intelligence (AI), with its applications freely and readily available, help tackle some important yet challenging tasks? We evaluate how well generative AI (the machine) can identify product market

competitors (peers) for a given focal firm. Peer identification is important for investors in at least three aspects of their investments: (1) valuing a stock, (2) evaluating corporate governance, and (3) predicting corporate events (e.g., acquisitions and alliances). Identifying peers would require tremendous efforts in monitoring, acquiring, and integrating data from multiple sources and is therefore challenging, especially for individual investors. Even when data are publicly available, investors must invest time, effort, and resources to become aware of the data and acquire, process, and integrate them (Blankespoor et al. 2019, 2020). The explosive increase of publicly available data in the past two decades might have further hampered investors' ability to find information they need (Stice 2023). The process of peer identification is so complex that some practitioners describe the task as "essentially 'an art form'" (Bhojraj and Lee 2002, p. 408).

Generative AI can collect and aggregate data from a wide range of public sources and may be able to identify peer firms. The generative AI technology we focus on is generative large language models (LLMs). Language models analyze natural language to predict a masked word in a textual sequence or what word will come next (Bochkay et al. 2023). $ ^{1} $ LLMs are deep learning language models that are pre-trained on a large amount of data. The predominant architecture for LLMs is transformers, which have a mechanism to identify textual relations between words in the input text and pay more attention to the parts of text that are more relevant to other parts. As a result, transformers can recognize, classify, translate, and interpret text and predict and generate new text. Because LLMs are pre-trained on a large amount of public data, they possess “knowledge” and can function as information aggregators. Generative LLMs can generate new, coherent, and human-like content in response to user prompts.

As information aggregators, generative LLMs have three unique features. First, they draw data from almost all public sources. In theory, generative LLMs can have extensive knowledge (Kim et al. 2023b). Second, generative LLMs can respond to a user request within seconds. This efficiency can facilitate investors' decision-making in real time. Last, applications of generative LLMs are available to anyone who has an internet connection and a computer or smartphone. For example, OpenAI launched ChatGPT, where GPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformers, on November 30, 2022. Google introduced Bard on March 21, 2023, and renamed it Gemini on February 8, 2024. Both ChapGPT and Bard are built on transformers and are freely available to the public. These three features enable generative LLMs to substantially reduce investors' information costs and relax their resource constraints. $ ^{2} $

For each focal firm in a given year, we use the Bard Application Programming Interface (API) to identify peer firms that are publicly listed in the United States. $ ^{3} $ We require Bard to use its knowledge pool at the end of the specified year and list peer firms in the order of closeness to the focal firm; we refer to the list as “LLM peers.” $ ^{4} $ We start our sample period with 2003—the year when Wikipedia established 100,000 pages—and end it with 2022. We instruct the machine to generate peers not only in a recent year but also at historical snapshots because past peers can be helpful in evaluating a focal firm’s performance pattern. On average, the machine identifies 7.8 peers for a focal firm. For a given firm, about half of the LLM peers identified in the current year remain as peers in the next year.

We evaluate LLM peer identification in four ways. First, we compare the LLM peers for the 40 largest firms in the software industry in 2021 with the peers identified independently by three experts in that industry. On average, the overlap between LLM peers and expert-generated peers is 42.1%, which is substantially higher than chance. $ ^{5} $ Moreover, this rate is higher than the overlap rate of 10.7% between expert-generated peers and the peers identified via the similarity of business descriptions in firms’ 10-K filings, referred to as the text-based network industry classification (TNIC) peers. Therefore, LLM peer identification represents significant signals.

Second, we compare LLM peers with the peers identified via three existing identification systems: (1) TNIC peers, (2) peers based on the intensity of mentioning in analyst reports covering the focal firm during the fiscal year, and (3) compensation-benchmarking peers disclosed by the focal firm in its proxy statement. A caveat of these comparisons is that peers can be defined from different vantage points. For example, LLM peers and TNIC peers are based on product markets, analyst-mentioning peers are based on equity markets, and compensation peers are based on labor markets. We observe overlap between LLM peers and peers identified by the alternative systems at rates substantially higher than chance, lending credence to LLM peer identification.

In our third analysis, we examine the stock return correlation between a focal firm and its LLM peers in the year subsequent to peer identification. The focal firm and its peers should share similar product demands, growth opportunities, economic

shocks, and technological constraints. Even though idiosyncratic factors may result in a focal firm and a peer firm to be positively or negatively correlated in performance, a peer portfolio is expected to be positively correlated with the focal firm because idiosyncratic factors cancel out within the portfolio. We estimate the coefficient on the LLM peer portfolio return, LLM Beta, for the time series of each focal firm-year using monthly returns. The mean value of the LLM Betas is 0.660, significantly positive, suggesting that, when the LLM peer portfolio return increases by 10%, the focal firm's return increases by 6.6%.

Over the past decade, TNIC has been considered a state-of-the-art peer identification system. For example, among all established peer identification systems, TNIC peers result in the most accurate peer-based valuation predictions (Geertsema and Lu 2023). We compare LLM Beta with TNIC Beta, which is the coefficient on the TNIC peer portfolio obtained using the top number of TNIC peers that is equal to the number of LLM peers. In the full sample, LLM Beta is significantly greater than TNIC Beta, suggesting that LLM peers are more relevant than TNIC peers. Among the 10 groups sorted by firm size, LLM Beta is significantly greater than TNIC Beta for the three largest firm-size groups. The outperformance for large firms is consistent with the idea that the machine exhibits strength for firms with a large amount of public data.

We also examine the correlations in accounting fundamentals between a focal firm and its peer group in the year after peer identification. We focus on sales growth and gross profit margin because they are arguably the two most important metrics for assessing product market performance (Li and Tang 2022; Ittner and Larcker 1998). For completeness, we also examine bottom-line profitability. We expect a peer portfolio to reflect the common factors in the product market and, therefore we expect positive correlations in product market performance between the LLM peer portfolio and the focal firm. That is indeed what we find. Moreover, the correlations in sales growth and gross profit margin are stronger under LLM peer identification than under TNIC peer identification.

In our final evaluation, we examine the homogeneity of stock performance and accounting fundamentals among LLM peers relative to the focal firm. Peers identified by a superior system should exhibit greater homogeneity among themselves. We calculate the absolute difference in monthly stock returns between a peer firm and its focal firm and then use the mean value across all peers as our deviation measure. Smaller deviations indicate greater homogeneity. For each firm-size group, LLM peers demonstrate more homogeneity in stock returns than do TNIC peers. We find similar patterns for the accounting fundamentals variables.

After evaluating LLM peers, we demonstrate their usefulness in two settings. First, we examine whether LLM peers are less biased in serving as compensation benchmarks than firm-selected peers and find this is indeed the case. Second, we mimic researchers who are interested in testing the theory that the delisting of peers following a merger or acquisition would worsen the focal firm's information environment (Brown et al. 2024). We find evidence of this relation when using LLM peers but do not find evidence when using TNIC peers.

Our study makes two contributions to the accounting literature. First, we evaluate the potential of a new technology in identifying peer firms. Our evidence suggests

that generative AI can execute this task. Our study joins the emerging body of research on the impact of generative AI in the capital markets (Bernard et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2023a, 2023b; Lopez-Lira and Tang 2023). We distinguish our work from the other studies by focusing on the role of generative AI as an information aggregator rather than as a tool to summarize or classify user-provided content.

Second, we propose a new approach to identify peer firms. The capital markets allocate capital across various classes of assets and, within equity assets, among multiple firms. Knowledge of peer firm identities is indispensable. Even though other systems can help investors identify peers, each has its limitations. For example, identifying peers as members in a focal firm's industry code might result in too many peers (e.g., there are 27 members in a four-digit SIC industry on average), not reflecting fast changes in the product market, and being ineffective for firms operating in multiple industries. The algorithm-driven approaches would require processing a large amount of data or relying on other researchers for timely data sharing. Our approach is highly convenient for investors, regulators, and researchers.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Peer firm identification

There are two approaches to identify peer firms (Zhao et al. 2025). Under the expert-driven approach, peers are identified as members in a focal firm's industry according to a classification system developed by experts. There are four popularly used expert-driven industry classification systems. The SIC was introduced by the U.S. government in 1937 to measure and report economic activity in a comparable way across agencies. Even though the system was last updated more than three decades ago, it is still widely used by governmental agencies, such as the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) debuted in 1997 to remedy the SIC's shortcomings but could not completely replace the SIC (Phillips and Ormsby 2016). The NAICS is updated every five years with its last update in 2022. Both the SIC and NAICS classify firms into the same industry if the firms use similar processes to produce goods or services. Fama–French industries are modifications of SIC industries. The Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS) was developed in 1999 by Standard & Poor's and MSCI for the investment community to track companies in global capital markets. The system is securities-rather than production-oriented (Phillips and Ormsby 2016).

The expert-driven approach offers benefits such as the use of expert knowledge and judgment and the taxonomy for several levels of granularity. This approach has three disadvantages, though. First, the systems produce many industry members, even at the most granular level, and, as a result, a peer group might be too large. Second, the systems might be stale, and stale information can harm investors in today's ever-changing business environment and market conditions. Last, the systems might not produce strong peers for firms that operate in multiple industries.

Under the algorithm-driven approach, researchers use algorithms to identify peers based on shared characteristics in the data. Ramnath (2002) proposes grouping firms based on analyst coverage: all firms in a group are covered by at least the same five financial analysts. His method applies to only a few firms. Bhojraj and Lee (2002) identify peers for valuation purposes by estimating the relation between several valuation multiples and valuation outcomes and then ranking firms based on the estimated relation. Geertsema and Lu (2023) follow a similar approach but use tree-based ensemble machine learning models to estimate the relation. Lee et al. (2015) identify peers based on investors' web traffic of downloading firms' financial reports from EDGAR and argue that investors tend to download peer firms' filings on the same day after downloading a focal firm's filings.

A popular algorithm-driven method in the past decade is TNIC. The idea originally appears in Hoberg and Phillips (2010), who compare the similarity of firms' business descriptions (Item 1 in 10-K filings) to identify peers for mergers and acquisitions and use the value of 0 (1) for the absence (presence) of a word. Hoberg and Phillips (2016) improve the method by using the frequency of each unique word in calculating document similarity. The authors argue that their peer identification is based on a firm's final products or services. $ ^{6} $

The algorithm-driven approach can generate peer identities that reflect recent changes of firms. The resulting peer list can reflect a peer’s relative closeness to the focal firm. However, these methods require intensive programming, and each method uses only one type of data source.

### 2.2 Generative AI

AI includes the subfields of machine learning, robotics, and symbolic logic (Agrawal et al. 2019). LLMs are deep learning language models that are pre-trained on a large amount of data. Deep learning is an advanced form of machine learning and uses neural networks to mimic how human brains process signals. The technology experienced a breakthrough in 2017 when transformers were introduced. The core of a transformer is the attention mechanism, which allows the machine to "look around" the text to both the left and the right—within and outside the sentence—and pay attention to relevant context clues. The multi-head attention block (e.g., position head, syntactic head, and rare-words head) can discover complex relationships in the text and determine "how each word contributes to the meaning of the input sequence" (Wang 2023). In this way, transformers can learn the contextual relations between words in a text and therefore understand the text "in a way that is similar to how humans analyze language" (Wang 2023). Transformers can even reason (Radford et al. 2018). A typical transformer may estimate hundreds of millions of

parameters. LLMs are typically built on transformers. The term “generative” means the ability to generate new, coherent, and human-like content beyond performing standard or predefined tasks (e.g., classification).

Generative LLMs can function as information aggregators because they are trained on massive datasets. For example, ChatGPT was trained on 300 billion words from archives of public webpage data, Wikipedia, books, articles, documents, etc.; Bard was trained on 1.56 trillion words from archives of public webpage data, Wikipedia, documents, and conversations and dialogues from the web (Sharma 2023; Lau 2023). The models' ability to "understand" text enables them to possess knowledge. Their ability to identify what textual clauses are relevant and important, to summarize text, and to reason enables the models to aggregate information. Transfer learning (i.e., the architecture of deep learning models that allows the knowledge gained from one batch of data to be applied to the next training step) allows knowledge to accumulate. Moreover, the models can draw data from various sources before aggregating them, even though the specific data sources and aggregation processes remain a black box to users (Roose 2024). Our study examines the efficacy of generative LLMs as an information aggregator.

## 3 LLM peers, sample collection, and descriptive statistics

### 3.1 LLM peers

We enter the following prompt in Bard to generate a focal firm’s peers:

A firm’s product market competitors refer to other companies that operate within the same market space, offer similar products or services, fulfill the same customer needs, and compete for market share. For the following tasks, please restrict your knowledge pool to the end of {year}. Among all publicly traded companies in the United States in the year of {year}, please list the major product market competitors of the firm, {cname}. The order of the list of major product market competitors should reflect product market closeness between the focal firm, {cname}, and its competitors, in a descending order.

The prompt defines product market competition, instructs the machine to identify the publicly listed U.S. firms that are peers of the focal firm (i) using the knowledge pool up to the end of a specific year (t), and list the peers in descending order with the closest peer at the top. Appendix 1: Table 11 provides examples of three focal firms from distinct industries with their LLM peers in 2022 generated by Bard. We hand-checked and confirmed the validity of these peers.

Both ChatGPT and Bard allow users to specify a cutoff date for the knowledge pool. For example, ChatGPT has a built-in system message (i.e., the message that provides instructions or context to the machine) for users to specify the knowledge pool cutoff date. The message reads: “You are ChatGPT, a large language model trained by OpenAI. Answer as concisely as possible. Knowledge cutoff: {knowledge_cutoff} Current date: {current_date}. It is advisable that users also specify the knowledge pool cutoff date in their user message (i.e., the message input by the

user). $ ^{7} $ Bard does not have a system message for users to specify the knowledge pool cutoff date; instead, users can specify cutoff in their prompt.

To gauge the effectiveness of the knowledge pool cutoff date, we use our main prompt in Bard to identify peers of Tesla from 2020 to 2022. We select Tesla because three competitors of Tesla—Rivian, Lucid, and Stallentis—went public in 2021 and one competitor—Polestar—went public in 2022. If Bard indeed uses the knowledge pool before the specified cutoff date, then the above competitors should not appear on the list of LLM peers until after their initial public offerings (IPO). Appendix A of the online appendix presents the screenshots of three separate inquiries with the knowledge pool cutoff dates of 2020, 2021, and 2022. The above four competitors indeed appear on the list of LLM peers only in the year of or after their IPOs, suggesting that specifying the knowledge pool cutoff date works as expected.

We check the stability of machine responses generated by repeated prompts over time. LLMs are probabilistic, and their responses to the same prompt in different trials might not be identical. Users can set a hyperparameter called “temperature” to 0 for a response to be most deterministic or to 1 for it to be very spontaneous. We randomly selected 500 focal firm-years and repeated the prompts 65 times over 16 days. Figure 1 shows that machine responses are very stable when the temperature is set to 0 and that stability drops substantially when the temperature is set to 0.5 or 1. We set the temperature to 0 when collecting LLM peers for our main analysis.

Wikipedia—a main source of training data for LLMs—was founded in January 2001 and reached 100,000 webpages in 2003. $ ^{13} $ We start the sample in 2003 to ensure that Wikipedia had been established at the beginning of our sample period. We exclude 2023 because of incomplete data at the time of sample collection. We exclude foreign firms, financial firms (SIC 6000–6999) and utility firms (SIC 4000–4999). Then we exclude focal firm-years that miss income before extraordinary items; do not have positive values of book value of equity or cost of goods sold; or have a stock price less than 3 or market capitalization less than 50 million at the end of the fiscal year or sales less than 10 million for the year. Our main sample has 37,867 focal firm-years with a total of 295,270 LLM peers. See Panel B of Table 1.

Panel A of Fig. 2 presents the histogram of the number of LLM peers per focal firm-year for our sample period. The most frequent peer firm counts are five, nine, and eight. Panel B shows the mean value of the number of LLM peers per focal firm for each year in our sample period, ranging from seven to eight with a generally upward trend. The trend is consistent with the idea that publicly available data have increased over time, allowing the machine to identify more peer firms. See Panel A of Table 2 for additional statistics. Panel C of Fig. 2 shows the mean value of the number of LLM peers per focal firm-year for each industry sector. The number is the highest for miners and lowest for retailers. See Panel B of Table 2 for additional statistics. Panel D of Fig. 2 shows the mean value of the number of LLM peers for each firm size group. To form size groups, we compare a firm-year's market capitalization at the end of the fiscal year with the market capitalization breakpoints for deciles of NYSE stocks at the same time. As a result, the smallest firm size group has disproportionately more members. The number of peers slightly declines for large firms perhaps because there are fewer large firms around to be peers. See Panel C of Table 2 for additional statistics.

We describe the extent to which the identities of a focal firm’s LLM peers change from one year (t) to the next (t+1). We calculate the continuing rate as the number of overlap peers between the two years divided by the number of peers in year t. Panel A of Fig. 3 plots this rate for each sample year (t) up to 2021. The measure ranges from 0.44 to 0.50 except for a notable drop in 2007—the onset of the financial crisis, which disrupted the economy and likely resulted in reshuffling of peers. $ ^{14} $

### 4.3 Return correlation between a focal firm and its LLM peers

After identifying peers in year t, we examine the return correlation between a focal firm and its LLM peers during year  $ t+1 $ by estimating Eq. (1) for each focal firm-year.

 $$ R e t_{i,t+1,m}=\alpha_{i,t}+\beta_{i,t}P o r t R e t_{i,t+1,m}+\varepsilon_{i,t+1,m,} $$ 

where  $ Ret_{i,t+1,m} $ is firm i's stock return in month m of calendar year  $ t+1 $ and  $ PortRet_{i,t+1,m} $ is a placeholder for the portfolio return of firm i's peers in the same year-month. Appendix 4: Table 12 provides all variable definitions. We equal-weight peers in calculating the peer portfolio return and use the robust-regression estimation method. This method uses iterative estimations and downweights influential observations in both the dependent and independent variables (Leone et al. 2019). Because of iterative estimations, the reported  $ R^2 $ is not meaningful. We focus on  $ \beta $ because it has an economic interpretation. $ ^{16} $

### 4.4 Accounting fundamentals correlation between a focal firm and its LLM peers

After identifying peers in year t, we examine the accounting fundamentals correlation between a focal firm and its LLM peers in year  $ t+1 $ by estimating Eq. (2) for each year. $ ^{20} $

 $$ A F P_{i,t+1}=\alpha_{t}+\beta_{t}P o r t A F P_{i,t+1}+\varepsilon_{i,t+1}, $$ 

where  $ AFP_{i,t+1} $ is firm i's accounting fundamentals performance for fiscal year  $ t+1 $ and  $ PortAFP_{i,t+1} $ is the equal-weighted peer firms' accounting fundamentals for fiscal year  $ t+1 $. AFP is a placeholder for sales growth (Sales Growth or SG), gross profit margin (GPM), and profitability (ROA). For a firm-year, sales growth is year  $ t+1 $'s sales minus year  $ t $'s sales, divided by year  $ t $'s sales; gross profit margin is year  $ t+1 $'s sales minus costs of goods sold, divided by sales; and profitability is year  $ t+1 $'s income before extraordinary items and discontinued operations divided by total assets. Accounting variables are notorious for having data errors. For focal firms' AFP, we truncate the variable at the first and 99th percentiles each year. For peer firms' AFP, we pool the portfolio values of LLM peers, TNIC peers, and SIC peers and truncate the pooled sample at the first and 99th percentiles each year. After these procedures, we have 22,971, 22,910, and 23,047 focal firm-years to estimate Eq. (2) when LLM peers are used and accounting fundamentals are sales growth, gross profit margin, and profitability, respectively.

Table 5 reports the robust-regression estimation results using the Fama–MacBeth (1973) approach. Panel A examines sales growth for year  $ t+1 $; LLM Beta is averaged at 0.263, significantly higher than TNIC Beta (0.192) and SIC Beta (0.020). Panel B examines gross profit margin for year  $ t+1 $; LLM Beta is averaged at 0.551, significantly higher than TNIC Beta (0.475) and SIC Beta (0.473). Panel C examines profitability for year  $ t+1 $; LLM Beta is averaged at 0.126, not significantly different from TNIC Beta (0.129) but significantly higher than SIC Beta (0.035). The subsequent three panels report the results for each firm size group. LLM Beta is significantly higher than TNIC Beta and SIC Beta in sales growth and gross profit margin correlations for each size group but not different from TNIC Beta in profitability correlations.

## 5 Additional analyses

### 5.1 Selected peers

Individuals often use heuristics in decision-making because information gathering and processing are costly (Simon 1959). A common heuristic is focusing on the Top N. For example, the top three athletes receive medals. We examine the performance of LLM peer identification if using only the top five, top three, top one, or bottom one peers from the list of LLM peers. Because this analysis does not require TNIC data, we can estimate Eq. (1) for 28,719 focal firm-years.

Panel A of Table 7 reports that LLM Beta is averaged at 0.693 when all peers are included, 0.664 with the top five peers, 0.614 with the top three peers, 0.444 with the top one peer, and 0.431 with the bottom one peer. Beta gradually declines as fewer peers are used, consistent with the idea that a larger portfolio better reflects common factors. The ranking in the LLM peer list also matters, as demonstrated by the higher beta from the top one peer than from the bottom one peer.

### 5.2 Simple prompt

We now investigate how sensitive our results are to the construction of the prompt. We simplify the original prompt to the following:

For the following tasks, please restrict your knowledge pool to the end of {year}. Among all publicly traded companies in the United States in the year of {year}, please list all major product market competitors of the firm, {cname}. This prompt does not define product market competitors and does not require an ordered list.

With the simple prompt, we use similar sample collection procedures as described in Table 1. We require a focal firm-year to have the original LLM peers, simple-prompt peers, and TNIC peers. $ ^{22} $ Using this reduced sample, we can estimate Eq. (1) for 17,792 focal firm-years. Panel B of Table 7 reports the return correlations between a focal firm and its peers. LLM Beta is averaged at 0.680 when the original prompt is used and is 0.668 when the simple prompt is used—the latter performance is significantly lower. The two LLM Betas are significantly higher than TNIC Beta (0.619) and SIC Beta (0.623).

### 5.3 Types of peers

There are three types of peers among the top s peers on the LLM and TNIC lists, where s is the smaller number of LLM peers or TNIC peers: (A) peers that appear on both lists (overlap peers), (B) peers that appear only on the LLM list (LLM-only peers), and (C) peers that appear only on the TNIC list (TNIC-only peers). In Panel A of Table 8, we examine the three types of peers separately without requiring a focal firm-year to have all three types. Our 20,820 focal firm-year betas in Table 4 now shrink to 12,330 (59.2%), 17,025 (81.8%), and 19,960 (95.9%) for A, B, and C types, respectively. The portfolio returns of all three types of peers are positively correlated with the focal firm's returns during year  $ t+1 $.

Panel B requires a focal firm-year to have all three types of peers. The beta for the overlap-peer portfolio is averaged at 0.633, not significantly different from that for the LLM-only peer portfolio (0.629). The latter is significantly higher than the beta for the TNIC-only peer portfolio (0.577), reflecting the benefit of the LLM approach in using various data sources.

### 5.4 Usefulness of LLM peers in compensation benchmarking

The peers that a firm selects as compensation benchmarks for its CEO pay tend to have higher CEO pay (Faulkender and Yang 2010; Bizjak et al. 2011). This strategic selection results in a peer-selection pay bias. We examine whether LLM peers result in less bias. To do so, we first replicate the peer-choice probit model, which was originally used by Faulkender and Yang (2010) and later replicated by Albuquerque et al. (2013). The objective is to identify an unobserved true peer (i.e., the firm in the same sample year with the closest propensity score) for each firm-selected peer. We then calculate a firm's peer-selection pay bias (LLM bias) as the difference between the median CEO pay of its selected peers (LLM peers) and the median CEO pay of its true peers, scaled by the latter.

From ISS Incentive Lab for the sample period of 2006–2021, we collect 19,831 focal firm-years that provide a list of compensation peers, and we also collect the listed peer firms. After matching with Compustat and collecting the firm

characteristic variables, we have 14,103 focal firms-years. The probit model uses focal-firm/peer firm pairs, where a peer candidate can be any firm listed as a peer by any focal firm in the sample year or be any other focal firm in that year. For example, in 2006, we have 863 focal firms and 1,230 other firms that are listed as peer firms; therefore each focal firm is paired with 2,092 other firms (i.e., 1230 +863−1). Panel A of Table 9 reports the probit estimation using 19,032,429 firm pairs from 23,236 unique firms. $ ^{23} $ The results resemble those of Faulkender and Yang (2010) and Albuquerque et al. (2013).

After requiring CEO compensation data from ExecuComp for both focal and peer firms, we have 8,438 focal firm-years to calculate the LLM bias versus firm peer-selection pay bias. Panel B reports that for the full sample, the LLM bias is 10.8% of the median pay of true peers, significantly smaller than the firm bias of 13.3%. In addition, the LLM bias is significantly smaller than the firm bias for all firm-size deciles, except for the three largest deciles. Bizjak et al. (2011) report that smaller firms exhibit larger bias in compensation peer selection. Thus, our evidence suggests that LLM peers can be useful for investors in evaluating CEO pay. $ ^{24} $

## 6 Conclusion

Our study is motivated by the challenges that users face in becoming aware of data and acquiring and integrating data in the capital markets. Generative AI can assist users because it can access, aggregate, and analyze a large amount of public data from various sources and it is freely available to anyone with an internet connection and a computer or smartphone. We investigate whether the machine can identify peers for a focal firm.

We evaluate the performance of LLM peers in a series of analyses and find that LLM peers perform reasonably well and often outperform TNIC peers, especially for large firms. Our findings suggest that, as an information aggregator, generative AI can be a useful tool for investors, regulators, and researchers in identifying peer firms. We provide a guide for best practices in Appendix C of the online appendix.

The breakthrough of generative AI in undertaking human tasks occurred just about two years ago. Competition to improve the technology is fierce. Our findings might represent the lower bound of the effectiveness of the technology. Moreover, the technology is flexible. For example, users can further their understanding of a focal firm's peer firms by asking the machine to elaborate on its peer selection. $ ^{26} $ Users can also customize their prompts (e.g., specifically asking for compensation peers). However, the technology is still new. Users should exercise human oversight in using the technology as a tool.
