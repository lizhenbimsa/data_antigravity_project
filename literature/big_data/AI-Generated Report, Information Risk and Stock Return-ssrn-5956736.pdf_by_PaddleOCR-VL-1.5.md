# AI-generated Report, Information Risk and Stock Return

Yunchuan Sun $ ^{*} $, Business School, Beijing Normal University, Beijing, 100875, China.

Ying Xu, Business School, Beijing Normal University, Beijing, 100875, China.

Abstract: The rise of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) has fundamentally changed the way how firms finish their financial reporting tasks. Using a sample of Chinese A-share listed firms from 2021 to 2025, this paper employs event study to explore whether AI-generated texts in MD&A disclosures shapes information risk and investor responses. We measure the likelihood of AI-generated content (LAiGen) in MD&A narratives via text perplexity based on large language models. It is found that lower LAiGen in MD&A is positively associated with short-window stock returns around annual report releases. This effect is more pronounced for firms with heightened uncertainty, such as negative unexpected earnings, lower proportions of quantitative disclosure, lower textual burstiness, or weaker external monitoring. Further analyses suggest that AI-generated narratives reduce the firm-specific informational content of disclosures, increase reliance on generic industry- and market-level language, and widen bid-ask spreads, thereby elevating information asymmetry. We address endogeneity concerns using an instrumental variable approach and exploit the exogenous shock of the public release of ChatGPT in a difference-in-differences design, which confirms that reduced AI-adoption costs lead to more widely use of AI-assisted disclosure, particularly among non-AI-industry firms. Our findings highlight the potential unintended consequences of generative AI in firm reporting: while AI may enhance linguistic polish, its use could impair disclosure informativeness, elevate information risk, and weaken market efficiency. The results carry implications for regulators, investors, and firms navigating the evolving landscape of AI-integrated financial communication.

## 1. Introduction

Corporate narrative disclosure extends well beyond financial statements. Within financial reports, Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) section is particularly critical, which can convey soft information that is not easily captured by financial statements alone and has the potential to substantially shape investors' beliefs and expectations (Li, 2008; Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Bochkay and Levine, 2019; Cheng et al., 2025). A large body of empirical research has examined the impacts of MD&A content on capital markets from various perspectives, such as tone and post earnings announcement drift (Feldman et al., 2010), incremental information and analyst forecasts (Bozzolan et al., 2009; Mayew et al., 2014), uncertainty perception and default prediction (Kong et al., 2025), forward-looking disclosure and M&A outcomes (Chen et al., 2025), etc.

Nowadays, large language models, such as GPT series, are widely used in reporting tasks because of their capability to generate fluent, consistent, and readable content at lower costs (Eloundou et al. 2024). It is documented that firms have indeed incorporated GAI into the preparation of corporate disclosures, including MD&A sections, which also highlights that AI-generated contents exhibit systematically different linguistic properties (Blankespoor et al., 2025). The potential implications are substantial: AI-generated narratives may improve linguistic clarity and coherence, but may also

facilitate strategic obfuscation or dilute the informational content of managerial communication. Yet, little is known about how the adoption of AI-generated content impacts information environment faced by investors. We address this gap by exploring whether the likelihood of AI-generated content (LAIGen) in MD&A is associated with information risk and subsequent stock-market responses.

Our premise is that AI-generated MD&A narratives embed algorithmic linguistic structures into corporate disclosure in ways that systematically alter textual properties. It is shown that financial reports with greater reliance on GAI exhibit lower readability and more positive managerial tone, suggesting that AI-assisted drafting may homogenize narrative style, soften negative information, or obscure firm-specific details (Blankespoor et al., 2025). If AI-generated text reduces specificity or amplifies optimistic framing, it may weaken the link between underlying fundamentals and disclosed information, thereby increasing opacity and information asymmetry (Diamond and Verrecchia, 1991; Bushee et al., 2018). However, AI assistance could also improve structural coherence and reduce mechanical errors, potentially enhancing informativeness. Whether these competing forces lead AI-generated MD&A content to raise or lower information risk therefore remains an important empirical issue.

In this study, the baseline sample comprises 20,486 firm-year observations for Chinese A-share listed firms, over the annual report from 2020 to 2024. For each firm-year observation, we compute MD&A text perplexity based on large language models, where higher perplexity indicates lower probability of AI generation. It is shown that short-window cumulative abnormal returns around announcement are significantly more positive when firms exhibit lower LAIGen. Moreover, we find no evidence of long-term return reversals, indicating that the market views AI-generated MD&A content as the increase of information risk, rather than timing or sentiment effects. These results hold when we account for additional firm-level and text-feature controls, firm-year fixed effects, and change short-term event windows.

As the interpretation, we consider that MD&A with higher LAIGen would decrease the capital market's information efficiency and undermine investor beliefs. Consistent with our expectations, it is found that firms with a higher estimated probability of AI-generated MD&A increases reliance on information related to industry and market, and exhibits more severe information asymmetry reflected by wider bid-ask spreads. Next, we investigate how the association between MD&A AI-generation likelihood and short-window market reactions varies across firms operating under different information environments. We selected several scenarios, including negative unexpected earnings, less quantitative information disclosures, and weaker external monitor environment, where investors face greater uncertainty about the interpretation of reported performance (Feldman et al., 2010; Jang et al., 2022; Campbell et al., 2025). In such settings, we find that the negative relationship between LAIGen in MD&A and market returns is more pronounced. It can be inferred that MD&A sections with less AI involvement appear to improve the informativeness of disclosures while higher information risk.

Besides, we use the public release of ChatGPT as an exogenous shock and employ a difference-in-differences design, which demonstrate the prominence of AI-generative tendency increases for non-AI-industry firms. To address potential endogeneity in disclosure choices, we exploit an instrumental-variable (IV) approach based on the industry-average LAIGen in the last year, which captures variation in disclosure at the industry level independent of firm fundamentals. The IV estimates closely mirror the baseline results. Furthermore, we re-compute LAIGen based on the LLaMA-3 model, which provides a substantially different generative architecture. The main effect

persists under the alternative construction of the AI-generation measure.

Our paper contributes to the literature on corporate disclosure, information risk, and capital-market consequences of AI technologies. First, we add to research on narrative disclosure and the informativeness of MD&A reporting. Prior works show that textual attributes such as readability, tone, and linguistic complexity meaningfully shape investors' beliefs and the quality of the information environment (Li, 2008; Feldman et al., 2010; Bushee et al., 2018; Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Aghamolla and Smith, 2023). Recent evidence further suggests that generative AI may alter the linguistic structure of corporate narratives by reducing readability and increasing positive tone (Blankespoor et al., 2025). Yet few studies examine how investors interpret disclosures that differ in their likelihood of being human-versus AI-authored. We extend this literature by documenting that MD&A sections with lower LAIGen are associated with lower information risk and more favorable market reactions, particularly in settings where narrative explanations play a central role in shaping investor beliefs.

Second, our study contributes to the emerging literature on the relationship between GAI and capital markets. While researchers primarily focus on how firms use AI for prediction, internal controls, or automation of reporting tasks, little is known about how GAI affects stock returns. Our findings indicate that AI-generated MD&A narratives weaken the transmission of firm-specific soft information. The results are consistent with impression-management theory, which emphasizes managers' strategic use of narrative discretion to contextualize performance, especially when fundamentals are unfavorable (Merkl-Davies and Brennan, 2007; Leung et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2024). By showing that higher AI involvement is associated with wider bid-ask spreads, we provide direct evidence that GAI can alter the mechanism through which narrative reporting communicates managerial private information.

Finally, our study provides insights relevant to the realistic discussion on the use of GAI. As regulators and market participants evaluate the implications of integrating AI tools into disclosure, understanding how AI-generated narratives affect capital markets is increasingly important. Our findings imply that the adoption of GAI tools in financial reporting may require monitoring frameworks that preserve disclosure informativeness and protect investors' rights and interests. More broadly, our analysis highlights the value of transparency regarding the use of AI in narrative reporting and provides empirical grounding for regulatory initiatives aimed at maintaining the integrity of firm communication under rapidly advancing AI capabilities.

Section 3 describes the sample selection, variable construction, and baseline results. Section 4 presents mechanism discussions. Section 5 examines endogeneity concerns and robustness tests, and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Hypothesis development

With the development of textual processing and analysis technology, finance and accounting researchers have widely recognized and identified the importance of firm narrative disclosure in shaping the information environment faced by investors and regulators (Lambert et al., 2007; Li, 2008; Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Liberti and Petersen, 2019; Garcia and Rohrer, 2023). The narrative disclosures can convey soft information that cannot be fully captured by quantitative metrics. Much of this work focuses on how textual features influence market participants' ability to process information (Bonsall and Miller, 2017; Merkley, 2014; Campbell et al., 2020). For example,

it is shown that firms with poorer performance tend to issue less readable financial reports, which investors interpret as a signal of lower-quality information (Li, 2008). Subsequent research builds on this insight by examining tone and sentiment (Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Garcia et al., 2023), the complexity of narrative structure (Bushee et al., 2018), and other text-based indicators of disclosure opacity.

MD&A reporting is particularly important in this regard. It summarizes managerial assessments of operating risks, strategic priorities, and future performance prospects, all of which are subjective, forward-looking, and difficult to verify (Muslu et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2024). As voluntary disclosure content, managers are inclined to strategic disclosure in MD&A out of opportunistic motivations, thereby influencing market reactions (Brown and Tucker, 2011; Davis and Tama-Sweet, 2012; Lo et al., 2017). Many studies show that narrative disclosures contain value-relevant information and significantly affect investors' beliefs and trading behavior (Li, 2008; Feldman et al., 2010; Loughran and McDonald, 2011). Because managers have substantial discretion in framing, emphasizing, or obfuscating information through textual choices, MD&A sections are also central to the literature on disclosure opacity and information risk (Brown and Tucker, 2011; Bushee et al., 2018).

The rapid adoption of GAI has begun to alter how MD&A disclosures are produced. Recent large-sample evidence shows that disclosures containing more AI-generated language are less readable, longer, and more positive in tone (Blankespoor et al., 2025). These linguistic features can reduce the informational richness of disclosure and increase processing costs for investors. In addition to lowering information content, AI-generated language may raise concerns about firms' bad news withholding. According to impression-management theory, it is suggested that managers could rely on narrative discretion to frame unfavorable outcomes, highlight strategic rationale, and mitigate investor concerns when fundamentals are uncertain (Merkl-Davies and Brennan, 2007; Leung et al., 2015). More uniformly positive tone and templated narrative structures can lead investors to suspect that managers are using AI tools to frame or soften unfavorable information rather than to convey substantive insights. When disclosure appears overly polished, investors may discount the credibility of the narrative and infer higher information risk.

Taken together, these arguments suggest that MD&A sections exhibiting lower likelihood of AI generation that captured by higher perplexity should contain more firm-specific soft information, reduce information risk, and elicit stronger market reactions around annual report releases. Accordingly, our testable hypothesis is as follows:

MD&A with lower AI generation probability—characterized by higher perplexity—is positively associated with short-window stock returns around annual report disclosures.

## 3. Research design and baseline results

### 3.1 Data and sample selection

As previously discussed, we focus on the MD&A section because it provides managers' narrative explanations of past outcomes and strategic outlooks, and therefore embeds soft information that is not observable from financial statements alone. The MD&A is also less constrained by line-item reporting rules, making it a suitable setting to examine how the likelihood of AI-generated text affects the informativeness of disclosure and subsequent market reactions.

We obtain annual report texts from CNINFO during fiscal year 2020 to 2024 and use keyword

anchors and manual verification to extract the MD&A sections. We then compute text perplexity index using a GPT-2 language model used for the likelihood that the narrative resembles AI-generated content. We gather stock return data and financial statement information from CSMAR. Following prior literature, we drop firms in the financial industries and labeled with ST or PT. To mitigate the possible effects of outliers, we winsorize the continuous variables at the top and bottom 1% of their distributions. Finally, we obtain a sample that consists of 20,486 firm-year observations of 4,730 unique firms.

### 3.2 Measuring AI-generation likelihood

To quantify the textual complexity and generative characteristics of MD&A narratives, we compute a perplexity (PPL) index using a sliding-window language-modeling framework based on GPT-2. Perplexity assesses the inverse likelihood of the observed text under a pretrained language model and increases when the text exhibits lower predictability or more irregular linguistic patterns. It is widely used in computational linguistics as the primary statistical indicator for detecting AI-generated text. Contemporary detection tools, like GPTZero, rely fundamentally on perplexity to identify content generated by GPT series models, since generative models typically produce texts with higher predictability and more homogeneous token-transition structures. Accordingly, lower perplexity signals more templated and stylized language patterns that resemble algorithmically generated narratives, whereas higher perplexity reflects more irregular and human-specific linguistic variation (Bertomeu et al., 2025).

Here, we choose GPT-2 to calculate PPL for following reasons. First, GPT-2 is publicly available, lightweight, and well documented, which makes our procedure transparent and easily reproducible for other researchers. Second, although newer large language models (e.g., GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and beyond) differ in scale and training data, they retain the same autoregressive token-prediction mechanism that the model generates text by assigning conditional probabilities to the next token given the preceding context.

To implement this measure for Chinese MD&A, we first tokenize each MD&A document using the GPT-2 Byte-Pair Encoding tokenizer and evaluate the negative log-likelihood of the text using the GPT-2 language model. Because the maximum input length of the model is limited by 1,024 tokens, we adopt an overlapping sliding-window approach. For each window w, we compute the token-level negative log-likelihood and aggregate it across windows to obtain an overall perplexity score for the document:

 $$ PPL=\exp\big(\frac{\sum_{w}NLL_{w}}{Total Tokens}\big). $$ 

The sliding-window structure is purely a computational device to evaluate long sequences without truncation. After aggregation, the resulting PPL reflects the overall predictability of the narrative rather than that of individual windows. This design preserves transitional information across adjacent windows and yields a consistent, interpretable measure of how predictable the MD&A text appears from the perspective of a pretrained generative model, which we use as our empirical proxy for AI-generation likelihood.

### 3.3 Model specification

To test the hypothesis that MD&A sections with lower AI-generation likelihood elicit stronger market reactions, we estimate the following baseline regression model:

 $$ \begin{aligned}CAR_{i,[t_{1},t_{2}]}=\alpha+\beta PPL_{i,t}+\gamma_{1}Size_{i,t}+\gamma_{2}BM_{i,t}+\gamma_{3}EarningsFore_{i,t}+\gamma_{4}Lev_{i,t}+\\ \gamma_{5}Readability_{i,t}+\delta_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t},\quad(1)\end{aligned} $$ 

where  $ CAR_{i,[t_1,t_2]} $ is the cumulative abnormal return for firm i in time windows from day  $ t_1 $ to  $ t_2 $ around the annual reporting dates, including periods of  $ [-1, 1] $,  $ [0, 1] $,  $ [0, 2] $,  $ [0, 3] $, and  $ [0, 5] $. The abnormal return is computed using a market-model estimation window prior to the disclosure date (Ball and Brown, 1968; MacKinlay et al., 1997), where the betas are estimated using the daily returns in the interval of  $ [-130, -11] $ relative to the date of the annual reports. We standardize all the continuous independent variables. The variable of interest,  $ PPL_{i,t} $, is the MD&A perplexity calculated using a GPT-2 language model; higher  $ PPL_{i,t} $ indicates lower similarity to AI-generated text and therefore lower AI-generation likelihood.

We include a basket of firm-level control variables, such as firm size ( $ Size_{i,t} $), market-to-book ratio ( $ BM_{i,t} $), debt-to-asset ratio ( $ Lev_{i,t} $), a dummy variable that records the earnings forecast situation ( $ EarningsFore_{i,t} $), and MD&A content readability ( $ Readability_{i,t} $). To control for unobserved firm characteristics and year-specific economic conditions, we include firm and year fixed effects in all specifications. Standard errors are heterogeneity-robust and clustered at the industry level to account for within-industry correlation over time. ## 4. Mechanism tests

### 4.1 Long-horizon market responses

To complement the short-window analyses, we examine whether the valuation effects associated with MD&A perplexity persist over longer horizons. Specifically, we re-estimate our baseline model using cumulative abnormal returns measured over extended event windows, including  $ CAR_{[0,10]} $,

CAR_{[0,15]}, CAR_{[0,20]}, CAR_{[0,25]}, and CAR_{[0,30]}. Across all specifications, the coefficients on PPL remain positive but lose statistical significance once the window extends beyond the closed disclosure period (Appendix Table A9). It is proved that the market response to variation in AI-generation likelihood is primarily a short-horizon phenomenon, consistent with the role of MD&A narratives in shaping investors' initial interpretation of the annual report.

The absence of long-run return drift further indicates that the short-run positive reaction to higher PPL does not reflect systematic mispricing that later reverses. Instead, the evidence is consistent with a story in which perplexity captures disclosure features that reduce perceived information risk at the time of release, but do not affect investors' longer-term assessment of firm value.

### 4.2 AI-generation MD&A and informational content

An important issue is whether MD&A sections that resemble AI-generated text actually convey less information. This also helps explain why firms whose MD&A exhibits higher perplexity face higher information risk. To explore this channel, we examine the extent to which LLM may have assisted firms in producing MD&A narratives that rely more heavily on industry- or market-level linguistic patterns. Such standardized information is generally easier for investors to process but less informative for valuation and forecast (Peng and Xiong, 2006; Bertomeu et al., 2025).

Following the vector-based textual analysis framework used in prior MD&A studies (Hanley and Hoberg, 2010), we quantify the degree to which a firm's MD&A narrative conforms to industry-and market-level linguistic patterns. For each firm-year, we first convert the MD&A text into a normalized term-frequency vector,  $ Norm_{i,t} $, using a bag-of-words representation with Chinese word segmentation and stopword removal $ ^{1} $. Similarly, we compute two benchmark vectors: an industry-level vector  $ Norm_{Ind,t} $, defined as the average normalized term-frequency vector of all other firms in the same industry in year t, and a market-level vector  $ Norm_{Mkt,t} $, defined as the average vector of all firms outside the focal firm's industry.

To isolate the portion of a firm’s linguistic content that can be explained by these broader language patterns, we run the following cross-sectional regression for each firm-year using the vocabulary dimension as the observational unit

 $$ N o r m_{i,t}(k)=\alpha_{0}+\beta_{I n d}N o r m_{I n d,t}(k)+\beta_{M k t}N o r m_{M k t,t}(k)+\mu_{i,t}(k), $$ 

where k indexes words in the dictionary. The coefficients  $ \beta_{Ind} $ and  $ \beta_{Mkt} $ capture the extent to which the firm’s narrative style aligns with the prevailing linguistic structure of its industry and the overall market, respectively.

We define standard information measure as the sum of these two components:

 $$ S t d I n f o_{i,t}=\beta_{I n d}+\beta_{M k t}. $$ 

A higher value of this metric indicates that the firm's MD&A language closely mirrors common industry- or market-level discourse, implying a more template-like disclosure style. Conversely, a lower value suggests weaker conformity to these broader linguistic norms and a greater reliance on firm-specific narrative content. Columns (1) and (2) in Table 3 presents that PPL loads negatively and significantly on standard information, consistent with the information-risk interpretation of our main findings. AI-generated linguistic patterns diminish the amount of economically relevant information embedded in MD&A disclosures, while providing substantially more industry- and

market-related information. According to column (2), it is implied that a one-standard-deviation increase in PPL is associated with roughly a 6% improvement in standardized information content. This pattern suggests that narratives produced with more AI assistance contain poorer firm-specific detail and contribute less to investors' understanding of the firm's economic environment.

### 4.3 Cross-sectional heterogeneity

#### 4.3.1 Earnings announcements

It is considered that managers have stronger incentives to engage in impression management when fundamental performance deteriorates, often emphasizing favorable language or strategic framing to soften the market impact of bad news (Leung et al., 2015). Thus, we premise that the association between MD&A perplexity and market reactions should be more pronounced when unexpected earnings are negative.

To investigate this prediction, we partition the sample by the sign of the unexpected earnings (UE) and re-estimate the baseline regression within each subsample. Table 4 presents the results. Consistent with the impression-management channel, PPL exhibits no significant relation with  $ CAR_{[-1,1]} $ in the positive-UE subsample, whereas the coefficient becomes large and statistically significant in the negative-UE subsample. As shown in column (2), for firms reporting negative unexpected earnings, the coefficient on PPL is 0.506 (T-statistics=2.50), and the estimate remains economically meaningful when additional controls are included. These magnitudes imply that the market responds substantially more positively when MD&A narratives are less AI-like precisely in periods when firms have incentives to manage impressions.

This asymmetric pattern reinforces the idea that AI-generated disclosures substitute for manager-written narrative in environments where managers face greater pressure to justify poor performance. Under bad-news conditions, more AI-like MD&A appears to dilute firm-specific content and reduce disclosure credibility, resulting in weaker investor reactions. By contrast, when fundamentals are strong, impression-management incentives are attenuated, and the market reaction to MD&A perplexity largely disappears. Overall, the results support the inference that the effects of AI-generated narrative are amplified in settings where impression management is more likely to arise.

#### 4.3.2 Disclosure characteristics

A large body of research shows that the structure and composition of narrative reports shape investors' information processing, resulting in information risk. Quantitative content is typically easier to verify and less open to managerial discretion than qualitative language, and it is found that numerical elements anchor investors' interpretation of narrative disclosures and reduce ambiguity, resulting in lower information risk (Biddle et al., 2023). While linguistic burstiness increases processing costs and can blur the distinction between informative and boilerplate text (Bertomeu et al., 2025). Motivated by these insights, we examine whether the market's response to MD&A perplexity varies systematically across disclosure environments characterized by: (i) the proportion of numerical content and (ii) the burstiness of textual expression.

Firstly, following Campbell et al. (2025), we extracted the number of words and numbers and calculated the proportion of numerical information as group-variable NumR. We then sort firms based on the median of NumR in the MD&A. Panel A of Table 5 shows that the positive association between PPL and CAR is concentrated in the low- NumR subsample. In these observations, where MD&A contains relatively little numerical content, higher PPL is associated with significantly stronger short-window abnormal returns. This pattern is consistent with the view that, when disclosure is largely qualitative and information risk is high, more human-like and less AI-generated MD&A helps mitigate that risk and is rewarded by the market. By contrast, in the higher subsample, where numerical disclosure already provides a substantial amount of hard information, the incremental risk-mitigating role of narrative style is much weaker.

We then consider textual burstiness, calculated as the Coefficient of Variation of PPL, which captures the suddenness within the MD&A. Lower burstiness implies a more stable and coherent narrative structure, which is regarded as higher AI-generation likelihood (Bertomeu et al., 2025).

We therefore expect information risk to be higher in the low-burstiness group.

#### 4.3.3 External monitor

Here, we explore the impacts of MD&A perplexity on market reactions under different external monitor environments. Institutional investors and financial analysts routinely evaluate managerial narratives, challenge overly stylized language, and help interpret firm fundamentals for the broader market. When these monitors are active, investors have access to richer, independently processed information, thereby alleviating information asymmetry and reducing the market's dependence on the linguistic cues embedded in the MD&A. In contrast, when monitoring is weak, investors rely more directly on the narrative's linguistic signals to infer credibility and information quality. Consequently, we infer the risk-mitigating effect of higher PPL should be particularly salient in low-monitoring environments.

Columns (1) to (4) in Table 6 partition the sample based on institutional ownership. We find that there is a significantly positive association between PPL and CAR in the lower subsample, while the coefficient becomes small and statistically insignificant in the higher group. This suggests that institutions serve as an effective external monitor that partially substitutes for the informational role of narrative predictability. When institutional oversight is limited, however, disclosures that are less AI-like (higher PPL) help reduce perceived information risk and are rewarded with stronger short-window market reactions.

Columns (5) to (8) report results using analyst following number as the monitoring channel. Consistent with the findings for institutional ownership, the positive association is substantially stronger in the low-AF subsample. In firms with limited analyst coverage, public investors receive fewer intermediary signals, face higher processing costs, and are more exposed to potential opacity in MD&A disclosures. Consequently, the extent to which the narrative resembles genuine managerial communication has greater value in mitigating these frictions. By contrast, in firms with extensive analyst following, professional intermediaries already provide substantial verification and

interpretation of disclosure content, reducing the incremental informational role of MD&A perplexity. In conclusion, the results in Table 6 show that the risk-mitigating effect of higher PPL is amplified when external monitoring is weak. This evidence further supports the interpretation that AI-generated MD&A increases information risk, and that more predictable, less AI-generated narratives are particularly beneficial in environments where external monitors are less able to discipline or supplement managerial communication.

### 5.1 Exogenous shock to AI adoption: Evidence from the launch of ChatGPT

The public release of ChatGPT in late 2022 represents a discrete and externally imposed shock that substantially lowered the cost of accessing and applying large-language-model technology. Prior literature documents that reductions in information-processing costs or technological frictions

often trigger rapid adoption of new communication technologies, especially among firms that rely heavily on narrative disclosure or face internal resource constraints (Blankespoor et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021). Consistent with this view, the introduction of ChatGPT created a setting in which firms not previously using AI-assisted writing tools (“non-AI firms”) could suddenly and inexpensively incorporate algorithmic assistance into MD&A preparation.

To the extent that generative AI encourages templated, stylized, or less idiosyncratic narrative construction, we such firms to experience a decline in perplexity (i.e., higher AI-generation likelihood) after ChatGPT became available. Moreover, this effect would be stronger among firms that historically exhibited low AI-generation intensity.

Thus, we estimate the DID specification as follows:

 $$ \begin{aligned}PPL_{i,t}&=\alpha+\beta(Treat_{i}\times Post_{t})+\gamma_{1}Size_{i,t}+\gamma_{2}BM_{i,t}+\gamma_{3}EarningsFore_{i,t}+\gamma_{4}Lev_{i,t}\\&\quad+\gamma_{5}Readability_{i,t}+\delta_{i}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t},\quad(2)\end{aligned} $$ 

where the interaction term  $ Treat_{i} \times Post_{t} $ captures whether non-AI firms (the treatment group) exhibit differential changes in perplexity following the public introduction of ChatGPT.  $ Treat_{i} $ is an indicator equal to 1 for firms operating in non-AI industries, and  $ Post_{t} $ equals 1 for fiscal years after the ChatGPT release (after 2022).

Table 7 presents the difference-in-differences estimates evaluating how the release of ChatGPT affected firms' MD&A perplexity. Across both specifications, the coefficient on the interaction term is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level. This estimate indicates that, relative to AI-industry firms, non-AI firms exhibit a pronounced decline in MD&A perplexity following the public introduction of ChatGPT. Given that perplexity inversely reflects the likelihood of AI-generated disclosure, the result suggests that the reduction in adoption costs brought about by ChatGPT led treated firms to more heavily rely on generative-AI tools when drafting their MD&A narratives. The effect remains stable after including firm-level controls, consistent with AI adoption representing a broad reporting adjustment.

### 5.2 Addressing endogeneity

A remaining concern is whether the relation between MD&A perplexity and market reactions is partly shaped by firms' endogenous disclosure choices. Managers anticipating a muted market response may resort more heavily to AI-assisted drafting, producing lower PPL, while firms expecting favorable reactions may put greater effort into composing richer, more human-generated narratives. Such reverse causality would make PPL reflect managers' expectations about returns rather than the informational value of the disclosure itself. In addition, unobservable firm traits, such as disclosure culture, reporting capability, or governance quality, may simultaneously influence both MD&A style and how investors interpret the report.

To mitigate the endogeneity concerns, we follow the diffusion-based disclosure literature and construct an instrument that captures industry-wide narrative tendencies unrelated to the focal firm's market reaction. Specifically, for each firm-year we use the lagged average PPL of other firms in the same industry (a leave-one-out measure) as an instrument for the firm's own perplexity. The idea is straightforward: MD&A drafting practices often evolve at the industry level, so the disclosure style of peers in the prior year is a strong predictor of a firm's current narrative approach. At the same time, once we control for firm and year fixed effects, last year's peer disclosure style should have no direct bearing on the short-window return reaction to firm i's own annual report.

Table 8, the first stage shows a strong and positive association between peer-industry PPL and the firm's PPL, with F-statistics well above weak-instrument thresholds (Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic = 201.93). In the second stage, the coefficient on PPL remains positive and statistically significant. The IV results therefore reinforce the view that the market rewards less AI-generated, more human-like MD&A narratives because they carry greater informational value, rather than because firms strategically choose such narratives in anticipation of the market reaction.

### 5.3 Robustness tests

We conduct a series of robustness tests to verify that our main findings are not sensitive to the choice of event window, the construction of the perplexity measure, or model specification. Across all exercises, the core result that higher MD&A perplexity is associated with more positive short-

window market reactions remains quantitatively and statistically robust.

First, we examine alternative event windows surrounding the annual report disclosure. Using  $ CAR_{[-2,2]} $ and  $ CAR_{[-3,3]} $ yields coefficient estimates on PPL that are similar in magnitude to those in the baseline specification. The persistence of the effect across wider windows suggests that the relation between MD&A perplexity and market reactions is not driven by narrow timing choices or microstructure-induced return patterns.

Second, we verify that our results are not tied to the specific language model used to compute perplexity. We recompute the AI-generation likelihood measure using the recently released LLaMA-3 foundation model, which differs from GPT-2 in size, architecture, and tokenization. Despite these differences, the estimated coefficients using the LLaMA-3-based perplexity measure closely mirror the baseline results. This alignment across models reinforces the interpretation that perplexity captures systematic variation in AI-generated narrative features rather than artifacts of a particular model or training corpus.

Finally, we re-estimate all baseline specifications with the inclusion of industry fixed effects to absorb time-invariant industry-level disclosure practices, reporting norms, and AI-technology spillovers that might correlate with both MD&A style and market reactions. The estimated coefficients on PPL remain stable and significant after adding these controls, indicating that our results are not driven by cross-industry composition effects or omitted sector characteristics.

## 6. Conclusion

This study investigates whether the increasing prevalence of AI-generated linguistic structures in MD&A disclosures alters the information environment faced by investors. Motivated by emerging evidence that generative AI tools can reshape narrative style, we examine whether MD&A sections that are more likely to be produced with AI assistance influence market reactions around annual report releases. Using a large sample of Chinese listed firms and a GPT-based perplexity measure that captures the extent to which MD&A narratives resemble AI-generated text, we document several central findings.

First, we show that MD&A sections exhibiting lower similarity to AI-generated language, are associated with significantly stronger short-window stock returns around annual report disclosures. This result holds after controlling for financial fundamentals, textual attributes, and fixed effects, and the economic magnitude is meaningful. The findings suggest that more AI-like narrative disclosures would increase information risk, and are reflected in stock returns during disclosure.

Second, we provide evidence on the mechanisms underlying this market response. We find that more AI-like narratives contain less firm-specific informational content and rely more heavily on industry- and market-level linguistic structures. Moreover, firms with lower MD&A perplexity exhibit wider bid-ask spreads, indicating elevated information asymmetry and weaker market efficiency. Together, these results highlight that generative-AI-assisted disclosures may dilute the informativeness of managerial communication, thereby weakening the alignment between reported narratives and underlying economic fundamentals.

Third, the relation between perplexity and investor reaction is concentrated in settings where disclosure credibility is most valuable. The return effect is stronger when firms report negative earnings surprises, when MD&A contains less quantitative information or exhibits low burstiness, and when external monitoring is weak. These cross-sectional patterns reinforce the interpretation that perplexity captures variation in disclosure informativeness that investors rely upon especially

in high-uncertainty environments.

Finally, using the launch of ChatGPT as an exogenous shock to AI-adoption costs, we show that firms in non-AI industries experience a pronounced decline in perplexity after the technology becomes publicly available. This finding provides direct evidence that improvements in generative-AI accessibility causally reshape firms' narrative reporting practices. Instrumental-variable analyses further confirm that our results are not driven by endogenous disclosure choices.

Overall, our findings indicate that the diffusion of generative AI into corporate reporting has measurable capital-market implications. While AI tools may improve drafting efficiency, their increased use in MD&A preparation can inadvertently elevate information risk. As regulators and market participants evaluate the role of AI in financial reporting, our results underscore the importance of transparency and governance surrounding AI-assisted disclosure and highlight the need for oversight mechanisms that preserve the informativeness of managerial narrative communication.
