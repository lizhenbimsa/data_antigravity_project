# Using Machine Learning to Measure Conservatism

Jeremy Bertomeu, Edwige Cheynel, Yifei Liao, Mario Milone $ ^{*} $

## Abstract

Machine learning can improve empirical proxies of conservatism by detecting patterns beyond linear regression techniques assumed in prior literature. Using a neural network approach, we show that measures based on machine-learning exhibit (a) better fit adjusted for degrees of freedom, (b) fewer economically anomalous observations, (c) less unexplained year-over-year instability, (d) a secular decline in conservatism, and (e) more robust associations with periods post restatements. In simulations, proxies based on machine learning methods are the most robust to specification error and reduce the incidence of false negatives. Our approach shows that, separate from their usefulness in predictive analytics, methods from machine learning can be used to capture more informative variation than existing measures.

Keywords: machine learning, neural network, accounting, conservatism, measure, proxy.

JEL Codes: C1, D2, M4.

Conservatism is traditionally understood as a differential response of the accounting process to losses versus gains, as manifested by a greater level of certainty required to record positive news (Watts 2003). The asymmetry is reflected empirically by a structural break in the properties of accounting numbers around favorable transactions. An extensive literature builds on this relationship to develop measures of conservatism from the differential timeliness of price and earnings; see, e.g., Basu (1997), Kothari et al. (2010), and Ball et al. (2013a,b). In this literature, the statistical models have many determinants entering linearly into the specification and do not account for interactions that may help better identify conservatism.

This study offers an exploratory analysis of machine learning techniques to measure conservatism when lifting functional form restrictions unrelated to conservatism. We propose and analyze an extension of the differential timeliness model (Basu 1997), in which observable characteristics related to conservatism as well as controls and their interactions need not follow a linear relation. While the current application is on the measurement of conservatism, the goal of the study is broader and suggests incorporating algorithms from machine learning to filter noise in conventional archival studies to recover more complex patterns in a dataset. Specifically, we develop machine learning measures of conservatism (hereafter, MLC) and examine potential benefits and drawbacks relative to conventional approaches.

Most existing applications of machine learning in accounting tend to focus on prediction (Perols et al. 2017; Bao et al. 2020; Bertomeu et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2022), which involves the use of current financial information to predict information observed at a future date (e.g., future earnings or write-offs) or an ongoing irregularity (e.g., AAERs or material restatement). In comparison, we use machine learning for a different purpose – as a tool for semiparametric estimation to fit an unknown function of interest  $ f $ – to capture a greater proportion of the informative variation in a sample; as Athey (2018) summarizes, “ML does not add much to questions about identification…, but rather yields great improvements when the goal is semiparametric estimation or when there are a large number of covariates relative to the number of observations.” This goal is conceptually different from the more common objective of prediction, which captures “the process of generating decision rules for new observations” (Krupa and Minutti-Meza 2021) and emphasizes out-of-sample accuracy to assess whether the model adequately removes specificities of the sample. Our objective in this study is to use all the information in the sample to semiparametrically

estimate an assumed relationship in the data generating process (the measure), which requires a different approach in selecting the model and assessing its performance. As Shmueli 2010 notes: “The top priority in terms of model performance in explanatory modeling is assessing explanatory power, which measures the strength of relationship indicated by  $ \hat{f} $. Researchers report  $ R^{2} $-type values and statistical significance of overall F-type statistics to indicate the level of explanatory power.”

Our empirical model aims to identify differential timeliness in terms of variation in observed economic proxies. The prior literature regresses earnings on returns and firm characteristics, and further includes interaction terms to account for the impact on negative returns to test for the asymmetric timeliness of earnings. We expand the set of commonly used features plausibly related to conservatism and generalize the specification of these features into the model so that the functional form need not be linear. We compare simple specifications augmented with additional firm characteristics, and further test the effect of relaxing the linearity on improving the model fit. Formally, the direct effect of prices on earnings and the interaction term conditional on negative returns may take a non-linear functional form. We further impose two reasonable theoretical restrictions on these functions reflecting various doctrines anchoring income as a measurement of a change in “net wealth,” as, for example, in Hicksian income defined as the amount that can be recovered by shareholders while preserving existing capital (Bromwich et al. 2010) or, as summarized in the FASB Concepts statements No. 8 “Comprehensive income as defined is a return on financial capital.” $ ^{1} $ Specifically, we require income to be non-decreasing in equity returns conditional on the set of characteristics used in the model.

We develop two benchmark measures of differential timeliness based on neural networks and eight economic characteristics, namely “MLC2” and “MLC2y,” where y indicates the inclusion of individual years as a feature. Validating an explanatory model is conceptually more complex than evaluating a predictive model as we cannot rely on out-of-sample accuracy, so we instead use multiple validation criteria. $ ^{2} $ Shmueli (2010) recommends using goodness-of-fit, so we first evaluate

the incremental fit of the machine learning models. We find that machine learning measures capture a larger portion of the variation than approaches involving linear models estimated by year, which suggests that the models better capture the information in the sample important to the constructs of interest. $ ^{3} $ Further, MLC2 and MLC2y both feature greater temporal stability, as the economic characteristics in the measure drive a much larger portion of the fit; these measures are consistent with a secular decline in concepts supporting income-statement conservatism and a shift toward the asset-liability view documented by accounting historians (Zeff 1999, 2013; Orthaus et al. 2022). $ ^{4} $

Goodness-of-fit is a desirable criterion if the assumed form of the data-generating process is true, but does not necessarily imply a better explanation if the assumed statistical model is misspecified or does not capture the intended economic construct. Our first approach to examine this question is to assume that the true data-generating process may be captured by one of the measures, but which measure is true is unknown to the researcher. Hence, a researcher having a sufficiently diffuse prior over existing methodologies may favor measures that are more robust to other measures being correct. We show that machine learning algorithms provide more robust inferences about the true data-generating process even if another measure is true: a misspecified machine learning model appears to keep an error relative to the true coefficient hovering at about half of its unconditional standard deviation against all models, versus often multiple standard deviations when linear models are used to estimate a process generated from another model. Further, when applying the measure to simulated data in which conservatism causes a change in a variable, a researcher using machine learning is less likely to falsely accept an insignificant coefficient.

Next, we validate the measures based on several presumed intuitive properties of conservatism,

along the arguments in Ettredge et al. (2012) and Givoly et al. (2007) that (i) conservatism should be persistent, and (ii) conservatism should increase after a detected misstatement. We document evidence that MLC2 and MLC2y more robustly capture these properties, while other measures feature more relationships that are insignificant or with a counter-intuitive sign.

In summary, methods from machine learning can be used to develop more accurate proxies of conservatism based on improving the fit of existing measures. A more accurate proxy can reduce attenuation bias in coefficient estimates and lead to fewer insignificant (“false negatives”) results when evaluating the effect of conservatism in small or noisy datasets. It can also reveal greater economic magnitudes when evaluating causal effects. We also heed the call by Jennings et al. (2020) to improve the measurement of accounting proxies given that, as they demonstrate in various simulations and empirical examples, noise in variables used to form controls may also bias toward finding significant coefficient estimates. Although we apply the model to the estimation of differential timeliness, the approach can more generally decompose the relationship between a right hand-side variable (“earnings”) and a kinked linear variable (“returns”) by using machine learning to flexibly capture a set of explanatory features. $ ^{5} $

Literature Review. Our study links to a growing literature exploring how computational and theoretical advances in machine learning can improve accounting measurements. This literature observes that many constructs relevant to the accounting process have functional forms that are unknown a priori to researchers. Studies such as Perols (2011) and Perols et al. (2017) are among the first to introduce machine learning methods in accounting. Recent studies have also examined the use of machine learning for textual analysis (Li 2010; Frankel et al. 2016), to analyze audit quality (Yang et al. 2020; Hu et al. 2020), compute more accurate accruals (Ding et al. 2020) or improve the analysis of financial statements (Chen et al. 2020), to examine the evolution of the relevance of accounting (Barth et al. 2019), to gauge the importance of corporate governance (Gow et al. 2022), or to predict misstatements (Bao et al. 2020; Bertomeu et al. 2020). $ ^{6} $ This literature overwhelmingly focuses on using machine learning for prediction. We adopt a different perspective and employ machine learning methods to separate signal from noise and to extract an economic

characteristic of interest. Our objective is thus to show how machine learning methods can be nested into commonly used measures of differential timeliness, and test for the ability of machine learning to incorporate a greater portion of informative variation into the measure. $ ^{7} $

There also exists an extensive literature aiming to measure conservatism, which we cannot discuss in its entirety. Our baseline specification draws heavily from the differential timeliness model in Basu (1997) and Khan and Watts (2009). Taking the example of an asset impairment, Basu observes that timely reporting of bad news is reflected into earnings when a sufficient loss is expected; by contrast, gains are delayed until the earnings process is complete, implying a flatter relation between earnings and gain expectations. Conservatism results in a broken symmetry around gains and losses, which can be tested with a linear regression of earnings on returns with an interaction in the form of an indicator variable for negative returns (“bad news”). The original Basu (1997) specification assumes that conservatism does not change over time, while Khan and Watts (2009) assume that the degree of asymmetry may vary within firm. Differential timeliness is a workhorse model to test theories of conservatism; see, e.g., Ball et al. (2000), Givoly and Hayn (2000), Ball et al. (2003), Lafond and Roychowdhury (2008). We develop a measure that retains the broken symmetry around market news but otherwise does not impose a specific parametric form on its components.

There are nevertheless continuing debates about using asymmetric timelines in the measurement of conservatism (Beaver and Ryan 2005; Ryan 2006; Givoly et al. 2007), and a machine learning algorithm is a statistical tool that does not, on its own, provide a new method of identification. Our machine learning measure should likely be used in conjunction with measures that capture other implications of conservatism. Roychowdhury and Watts (2007) argue market-to-book (M/B) is complementary to asymmetric timeliness in that conservatism need not be a single-dimensional property of measurement. M/B captures cumulative conservatism through systematic under-recognition of accounting assets relative to investor expectations. In addition, income statement and balance sheet conservatism may have different economic implications. For example, if a firm reduces the value of its assets, high balance sheet conservatism may be followed by low income statement conservatism if there is no further scope for impairments (Sunder et al. 2018). $ ^{8} $

The ability of the algorithm to allow for an asymmetric timeliness that varies with more covariates (including M/B) and in a non-linear manner, provides a more comprehensive, albeit certainly imperfect, empirical design to control for biases due to omitted factors.

## 1 Empirical Strategy

### 1.1 Model and Neural Network

We assume the following non-linear model of earnings and prices:

 $$ X_{i}=\underbrace{f(Y_{i})+R_{i}g(Y_{i})+R_{i}D_{i}c(Y_{i})+D_{i}d(Y_{i})}_{=m(Y_{i},R_{i})}+\epsilon_{i}, $$ 

where  $ X_i $ is earnings,  $ R_i $ is return,  $ D_i $ is an indicator variable equal to one if  $ R_i < 0 $ and 0 otherwise,  $ Y_i $ is a vector of explanatory variables that does not include  $ R_i $ or  $ D_i $, and  $ \epsilon_i $ is an i.i.d. error term. $ ^9 $ The functions  $ f $,  $ g $,  $ c $ and  $ d $ have a specified form in the regression models, whereas the machine learning algorithm does not impose any conditions on the shape of the functions. The function  $ c $ is the measure of conservatism and will be determined in the different models.

In Basu (1997), the model is estimated with a pooled time-series regression where all functions are constants and researchers commonly use firm-level regressions to estimate c by firm (the Basu coefficient). In Khan and Watts (2009), hereafter KW, the functions are linear in three features – size (total assets), market-to-book (M/B), and leverage (total debt to market value of equity) – and estimated by year. These designs are special cases of this specification choosing the features  $ Y_{i} $ as firms' fixed effects or a linear function of characteristics interacted with time fixed effects. Importantly, the function c cannot be directly computed from data because we do not observe it, so a statistical method and assumptions about  $ Y_{i} $ are required to estimate it.

Our objective is to estimate equation (1.1) potentially including more features in  $ Y_{i} $ and without

assuming a linear functional form. Because a neural network is used to encode the restrictions from (1.1) into a more general structure, it is helpful to obtain a general understanding of how neural networks fit data. $ ^{10} $ A network is composed of perceptrons (also called nodes or neurons) connected to each other. Each perceptron is a mathematical function that receives a vector as input and returns a scalar. If we denote  $ \boldsymbol{x} $ the input vector, a perceptron returns a scalar  $ z = \Phi(\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}) $, where  $ \boldsymbol{w} $ is a vector of weights, b is a bias term, and  $ \Phi(\cdot) $ is called the activation function. When connected in a network, the output value of a perceptron becomes a component of the input vector of the next perceptron.

We estimate equation (1.1) with a custom neural network whose structure is illustrated in Figure 1. The input layer of the network is a vector of dimension  $ n+2 $ where the first n inputs correspond to the variables  $ Y_i $, and the last two inputs are the return  $ R_i $ and a dummy variable  $ D_i $ equal to one when the return  $ R_i $ is negative. The square nodes in the figure correspond to embedded neural networks in Figures 2a and 2b whose outputs are the values of the functions  $ f $,  $ g $,  $ g+c $, and  $ d $. The output layer is a single perceptron whose inputs are the values of the functions as well as  $ R_i $ and  $ D_i $. This perceptron does not have any estimated weights: its only purpose is to compute the function  $ m $ in Equation (1.1).

We design the network to extract the g and c semi-parametrically, thus imposing the break at  $ R_i = 0 $ in (1.1) embedded in the theory of differential timeliness, without requiring other parametric assumptions. The input layer of the network encoding functions f and d is a vector of dimension n corresponding to the variables  $ Y_i $, the intermediate layer uses hyperbolic tangent activation functions $ ^{11} $, and the output layer consists of a single perceptron whose output is the value of the respective functions in Figure 2a. As we do not impose restrictions on the values of  $ f(\cdot) $ and  $ d(\cdot) $, the activation function of their output perceptron is chosen to be linear, thus allowing for any real value. For the full sample, we require non-missing net income, share price, common outstanding shares, book equity, debt and total assets. To further motivate the benefits of a machine learning based measure, we note a potential drawback of the benchmark model KW (i.e., L1y). Given that the model is estimated with separate yearly regressions, the coefficients on size, M/B, and leverage are expected to vary from year to year. As shown in Figure 3, the coefficients exhibit large spikes with only limited interpretable persistence that would map to trends in economic transactions or macroeconomic changes. In fact, all coefficients change sign over the sample period: the M/B coefficient oscillates around zero and the coefficients on size and leverage can change three-fold year-over-year, occasionally becoming zero or slightly negative. Indeed, the time-variation in coefficients contains most of the explanatory

power of the model so that the averaged yearly coefficients do not fit the sample well: the full-sample adjusted  $ R^{2} $ using the average yearly coefficients drops from 26.5% to 6%; this seems intuitively surprising given that accounting practices are likely to be persistent. Specifically, if the true relation of interest is a complex function of firm characteristics that have changed over time, yearly linear regression with fewer characteristics or a misspecified functional form project the true relation as changes to yearly coefficients. $ ^{18} $

Another diagnostic that may indicate noisy estimates is that the fitted relation between earnings and returns is often negative, which likely occurs only under unusual circumstances. We plot in Figure 4 (first column), the density of the relation conditional on positive news g and conditional on negative news  $ g + c $ in KW (L1y) and in a simpler model estimated with a single pooled regression (L1). In both cases, we expect the average effect to be such that earnings reflect information in the direction of the news, consistent with prior research on the relation between earnings and price (see, e.g., Ohlson 2001 and Penman and Zhang 2002). However, in the KW measure, g is counter-intuitively centered around zero, indicating that a random observation is about equally likely to exhibit lower earnings conditional on higher returns. The large magnitudes of the coefficient (between -0.2 and 0.2) suggest that this feature is not due to a weak association between earnings

and returns (Ball and Brown 2014). Conditional on negative returns, observations are more likely to feature a positive association  $ g+c>0 $ but, even then, more than 11% of all observations feature a negative association. $ ^{19} $ Given that the features used in KW are unlikely to capture the special circumstances leading to a negative association (early stage growth firms with unusually high levels of investments in intangibles), the distribution of parameters suggests that the functions g and  $ g+c $ should be restricted to remain positive. $ ^{20} $

We develop four models estimated using a neural network approach by choosing the set of variables  $ Y_{i} $ and derive four measures of machine learning conservatism (MLC). The models MLC1 and MLC1y use the same three features as KW and are included to provide a direct comparison

to L1y. In MLC1, we only include the three firm characteristics and assume that the relation does not change over time. In MLC1y, we allow for the relation to be different between years. Within a neural network approach, year-level variation can be incorporated by adding time fixed effects as additional features in  $ Y_{i} $. Unlike fixed effects in a pooled time-series linear regression, the algorithm interacts these fixed effects to capture any time-variation in coefficients of the model. Models MLC2 and MLC2y are identical to models L2 and L2y in terms of features but use the neural network. Overall, we derive eight different models, which can be compared pairwise over any of the following dimensions: (i) linear “L” vs. machine learning “ML”, (ii) three “1” vs. eight features “2”, and (iii) only economic features vs. including time effects “y.”

### 1.3 Model Training

Under the objective of using a model for prediction, a model is evaluated based on its performance out-of-sample. However, following Shmueli (2010) and Kleinberg et al. (2015), our purpose in using machine learning is fundamentally different and aims to semi-parametrically capture a pattern in the data. Hence, we train and evaluate the model differently from the traditional approach so that we can emphasize the ability of the model to capture the relationship of interest in (1.1).

We tune the two fitting parameters of the neural network that have the greatest impact on the neural network: the number of iterations and the intermediate layer size. The number of iterations is the number of times the gradient of the loss function is computed and the weights are updated. The layer size refers to the number of perceptrons used in the intermediate layer of the network. This step is conceptually needed in a neural network because the network would always fit all the variation with enough perceptrons (Cybenko 1989) if the fitting parameters are set to maximize in-sample fit. So, we cross-validate here as a statistical procedure to choose parameters in the model, similar to cross-validating the bandwidth with kernel density estimation (Pagan and Ullah 1999) or the regularization in a LASSO or ridge regression, given that these methods are commonly used for purposes other than prediction.

This different purpose ties to structuring the cross-validation differently from a prediction exercise. The usual approach when machine learning is used for prediction is to divide the dataset by year (Bao et al. 2020; Bertomeu et al. 2020), which reflects the prospective use of the model to predict different years and makes it more likely that the model performs well to future years over

fitting specific aspects of the present. However, our use of machine learning is different in that we are not predicting but fitting a non-linear model whose form is unknown. Furthermore, comparability with existing measures estimated by time or with time fixed effects requires samples that have all years. For this reason, we conduct the cross-validation by sampling half of all the firms used for tuning the parameters (rather than sampling by year), train the model on the selected half, and compute its fit on the other half. $ ^{21} $

### 1.4 Estimation and Goodness-of-Fit

Once the parameters of the neural network are tuned, we always train and evaluate the full model on the entire dataset, which reflects that we seek to use all the information in the sample to estimate the relationship of interest. We proceed next to estimate the eight models: the linear models L1, L1y, L2, and L2y, and the neural networks MLC1, MLC1y, MLC2, and MLC2y, where models “2” refers to the inclusion of the additional features and models ending with “y” are estimated with separate yearly regressions (for linear models) or fixed effects (for neural networks).

We compare these measures in the correlation matrix in Table 2. As expected, all measures are positively correlated, suggesting that they directionally capture a common construct. Machine learning measures tend to feature a correlation of .5 or above with their linear paired counterparts, providing a sanity check that the first-order factors recovered in linear models are still present with the machine learning measures. Among such comparisons, the lowest correlation is between L2y and MLC2y, which suggests that many of the time-varying coefficients in L2y are being captured by characteristics in MLC2y. Models without time fixed effects tend to be more correlated, which suggests that the inclusion of additional fixed effects may make the estimation noisier and capture less of a common component.

## 2 Validation

### 2.1 Sampling Errors

We demonstrated that the ML measures fit the data well (e.g., it can extract sufficient information on a construct of interest). However, while necessary conditions, these are not sufficient to guarantee the validity of the measure if the model is misspecified. Unfortunately, the researcher does not know the true data-generating process (hereafter, DGP) for conservatism, which creates complications given that all measures are misspecifying the true process. Before we pursue subsequent analysis to examine properties of the measures and validate established economic relations or priors, we assess the performance of these measures when confronted to various DGPs.

A common approach is to conduct simulations where the researcher defines a representative DGP and examines the behavior of measures when applied to a problem with finite samples or

whether the measure may be misspecified to some degree because of either its assumptions or computational methodology. Any of these reasons will lead to statistical error in our setting, given that even the most general algorithms presume some regularities in the data, we use a finite number of observations, and the computational methods are imperfect. Evaluating the properties of an estimator by simulation enables us to assess qualities that cannot be analytically derived and is sometimes used in applied work to evaluate complex models.

As in Breuer and Schütt (2019) and Jennings et al. (2020), we use simulations to assess errors in the empirical analysis. In Table 4, we block bootstrap by firm the features  $ (Y_i) $ of 100 new datasets, construct simulated earnings from the model estimated in the earlier section, and add a random Gaussian noise term whose standard deviation is equal to the standard deviation of the residual of that model. Then, the researcher re-estimates the measure using their own model. We rotate the true DGP and then run each measure against another DGP. $ ^{25} $ The mean absolute error (MAE) is the average absolute error between the true c and the estimated measure. Then, we average across all simulations and compute a standard error across the 100-point estimates of MAEs.

For example, in column L1y, we reconstruct earnings  $ X_{i} $ for each observation in the sample

by using estimation L1y. Then, in row MLC2, the researcher runs the analysis and obtains a new MLC2-based C score for this particular sample – of course, the researcher is potentially misspecified and uses a model that has functional forms that are possibly too complex but ignores the time effects. We find that a miscalibrated researcher using MLC2 when the true DGP is L1y would have a mean absolute error equal to 52.3% (half of a standard deviation of the true measure), versus 22.3% if (correctly) using L1y. Vice versa, if the true DGP is MLC2, the error is 49.6% using MLC2 versus 493% (or five standard deviations) using L1y.

As is intuitive, estimation procedures do well in the diagonal, since they are correctly specified for the process of interest. Errors are, unsurprisingly, larger when the process is more complex and includes non-linearities or time effects. As to robustness, a better model can be read in rows and must be such that its errors are smaller than in other rows (i.e., comparing row vectors). All “1” models tend to perform poorly if the true DGP has more variables (columns for “2” models), and are often multiple standard errors away from the true value. MLC2 and MLC2y are the most robust approaches across models, with similar errors across all specifications (hovering at roughly half of a standard deviation). Also, MLC2y appears slightly less robust than MLC2: MLC2’s error against MLC2y remains at 45.1% while MLC2y’s error against MLC2 is 80.9%, likely because MLC2y has many variables and incorrectly assigns the fit to time effects and their interactions.

The comparison with L1y reveals that this model works well mainly when it is the correct DGP, but otherwise features very large MAEs, with mean absolute errors greater than one standard deviation against MLC1, L2, MLC2, and MLC2y. This suggests that a researcher would need a high degree of confidence that L1y is the true specification to use it. Both MLC2 and MLC2y exhibit good performance if the true DGP is L2 (the best linear model based on adjusted  $ R^{2} $), at roughly 44.1% for MLC2 and 63.9% for MLC2y. In the last two columns, only MLC2 and MLC2y fit well their respective DGPs while, by contrast, in the first three columns, most approaches perform reasonably well against DGPs with only three features (excluding time).

### 2.2 Temporal Stability

Linear C score measures have been criticized for their lack of temporal stability (Givoly et al. 2007). If measures based on machine learning capture firms' conservatism, they should capture some inherent characteristics, such as corporate governance, leadership, organizational culture, or auditing procedures, many of which are likely highly persistent. Measures including time effects or separate regressions by year ('y' models) exhibit significant instability over time, while MLC2 can attain a reasonable fit without the instability caused by time effects; further, along the lines of Givoly et al. (2007), we explore later in this section the stability of the measure within firm.

Figure 5 plots time-series changes in the construct of interest to examine long-term trends in accounting (Barth et al. 2019). Models L1, MLC1, L2, and MLC2 exhibit an increase until the mid 1970s, followed by a long-term trend of declining conservatism, starting in the decade of the 70s and revealing that conservatism is currently near its lowest ever. To our knowledge, this pattern has not been documented with archival data in prior literature but is consistent with a shift in conceptual statements toward relevance, initiated with the first concepts statements of the FASB and, later, a convergence with international accounting standards and a change in focus toward neutrality and prudence (Zeff 2013). The models with yearly effects tend to reveal large variations in conservatism within short time spans, and do not clearly show this trend expected from institutional facts, plausibly because the yearly coefficients are noisily estimated.

### 2.3 Restatements

We develop next a separate validation based on Ettredge et al. (2012) to examine whether our ML measures can capture firms' conservative behavior where it is expected. A natural setting is to study the restatement data in light of the information conveyed by the C-score measures. To

conduct these tests, we pool the data from audit analytics from 2000 until 2019. $ ^{27} $

We identify misstatements consistent with prior research (Srinivasan et al. 2015; Zakolyukina 2018; Cheynel and Zhou 2020) to reduce frequent restatements that are economically innocuous or clear clerical errors (Hennes et al. 2008). To select observations more likely to capture accounting irregularities, we retain in Audit Analytics restatements that (i) trigger an investigation by the board of directors or the SEC, or affect a revenue or core expense account as in Zakolyukina (2018), and (ii) are income-decreasing. Following Ettredge et al. (2012), we test whether the C-score measures detect higher conservatism in the years following the overstatement, as firms are expected to become less aggressive in the years following the detection of an irregularity. We define the post-overstatement period as being of the same length as that of the misstatement it relates to. $ ^{28} $

We conduct a test in means by differentiating the C score during post-overstatements periods and other periods, noting that greater conservatism in the post period should imply a positive difference. We consider two approaches to report this difference. Table 9 panel (a) displays the results in the original models and panel (b) uses retrained models that include the post-misstatement information as a feature. We find a significant positive difference for MLC2 and MLC2y in both panels. MLC1 and MLC1y do not perform as well: their differences are insignificant using the original models and become positive and significant only in the retrained models. L2 and L2y yield positive and significant differences in both panels. Once the models are retrained (panel (b)), the firm's attributes are less important, and all MLC-score measures display a positive and significant difference. The magnitudes of the differences are, in general, larger in the retrained models, consistent with the ML algorithm training taking into account the restatement information.

We further conduct multivariate analyses in Table 10: we regress the C scores on post over-statement periods (dummy equal to 1 for the post overstatement periods) controlling for all firm

characteristics used in models “2,” year fixed effects, and firm fixed effects to remove variations due to firms’ heterogeneity and time shocks. For the original models, MLC2 is the only measure that supports a significant positive coefficient at the 5% level on the post-overstatement periods. The coefficient on the post-overstatement periods L2 and L2y is insignificant, suggesting that the significant positive difference in means may be affected by yearly shocks or persistent firm characteristics. Once the models are re-trained, all MLC-score measures and L2 and L2y yield a positive and significant coefficient on the post-overstatement periods. MLC2 has the largest positive coefficient on the post-overstatement periods. Collectively, this table provides evidence in favor of MLC2 to capturing firms’ conservatism superior to other measures.

## 3 Concluding Remarks

Measuring characteristics of accounting information is notoriously difficult because, as a researcher, one only observes the joint result of inherent economics and the choices made in the accounting system (Dechow et al. 2010). The problem of assessing conservatism combines all of these challenges: new transactions are continuously being recorded and are affected by the economic environment. Isolating the effect of conservatism requires assessing whether these transactions are within the scope of conservative rules defined by GAAP, the level of verifiability and, more broadly, the level of certainty about future cash flows. The discretion that accounting policies offer in choices of conservatism can only be extracted after these economic factors have been satisfactorily included in the model. Machine learning is a statistical tool that, on its own, does not offer a complete model to solve this non-trivial identification challenge – presumably, the identification could only be re-

solved in principle via better consensus over the data-generating process of economic transactions in a structural model. However, machine learning provides new benefits in using observable economic variables more thoroughly given its ability to fit a flexible model that does not presume particular interactions or functional forms.

Our main result is that a parsimonious neural network with relatively few variables provides a proxy for differential timeliness with a fit equivalent to standard models estimated with separate yearly regressions. We cross-validate the hyperparameters of the network and provide routines that allow for a simple recovery of the measure with minimal computing needs. The measure provides three primary benefits. First, the measure is expressed in terms of interpretable economic factors widely used across studies, avoiding the difficulties in interpreting average coefficients in separate regressions. Second, the measure offers a new look at long-term trends in conservatism, revealing a secular decrease in conservatism over most time periods following the FASB conceptual statements. Third, the proxy exhibits new interactions and convexities that were previously unknown to researchers, and may be used in extended tests of economic theories that may have been insignificant due to lack of power. Fourth, we show that, across two validation exercises proposed in prior literature, the measure conforms more closely to what is a priori expected, in terms of persistence within-firm and its association with periods post restatements.

The analysis is part of a broader research agenda showing that machine learning tools can be used beyond prediction tasks. Refined algorithms provide new methods to filter noise when the relation between signal and noise is not fully captured by a closed-form statistical model. Most theories do not fully specify the correct empirical research design, and researchers may argue about functional forms, interactions, or variable inclusions. We illustrate this objective by improving a standard approach to differential timeliness; the same approach can nevertheless be used to improve any noisy proxy, such as accounting quality (Barth et al. 2019), cost of capital (Lambert et al. 2007, 2012; Bertomeu and Cheynel 2016), or accruals (Dechow et al. 1995; Armstrong et al. 2016). We hope that the methods can be used more broadly to improve existing empirical proxies and offer new insights on theories that require more accurate measurements.
